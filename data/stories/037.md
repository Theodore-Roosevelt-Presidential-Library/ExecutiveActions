## At a glance
In the spring of 1902 Theodore Roosevelt signed two small land proclamations that did the same narrow job. Each took a single tract that an earlier order had set aside for an Indian purpose and "restored" it to the public domain so that a named applicant could acquire it: a settler named Jacob Kuhner at Capitan Grande in California, and the young town of Anadarko in Oklahoma Territory, which wanted a park. They are tiny windows onto a very large process, the movement of reserved and tribal land into settler and town hands.

## Background
Both proclamations were countersigned by David J. Hill as Acting Secretary of State, and neither was a one-off. Proclamation 476 reused, word for word, a McKinley proclamation of April 16, 1901, which had released Sections 8 and 9 of the same township for a settler named Arthur F. Head. Roosevelt used the same form again on May 15, 1905, for Sections 5 and 6 and a homesteader named Joseph J. Henderson. Proclamation 478 has an exact twin ten weeks later, [Restoring Fort Sill Indian Sub-Agency Lands to Lawton for a Cemetery](story:40), which released a forty-acre tract to the city of Lawton under the same 1890 law.

**California (Proclamation 476, May 29, 1902).** The land was Section 7 of township 15 south, range 2 east, San Bernardino meridian, a single section in the San Diego County back country. President Grant's executive order of December 27, 1875, had reserved it, with other land, for the Capitan Grande band of Mission Indians (Kumeyaay people). The Mission Indian Relief Act of January 12, 1891, then had the Secretary of the Interior appoint "three disinterested persons as commissioners" to select a reservation for each band. That commission "intentionally omitted and excluded" Section 7 from Capitan Grande's selection, reporting that the omitted tracts held "the claims of Jacob Kühner and others." The band's patent of March 10, 1894, left Section 7 out too.

But President Harrison's executive order of December 29, 1891, approving the commission's work, had frozen every tract named in its report "from settlement and entry" until a presidential proclamation restored it. Kuhner "can not make the requisite filings on the land occupied by him" until that happened. Proclamation 476 is the release. It cites no statute, only "the power in me vested," and modifies the 1875 and 1891 orders just far enough to except Section 7.

Kuhner knew the ground. The *San Diego Union* listed him as school trustee for the El Capitan district in July 1901 and as "Jacob Kuhner of El Capitan" in August 1903; by 1904 the paper placed him at Lakeside. Whether he went on to secure title to Section 7 is not known.

**Oklahoma Territory (Proclamation 478, June 23, 1902).** The parcel was 38.60 acres in section sixteen, township seven north, range ten west of the Indian meridian, held back for the Kiowa and Comanche Indian Agency when the ceded Kiowa, Comanche, Apache and Wichita lands were opened to settlement. McKinley's opening proclamation of July 4, 1901, did not name the tract; it excepted, in general terms, lands "set apart for military, agency, school, school farm, religious, Indian cemetery, wood reserve, forest reserve, or other public uses," and opened the rest on August 6, 1901.

By June 1902 the agency no longer needed the land, which adjoined the new town of Anadarko, and the mayor had applied to take it for a park. The law he applied under, the act of September 30, 1890, let an incorporated city buy up to a quarter section within three miles of its limits for a cemetery or park, but only of "public land[s] not reserved for public use." As long as the tract stood reserved for the agency, the city could not touch it. Proclamation 478, acting under section six of the act of June 6, 1900, restored it "to be disposed of to said city for park purposes."

One detail the proclamation leaves unremarked: the 1900 act reserved sections sixteen and thirty-six in each township "for the use of the common schools," and let the territorial governor pick other land if any part of those sections was lost. Whether Oklahoma Territory did so for these acres is not known.

Local tradition in Anadarko credits the Indian agent, Lt. Col. James F. Randlett, who ran the Kiowa Agency from 1899 to 1905 and who, in the city's words today, "secured land from the government for a park." That fits the proclamation's account, an agency declaring the land unneeded and a mayor applying to buy it, but Randlett's own annual report for 1902 does not mention the park.

## Historical context
In California, the 1891 relief act grew out of two decades of agitation over Mission Indian villages being swallowed by settlers and confirmed Mexican-era land grants. It told the commissioners to include, "as far as practicable, the lands and villages which have been in the actual occupation and possession of said Indians," and to appraise any settler's improvements inside a selected reservation. At Capitan Grande the commission chose to exclude settler claims rather than appraise and buy them out. Three proclamations show how that played out along one reservation's edge over fourteen years: Head on Sections 8 and 9 in 1901, Kuhner on Section 7 in 1902, and Henderson on Section 5 in 1905. Henderson's proclamation records that he "entered upon" his land on March 10, 1895, a year after the band's patent, so not every claimant was there first.

Capitan Grande was no tiny holding; it remains a reservation of roughly 15,750 acres. And the same spring, the Indian Office faced a far sharper Mission Indian crisis. The Supreme Court's 1901 decision in *Barker v. Harvey* had confirmed the eviction of the Cupeño villagers of Warner's Ranch, and in January 1902 Inspector James McLaughlin was counting the people to be moved and searching for land to put them on. Beside that, releasing one excluded section to a long-resident settler was routine.

In Oklahoma, the 1901 opening of the Kiowa-Comanche-Apache and Wichita lands was one of the era's great land lotteries. The Jerome Agreement of October 1892, ratified with amendments by the 1900 act, gave each tribal member a 160-acre allotment, set aside 480,000 acres of common grazing land, and opened the rest under the homestead and town-site laws. The tribes fought it. On June 6, 1901, Lone Wolf and other Kiowa, Comanche and Apache plaintiffs sued to stop the opening, charging that three-fourths of the adult men had never signed the agreement as the Medicine Lodge Treaty of 1867 required and that signatures had been obtained by misrepresentation. The injunction was denied, and when Proclamation 478 was signed the case was on its way to the Supreme Court. Anadarko itself was less than a year old, established August 1, 1901. A park carved from an agency tract was a raw territorial town furnishing itself out of land only recently taken out of tribal hands.

## Political impact
Restorations like these were rarer than they look. Volume 32 of the *Statutes at Large* prints forty-four proclamations from March 1901 to March 1903, and only four restore a named Indian-reserved tract for a named applicant: McKinley's of April 16, 1901, for Arthur Head, and Roosevelt's Nos. 476, 478 and 492. The General Land Office did not turn these out by the hundreds. They were uncommon enough that the State Department simply reused the previous one's wording each time, and Roosevelt used the California form once more in 1905, for Joseph J. Henderson.

On paper, both releases moved through the government without a ripple. The Indian Office's annual report for 1902 reprinted Proclamation 478 among the year's proclamations, but Agent James F. Randlett's own report from Anadarko, dated September 1, 1902, says nothing about the park or the restoration. Within ten weeks the same two statutes were put to work again for Lawton's cemetery ([Restoring Fort Sill Indian Sub-Agency Lands to Lawton for a Cemetery](story:40)).

No reaction, protest or congressional notice of either proclamation has been found. In the *San Diego Union*, the paper of record for the Capitan Grande country, "Capitan Grande" appears only once between May and August 1902, in an unrelated list of voting precincts, and every 1901–1904 mention of Kuhner concerns probate, guardianships, jury lists or his school trusteeship. The paper's digitized text is poor, so that silence is weak evidence. In November 1904 the *Union* did report a fight between two men over a forty-acre, water-bearing tract in the same township near Lakeside, but the defendant there is named William R., not Jacob, with the surname partly garbled in the digitized text, the section number cannot be read, and nothing ties the dispute to Section 7. Anadarko's own papers could not be searched at all.

Neither document had the features that drew comment to the other land proclamations of 1902: a large acreage, a new reservation, a disputed opening. Neither argued a policy. Each carried out a step that Congress and earlier orders had already set up.

Behind the Anadarko release, though, a much larger contest was still running. The Kiowa, Comanche and Apache suit against the 1901 opening, *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock*, was on appeal when Proclamation 478 was signed. The Supreme Court heard it on October 23, 1902.

## Legacy
### Anadarko
In Anadarko the transfer took. The city formally acquired the tract in 1904 and named it Randlett Park, after the Indian agent whom local tradition credits with securing the land. The town's first water and electric plant had already been sited there beside the Washita River, and a 1908 hydroelectric plant and a 1926 municipal pool followed. The park was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2003 (reference 03000878), its nomination describing "approximately 40 acres," the 38.60 acres of Proclamation 478, running from the Washita south to Central Boulevard.

The tribes' challenge to the opening ended in *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock*, decided January 5, 1903. The Supreme Court held that "Plenary authority over the tribal relations of the Indians has been exercised by Congress from the beginning, and the power has always been deemed a political one, not subject to be controlled by the judicial department of the government." It remains one of the most consequential and most criticized decisions in American Indian law.

### Capitan Grande
In California the record is thinner. Whether Section 7 ever passed into Kuhner's hands is still not known. The reservation itself did not stay whole. An act of February 28, 1919, granted the city of San Diego lands in Capitan Grande "for dam and reservoir purposes for the conservation and storage of water," conditioned on compensating the Mission Indians, and the El Capitan Reservoir of the early 1930s flooded the heart of the reservation. Its families moved to the new Barona and Viejas reservations, whose bands today hold the remaining Capitan Grande land, roughly 15,750 acres, jointly in trust; it is uninhabited. The 1919 act also granted San Diego parts of Sections 8 and 9, the tracts McKinley had restored for Arthur Head, by then lying "within the Cleveland National Forest."

These two proclamations matter chiefly as ordinary instances of the machinery that moved reserved and tribal land into settler and town use. The fuller story, and its cost, belongs to the reservation openings themselves, among them [Opening the Ceded Fort Hall Lands, Idaho](story:35).

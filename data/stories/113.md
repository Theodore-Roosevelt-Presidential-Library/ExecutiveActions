## At a glance
In 1904 Roosevelt signed twenty-three executive orders that never received numbers. Most were housekeeping: Army reservations in Cuba, the Philippines and Alaska, land-office boundaries in the West, and the pay of the new Isthmian Canal Commission. Two reached further than they looked. One returned a fifty-square-mile strip of Nebraska from the Pine Ridge Sioux reservation to the public domain and set off a rush of settlers. And on October 4, Roosevelt set aside Breton Island off the Louisiana coast as the country's second federal bird reservation.

## Background
None of these orders appears in the numbered series. They are known from Lord's *List and Index of Presidential Executive Orders, Unnumbered Series*, a WPA finding aid published in 1943, which catalogs the year's twenty-three orders from the Guantánamo reservation on January 9 to Camp Eldridge on December 6. They carry on the pattern of 1903: overseas reservations, Western land administration, and Army and canal business.

**Guantánamo.** The United States had leased Guantánamo Bay from Cuba the year before. The leased ground's boundaries were fixed by the Cuban–American agreement of February 16/23, 1903, and the lease of July 2, 1903 set the rent. Roosevelt's order of January 9, 1904 did not create the naval station. It divided three parcels *inside* the station and assigned them to the **War Department**: Reservation No. 1 in the west part of the Cuzco Hills, No. 2 at Conde Bluff, and No. 3 on the west side of the harbor, with pieces held back for Navy wireless stations, the Lighthouse Establishment and the harbor master. The War Department's 1907 compilation put it plainly: "This reservation, declared by Executive Order, dated January 9, 1904, consists of three parts, within the limits of the naval station at Guantanamo Bay, which station was acquired by lease from the Republic of Cuba, signed at Havana July 2, 1903."

**The Pine Ridge strip.** On January 25, Roosevelt signed an order short enough to quote whole:

> "It is hereby ordered that the tract of country in the State of Nebraska 'withdrawn from sale and set aside as an addition to the present Sioux Indian Reservation in the Territory of Dakota,' by Executive Order dated January 24, 1882, be and the same hereby is restored to the public domain. Theodore Roosevelt."

The order states no acreage. The Indian Office counted the tract at about 32,000 acres (50 square miles), a round figure for ground that had never been surveyed. The 1882 order had held the land only "during the pleasure of the President," and the Sioux Act of March 2, 1889 reserved it "only so long as it may be needed for the use and protection of the Indians." On February 20 a second order kept back one section of the strip permanently for the Pine Ridge Boarding School: "the section of land embracing the Pine Ridge Boarding School irrigation ditch and the school pasture." It held the dam and the head of the school's irrigation ditch. Its location was given as "sec. 24, T. 35 N., R. 45 W.," a section that straddles the state line, with most of it in Sheridan County, Nebraska. It was one section, nominally 640 acres, and the record gives no exact figure.

**Breton Island.** On October 4, Roosevelt reserved Breton Island, the Old Harbor Islands and the Freemason Islands off the mouth of the Mississippi as a refuge for nesting seabirds. The full story is told in [Breton Island Bird Reservation](story:22).

**The canal.** Roosevelt appointed the Isthmian Canal Commission on March 8, 1904, under the chairmanship of Rear Admiral John G. Walker, U.S.N. (retired). The commission first met on March 22, and on March 26 Roosevelt fixed its pay: "The salary of each commissioner shall be at the rate of twelve thousand dollars a year, payable one thousand dollars per month," plus fifteen dollars a day while abroad on duty, with hotel expenses capped at seven. Every commissioner, the chairman included, drew the same salary. The order also drew a firm line: "The members shall be considered as on duty when in Washington. Any travel for private purposes, or visiting their homes, shall not be considered as traveling on duty." The bigger work of that spring, setting up a government for the Canal Zone, is told in [Governing the Panama Canal Zone: the Executive Orders of 1904](story:14).

**The rest of the year, in brief:**
- **Fort Yates, North Dakota** (January 13): the military reservation, about 2¾ square miles on the west bank of the Missouri next to the Standing Rock agency, went to the Interior Department for the Indian Service. The post had been established in December 1874; no executive order created it.
- **Fort Niobrara, Nebraska** (June 22): enlarged by about 26,000 acres, to roughly 60,000.
- **Whipple Barracks Target Range, Arizona Territory** (August 18): declared a reservation. Its roughly 1,680 acres came from three sources: this order, a railway quitclaim deed of 760 acres, and some forty acres deeded by John H. Smith and the city of Prescott.
- **Land offices**: the Oklahoma (City) land district was folded into Guthrie; the line between the Boise and Lewiston districts in Idaho was moved; and land in North Dakota passed from the Grand Forks district to Devils Lake.
- **Alaska**: Fort Egbert and the Signal Supply Station at Valdez were reduced, and the leasing of certain islands for fox farming moved from the Secretary of the Treasury to the Secretary of Commerce and Labor.
- **The Philippines**: new or adjusted Army reservations at Parang (Ludlow Barracks), Pasay, Zamboanga (Pettit Barracks), Jolo, Daraga and Augur Barracks; changes to the Manila Bay and Mariveles reservations; an ordnance-depot tract at Fort Santiago in Manila; and a larger Camp Eldridge.

## Historical context
The Guantánamo order shows how new the overseas military establishment still was. Inside the Navy's leased harbor, the President set aside ground for the Army, dividing the station between the services. That is why an Army order turns up in what is now remembered as a naval story. The split lasted: in 1938 Franklin Roosevelt's Executive Order 7800 moved part of Reservation No. 1 to the Navy, still describing it as "military reservation No. 1 at Cuzco Hills… created by order of the President dated January 9, 1904."

The fox-farming order passed along a power instead of creating one. As the Attorney General quoted it in 1905, the order directed "that the authority of the Secretary of the Treasury to lease certain islands in Alaska for the propagation of foxes, and all duties and powers pertaining thereto, shall be transferred to and vested in the Secretary of Commerce and Labor." It cited no statute. The Attorney General's view was that the Treasury practice, begun in 1882, "seems to have been originally without statutory sanction." The new Department of Commerce and Labor, created by the act of February 14, 1903, had taken over Alaska fisheries, but the leasing power had not moved with them. Roosevelt's order closed the gap.

The canal commission's pay was set before its work had really begun. On February 26, the day the canal treaty was ratified, the *San Francisco Call* reported that Roosevelt had offered the places and that the pay "had not been announced, but… would be fixed by the President and would probably be in excess of $10,000 per annum." The Senate confirmed the commission on March 3, Roosevelt's letter of appointment followed on March 8 ("Sirs: I have appointed you as the Commission"), and the salary order came eighteen days later.

At Breton Island the threat was eggers and market gunners, not plume hunters. The Audubon survey behind the order, made by Frank M. Miller, found "the eggs of the Royal and Least Terns, Laughing Gulls and Black Skimmers being used by glue manufacturers." It reported "600 dozen eggs of the Royal Tern… taken in one day by a single vessel from Old Harbor Island," and market hunters "principally in the employment of cold storage houses" killing ducks by the hundred thousand. Egrets and the millinery trade do not appear in the 1904–05 record.

## Political impact
Several of these quiet orders made news, and the two that reached furthest were covered the hardest.

**Breton Island.** The order went out on the wires the day Roosevelt signed it. The *Evening Star* reported from the cabinet room that Secretary Hitchcock had "presented to President Roosevelt for signature an order setting aside certain islands near the mouth of the Mississippi river." The *New-York Tribune* headlined it "U. S. RESERVATION FOR WILD FOWL — President Acts to Stop Slaughter of Ducks," the *Savannah Morning News* ran "TO THE AID OF THE DUCKS," and the *Washington Times* ran "TO SAVE SONG BIRDS," quoting Hitchcock and naming all three island groups. *Bird-Lore* printed the order's full text in its January–February 1905 issue. Enforcement followed fast. The Chandeleur Islands were a lighthouse reservation, not part of the order, so Frank Bond wrote the Light-House Board on October 7. The Board agreed on the 14th, and by the 21st the district inspector had ordered the keeper of the Chandeleur Light to patrol "as far as Freemason Island" against shooting, "egging," landing without permission and the taking of shells. No New Orleans daily of 1904 is digitized, so local coverage hasn't been checked.

**The Pine Ridge strip.** The restoration drew even more attention closer to home. Even before Roosevelt signed, the *Omaha Daily Bee* of January 13 ran "WANTS LAND FOR SETTLERS," and the *Beatrice Daily Express* carried "To Open Strip of Nebraska Land." The documented push came from Judge Westover of the Sheridan County district court, who wrote Representative Kinkaid urging that the land be opened and later filed a 640-acre claim on the strip himself. No proclamation opened the land. A General Land Office telegram of February 8, 1904 to the land office at Alliance did, and by February 12 the *Bee* was reporting "RUSH TO SECURE THE LANDS — Squatters Make a Break for Claims in Sheridan County… Fine Timber Along the Streams In What Has Been Known as 'No Man's Land.'" Formal entry came in June 1905, much of it in the 640-acre sections the Kinkaid Act allowed. At the agency, the Pine Ridge agent's annual report of August 1904 says nothing about the restoration, and no Oglala protest appears in the published record of 1904–05. The one mention of the strip in that year's reports concerns the companion order: the boarding-school superintendent noted that the section south of the school farm, which held the dam and head of the irrigation ditch, "has been reserved by the President and attached to the school farm."

**The canal commission.** The flat salary of twelve thousand dollars for every commissioner, chairman included, did not survive the commission's reorganization in 1906.

**Fort Niobrara.** The enlarged post did not last long as an Army post. It was abandoned in October 1906, a little over two years after Roosevelt added about 26,000 acres to it.

**The fox islands.** The Attorney General quoted the transfer order in a 1905 opinion and judged that the Treasury's leasing practice it passed along "seems to have been originally without statutory sanction."

**Guantánamo.** The January 9 order left no trace in the digitized press. A dozen searches, including "Cuzco Hills" and "Conde Bluff," found nothing, which means no notice has been found, not that none was printed.

## Legacy
Two of the year's orders cast long shadows, and both turn out to be less than they first seem.

### Guantánamo
Guantánamo Bay is still in the news more than a century later, though Roosevelt's 1904 order divided ground the United States already held instead of acquiring it. The Army's share lasted into the next Roosevelt administration: in 1938 Franklin Roosevelt's Executive Order 7800 moved part of Reservation No. 1 to the Navy, still describing it as "military reservation No. 1 at Cuzco Hills… created by order of the President dated January 9, 1904."

### Fort Niobrara
Part of Fort Niobrara did become a wildlife refuge, but the path ran through two later presidents, and Roosevelt's 1904 addition was not part of it. All but 1,093.50 acres of the old post went to the Interior Department on August 1, 1911. On January 11, 1912 President Taft's Executive Order 1461 created the Niobrara Reservation, a bird preserve of "approximately twenty-two and seven-eighths square miles." Most of the rest was opened to homesteaders by proclamation in September 1913. Every section of Taft's preserve except one school section lies inside the reservation as it stood before 1904, so the refuge grew from the old core of the post, not from Roosevelt's enlargement. The Fish and Wildlife Service gives the refuge as 13,279 acres when it was established and 19,131 acres today.

### Breton Island
Breton Island's order had a confusing afterlife. On November 11, 1905 Roosevelt signed a second, numbered order, Executive Order 369-A, covering the same three island groups, and that is the only one the State Department's numbered list carries. Roosevelt himself muddied the record: writing in 1915, he said the reservation "was set apart by presidential proclamation in 1905," while the appendix of his 1916 book lists it as "October 4, 1904. Breton Island Reservation." The Chandeleur Islands joined the refuge only in 1938 under Franklin Roosevelt's Executive Order 7983, which revoked both earlier orders. The whole became Breton National Wildlife Refuge by proclamation in 1940. A popular modern quotation blaming the reservation on Roosevelt's anger at Fifth Avenue hat designers cannot be traced to him. The full story is in [Breton Island Bird Reservation](story:22).

### The fox islands
The leasing arrangement Roosevelt moved to Commerce and Labor was later widened: Taft's Executive Order 1182½ of March 25, 1910 extended it to fur-bearing animals besides foxes.

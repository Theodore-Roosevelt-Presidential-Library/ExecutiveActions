## At a glance
Between June 13 and June 26, 1908, Roosevelt signed forty executive orders that redrew the map of the national forests across the West, almost without reserving a single new acre. The orders took giant reserves, some covering several mountain ranges under one name, and cut them into smaller, individually named national forests that one supervisor could manage. Every order took effect on July 1, 1908, the first day of the fiscal year, so the whole system changed over at once. Many of the names signed in those two weeks, including Whitman, Malheur, Umatilla, Deschutes, Flathead, Kootenai, Salmon and Boise, are still on the map.

## Background
These orders were administrative surgery, not new conservation. Each turns on the phrase "shall be known as," and each carries a careful disclaimer that it releases no land and reserves no new land. Boundary lines and names changed, but the federal title to the ground did not. Some orders are labeled "Created," for a freshly named forest carved out of part of an older, larger reserve. Others are labeled "Diminished," "Enlarged" or "Modified," squaring the edges between neighbors as the big reserves were parceled out.

The orders are short and nearly identical in form. Executive Order 820 of June 18, 1908, which created the Columbia National Forest in Washington, reads in full:

> It is hereby ordered that on and after July 1, 1908, the land within the boundary shown on the attached diagram heretofore set apart, reserved, and proclaimed as part of the Rainier National Forest, shall be known as the Columbia National Forest. It is not intended by this order to release any land from reservation or to reserve any land not heretofore embraced in a National Forest.
>
> THEODORE ROOSEVELT.
>
> THE WHITE HOUSE,
> *June 18, 1908.*

The diagram it mentions was a map attached to the order.

The work came in three waves:

- **Oregon, June 13 (Executive Orders 813–817).** The old Blue Mountains forest was broken up, and its family tree is more tangled than the headline names suggest. The Whitman and the Malheur were carved from parts of the Blue Mountains. The Umatilla took in the *entire* Heppner National Forest plus part of the Blue Mountains, and the Heppner name was dropped. The Deschutes came from parts of three forests: the Blue Mountains, the Cascade and the Fremont. The Fremont itself was not new. It had been established by proclamation on September 17, 1906, and the 1908 order only modified it, folding in the whole Goose Lake National Forest and passing other land to the Deschutes and the Umpqua. The Blue Mountains name was discontinued.
- **Washington and Utah, June 18 (Executive Orders 820–827).** The Columbia was carved from part of the Rainier, and the Rainier itself was modified. The Washington National Forest was reduced, its land distributed to the Rainier and to three new forests: the Chelan, the Snoqualmie and the Wenatchee. In Utah, the Fillmore was modified, passing a portion to the Nebo and absorbing the entire Beaver National Forest, whose name was dropped. The Nebo, named for Mount Nebo, was created from the Payson and Vernon forests plus part of the Fillmore, and both of those names were discontinued.
- **The Northern Rockies and beyond, June 25–26 (Executive Orders 833–859).** This long run reorganized forests in Montana, Colorado, Idaho, California, New Mexico, Wyoming and South Dakota. The Blackfeet and the Flathead were carved from parts of the Lewis and Clark; the Flathead revived a name that had been in use from 1897 to 1903. The Routt came from part of the Park Range; the Challis from parts of the Salmon River and Sawtooth; the Salmon from parts of the Bitter Root, Lemhi and Salmon River; the Clearwater from parts of the Coeur d'Alene and Bitter Root; the Kaniksu from part of the Priest River; the Idaho from part of the Payette; the Boise from part of the Sawtooth; and the Nezperce from parts of the Bitterroot and Weiser. The Pend d'Oreille, under that original spelling, was assembled from parts of the Cabinet, Coeur d'Alene, Kootenai and Priest River; it became "Pend Oreille" in 1910. In New Mexico, the Carson took in the entire Taos plus part of the Jemez. In California, the Angeles took in the entire San Bernardino National Forest plus parts of the Santa Barbara and San Gabriel, and the San Luis was formed from what remained of the San Luis Obispo National Forest of 1906. The Santa Barbara National Forest, which dated from a proclamation of December 22, 1903, was only modified.

None of the orders states acreages for the individual forests.

## Historical context
By 1908 the forest reserves had grown too big to run the way they had been created. More than 148 million acres were added to the national forests during Roosevelt's presidency, a net gain of about 132 million once land reclassified as farmland and opened to homesteading is subtracted. Many early reserves were huge, loosely drawn blocks. A single "Blue Mountains" or "Rainier" reserve might cover several distinct ranges and watersheds under one name and one distant supervisor.

The reserves had passed to Gifford Pinchot's bureau under the Transfer Act of February 1, 1905. It was then the Bureau of Forestry; a law of March 3, 1905 renamed it the Forest Service, effective that July. Pinchot believed in decentralized management on the ground: each forest should be small enough for one supervisor to know it well and to work closely with the ranchers, loggers and settlers who used it. Breaking the old giants into compact, named national forests put that idea into practice.

The new name had a modest legal source. The Agriculture Appropriations Act of March 4, 1907 changed it in passing, inside a funding clause for "forest reserves, which shall be known hereafter as national forests." The same law carried the Fulton Amendment, which took away the President's power to create or enlarge forest reserves in six states without Congress: "hereafter no forest reserve shall be created, nor shall any additions be made to one heretofore created within the limits of the States of Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado, or Wyoming, except by Act of Congress." That restriction shaped these orders exactly. In the six Fulton states Roosevelt could no longer add land, but he could still reorganize and rename land already reserved, and every June 1908 order says in plain words that it adds and releases nothing. In those states, reorganization was the most that could still be done without Congress.

## Political impact
The reorganization drew little public notice, and no press reaction to it has been found. It moved boundaries and names on ground that was already federal and released nothing to private use, so there was no local economic loss to rally against. The "lock-up" grievance behind the Western revolt over the reserves belonged to the fights over creating them. The big battle over Roosevelt's forest power had come the year before, over the Fulton Amendment and the "midnight forests" of March 1907 ([The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)). Next to that, the June 1908 orders were housekeeping. Where they mattered was inside the Forest Service, which came out of the summer with a rationalized map of named, supervisor-sized forests. The work continued in the last days of June and the first days of July ([The National Forests of July 1908](story:101)). Mixed in among the June forest orders were routine personnel orders, which have their own story ([The Administrative Orders of June 1908](story:100)).

## Legacy
Many of the names signed in those two weeks are still on the map. Whitman, Malheur, Umatilla, Deschutes, Fremont, Wenatchee, Snoqualmie, Flathead, Kootenai, Salmon, Boise and Routt survive as national forests or as the cores of forests that later absorbed their neighbors. None of them had been discontinued as of 1973, according to the Forest Service's own chronological record. Since 1954 the Whitman has been run jointly with the Wallowa as the Wallowa–Whitman. That pairing puts both under one supervisor's office, but the two remain legally separate forests with their own names and boundaries, a common arrangement on the Forest Service map.

Other names were folded into larger units or renamed. The Columbia National Forest created by Executive Order 820 became the Gifford Pinchot National Forest by Harry Truman's Proclamation 2845 of June 15, 1949, which names Executive Order 820 as the order that first defined the forest. The San Luis was consolidated into the Santa Barbara by an executive order of June 8, 1910, and the Santa Barbara became the Los Padres by an executive order of December 3, 1936.

The compact, individually named national forest is still the Forest Service's basic administrative unit. The layer above it came separately. The Service's six numbered districts had been created in May 1907, more than a year before these orders. On December 1, 1908 the Service was decentralized into staffed district headquarters at Missoula, Denver, Albuquerque, Ogden, San Francisco and Portland, each under a district forester. No executive order lay behind that change. It was a Department of Agriculture action, set out in a manual of procedure signed by Gifford Pinchot as Forester and approved by Secretary James Wilson. Districts became regions in 1930.

No acreages were published for individual forests at the July 1, 1908 changeover. The Forester's report for 1909 explained that the "extensive rearrangement of National Forest boundaries during the year, for better administration, with a renaming of many Forests, makes it possible to show the gross area changes only by States."

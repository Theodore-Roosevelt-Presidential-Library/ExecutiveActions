## At a glance
On November 1, 1904, one week before the election that would decide whether he won a term of his own, Roosevelt set apart Thursday, November 24, as a national day of thanksgiving. Like every Thanksgiving proclamation, it commanded nothing; it recommended. What sets it apart is its ending, which turns from counting blessings to a charge to every citizen.

## Background
This was Roosevelt's fourth annual Thanksgiving proclamation. It follows the ritual described in [Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20): a recital of the year's blessings, a call to gather in worship or at home, and no legal force. The President "do[es] recommend," and nothing more. The title "Thanksgiving Day, 1904" is an editorial label; the official printing in the *Statutes at Large* carries no title at all. Secretary of State John Hay countersigned it.

The proclamation opens by invoking "the long unbroken custom handed down to us by our forefathers." The custom was younger than that phrase suggests. After George Washington's proclamation of 1789, the national observance came and went for decades. The unbroken yearly chain began with Abraham Lincoln's proclamation of October 3, 1863. Even then the date was not fixed: Andrew Johnson named the first Thursday of December in 1865, and Ulysses S. Grant named November 18 in 1869. In 1904 the unbroken custom was forty-one years old, within the memory of many who read it.

Where the 1902 proclamation ([Thanksgiving Day, 1902](story:41)) counted blessings, this one ends with an argument. "In this great republic," it says, "the effort to combine national strength with personal freedom is being tried on a scale more gigantic than ever before in the world's history." Its success, the proclamation goes on, "must depend upon the high average of our individual citizenship, upon the way in which each of us does his duty by himself and his neighbor." One line, "Much has been given to us and much will be expected from us," echoes Luke 12:48 without saying so.

## Historical context
Roosevelt had come to the presidency at William McKinley's death in September 1901 and served out the rest of McKinley's term. The election of November 8, 1904, was for a full term of his own. By the custom of the day, a sitting president did not campaign for himself, and Roosevelt made no speaking tour that year. The proclamation's closing is best read as a theme he returned to often, that the republic depends on the character of the average citizen, rather than as a campaign message.

The proclamation calls the year "one of peace within our own borders as well as between us and all other nations." The second half held: the United States was at war with no foreign power, and in February it had declared neutrality in the war between Japan and Russia ([Declaration of Neutrality in the War Between Japan and Russia](story:13)). The first half was rosier than the facts. In the southern Philippines, American troops under Leonard Wood fought the Lanao Moros at Taraca in April 1904. In Colorado's Cripple Creek district, a labor war that had brought Governor James H. Peabody's militia in September 1903 peaked on June 6, 1904, when the railway depot at Independence was dynamited, killing thirteen non-union miners. A Thanksgiving proclamation is a ritual text, not a survey of the year, but readers should know the year was less calm than its language.

## Political impact
The proclamation went out on the wires the day after it was signed and ran widely, often in full. The Associated Press reported that it "was issued from the state department this afternoon by Secretary Hay." In California alone it appeared in the *San Francisco Call*, both Stockton dailies, Riverside's *Enterprise* ("TEDDY SAYS 'GOOD CAUSE FOR REJOICING'"), and papers from San Luis Obispo, Petaluma, Pacific Grove, Mariposa and Santa Cruz, two of them on election day itself.

The comment it drew was approving. An *Oakland Herald* editorial, reprinted as "NEW NOTE IN THANKSGIVING," found it "something unique in that while it enjoins upon the American people the duty of giving thanks to the Almighty for the blessing of another year it suggests that we have done something for ourselves." It heard "a wholesome note of self reliance," and called this "particularly characteristic of the administrative policy of President Roosevelt." A *Cincinnati Commercial Tribune* editorial, reprinted in Colorado, called its words "the characteristic language and the sincere sentiments of Theodore Roosevelt" and said "he will be an ungrateful citizen who will not cordially and sincerely respond." In Petaluma, a preacher made it the subject of his annual Thanksgiving sermon, calling it "spiritual, practical and ideal" and asking his congregation "to heed the request of the president."

The California printings carry a curiosity. Nine California papers printed "this scheme must depend upon the high average of our individual citizenship" where the official text reads "this success must depend." The AP text in Colorado and the Cincinnati editor's copy both read "success," agreeing with the *Statutes at Large*. The likeliest explanation is a slip in the telegraph relay that served the California papers, not a different version Roosevelt signed.

## Legacy
Proclamation 533 is one link in the chain of presidential Thanksgiving messages. Roosevelt issued one in each of his eight years in office. The date went on resting on custom, usually the last Thursday of November, until Franklin Roosevelt moved the holiday a week earlier in 1939, 1940 and 1941. A joint resolution of Congress approved December 26, 1941, then fixed it on the fourth Thursday.

The passage most likely to be quoted today is the closing sentence on national strength, personal freedom and "the high average of our individual citizenship." It is also the sentence where some period newspapers differ from the official text. Quote it from the *Statutes at Large*, and leave a period newspaper's "scheme" as printed when quoting that newspaper.

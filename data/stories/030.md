## At a glance
On May 11, 1908, Roosevelt reserved a 160-acre square of Montana hillside around "an extraordinary limestone cavern" in Jefferson County, using the Antiquities Act to protect a cave "of great scientific interest." The monument carried the name of Lewis and Clark, who never entered the cave. It is the only one of Roosevelt's eighteen national monuments that later left federal ownership, and today it is Lewis and Clark Caverns State Park, Montana's first state park.

## Background
Proclamation 807 (35 Stat. 2187–2188) set aside a compact square, a half-mile on a side and centered on the cave's main entrance, in the hills above the Jefferson River. The spot was "one mile more or less northeasterly from Limespur, a station and post office on the Northern Pacific railroad." The cave sits high on the mountainside: the Park Service later put the entrance "about 1,300 feet above the river and about 500 feet below the rim of Cave Mountain."

Like [Jewel Cave National Monument, South Dakota](story:29) three months earlier, this proclamation leaned on the "scientific interest" reach of section two of the Antiquities Act to protect a cave and its formations. It described the reserved ground in words rather than on a map, because no one had surveyed the land yet. The proclamation places the cave "upon unsurveyed land in township one North, range two West of Montana Meridian" and reserves "one hundred and sixty acres of land in square form with side lines running north and south and all sides equidistant from the main entrance to said cavern." No diagram was bound with it; with no survey lines, there was nothing to draw.

The warning clause is short and pointed. No one was to "appropriate, injure, or destroy any of the natural formations in the cavern," nor "locate or settle upon any of the lands reserved." Secretary of State Elihu Root countersigned. The *Statutes at Large* lists the document as "Setting aside Lewis and Clark Cavern National Monument, Montana."

**The name.** Meriwether Lewis and William Clark never entered the cave and never mentioned it. The site overlooks the stretch of the Jefferson the Corps of Discovery ascended on August 1, 1805, and came back down on July 12, 1806. None of the expedition's journalists mention a cave for those days. (On August 1, 1805, only Clark's canoe party actually ran the river past it; Lewis and three others went overland north of the river.) The name honored the association, not a visit. The proclamation itself never mentions the expedition and gives no reason for the name.

The cavern went by other names locally. It had been called Limespur Cave and Shoshone Cave, and for decades it was best known as Morrison Cave.

## Historical context
The monument was the work of the General Land Office. Its annual report for 1908 says the President, "by formal proclamation prepared in this Bureau," had created eight national monuments out of unreserved public land, Lewis and Clark Cavern among them. The same report allowed itself a moment of admiration: the cavern "is a cave whose beauty is probably second to no other in the United States." Seven weeks after the signing, Louis L. Sharp, chief of the Land Office's field division, was named the monument's supervisor. No local petition, letter of recommendation, or individual sponsor has turned up. The monument appears to have come out of the Land Office itself.

By 1908 the cave was no secret in Jefferson County. Local men knew of it in the 1890s, though accounts disagree about who found it and when. It opened to visitors about 1901. The earliest newspaper notice is "The Wonders of Limespur Cave," in the *Anaconda Standard* of December 8, 1901.

The man who turned it into an attraction was Dan A. Morrison of Whitehall, who managed a lime quarry owned by the Northern Pacific Railway. Morrison enlarged the entrance, built some 2,000 wooden steps down into the passages, and promoted the cave widely. In 1905 he filed a mineral claim on it, hoping to own it outright.

That set up a tangle typical of the West in those years: railroad land grants, a promising tourist cave, and a small federal reservation drawn on paper before anyone had run the survey lines to find out whose ground it was. Roosevelt reserved the cave as public land, "subject to any valid existing rights." The proclamation never mentions the railroad.

## Political impact
**Quiet, admiring notice.** The proclamation made little noise. The General Land Office, which had drafted it, allowed itself a moment of praise in its annual report for 1908: the cavern "is a cave whose beauty is probably second to no other in the United States." Nothing about the monument appeared in the digitized Montana papers until July 1, 1908, when the *River Press* of Fort Benton put a Helena dispatch on its front page under the headline "Montana's Mammoth Cavern." It reported that Louis L. Sharp, chief of the Land Office's field division, had been named the monument's supervisor, walked readers through the cave chamber by chamber, and offered practical advice: the only safe way in was to tie a spool of wire at the entrance and unreel it as you went. It added that the cave had long been known to a few local people and geologists but "has never been advertised by the Northern Pacific, whose trains pass the place daily." Two days later the *Whitefish Pilot* noted in its state news column that Montana had a cave comparing favorably with Kentucky's Mammoth Cave, and that Roosevelt had just set it aside under the antiquities act.

Both notices were admiring, and no opposition has been found. That silence should be read carefully. The digitized Montana papers for 1908 are all country weeklies. The Butte, Helena, Anaconda and Great Falls dailies that would have carried the news the next morning are not in that collection and have not been searched. The monument was tiny, remote, and threatened no one's livelihood. But because the land was still unsurveyed, the reservation was provisional in a way the proclamation could not yet see.

**The railroad's land.** When the General Land Office finally ran an official survey, it found that the 160 acres lay wholly within the Northern Pacific Railway's land grant: not yet patented to the railway, but the railway's to claim. The government had proclaimed a monument on ground it did not own. Dan Morrison, the cave's promoter, ran into the same fact when he tried to secure title under the mining laws. There was no lawsuit. As Senator Thomas J. Walsh of Montana later told the Senate (January 26, 1929), "in his endeavor to do so it was disclosed that the property really belonged to the Northern Pacific Railroad Co. under its grant." It was a title problem uncovered in a land-office filing, with no adverse party and no judgment.

The railway settled it generously. By quitclaim deed No. 18129E, dated February 14, 1911, it gave up Lot 12, Section 17, T1N R2W, to the United States "for the purpose of maintaining thereon the said Lewis and Clark Cavern National Monument." The gift had a string attached: the relinquishment would "become void and the premises immediately revert to the grantor should the Monument no longer be maintained." The land, in other words, would go back to the railway, not to the government.

President Taft then confirmed the same 160 acres on the new survey with Proclamation 1123 of May 16, 1911, expressly subject to the deed's conditions. It stated the finding plainly: "such survey having determined that the tract in question lies wholly within the limits of the grant of the Northern Pacific Railway Company but has not yet been patented to that company." This time a General Land Office plate, headed with the tract's surveyed description, was bound with the proclamation, a neat illustration of what "unsurveyed" had meant three years before.

## Legacy
**Morrison's reward.** Congress eventually paid the man who had opened the cave. A private act of February 4, 1929, granted Dan A. Morrison of Whitehall $5,000 "in full reimbursement of and remuneration for all expenses incurred and services performed in all matters touching the discovery, exploration, and development of the cavern known as the Lewis and Clark Cavern National Monument." Walsh told the Senate that Morrison had spent "about $15,000 or $20,000" opening the cave and building the approaches, and that the government had ended up owning all of it. As Representative Scott Leavitt first introduced the bill, it would also have given Morrison a ten-year concession to guide visitors; the House Committee on Claims struck that part and passed only the money.

**A neglected monument.** The cave was hard to reach and had no money behind it. A 1930 Park Service account places it about five miles off the Yellowstone Trail near Cardwell, reached by "a poor road [that] ascends part way up the mountain," and then a trail "requiring a climb of about 45 minutes." There were no lights and no rangers, and the cave was closed for years "on account of vandalism and lack of funds to put in a lighting system and ranger protection." A table of national monuments printed in the *Congressional Record* on April 7, 1930, put it more briefly: "Now closed to public because of depredations by vandals."

**The CCC.** In 1934, according to the National Register nomination, Governor Frank Cooney asked the federal government to improve access and develop the monument for visitors, which led to a year-long survey. Civilian Conservation Corps crews transformed the place between 1935 and 1941. Company 574, about two hundred men at Camp Cavern near Whitehall, cleared tons of bat guano, spent a year plotting a tour route, surveyed the cave by compass and transit, built rock stairways, and drove a 538-foot exit tunnel with doors at each end to control the airflow. They put in the first electric lighting and built the 3.2-mile road up Greer Gulch, along with a lodge, headquarters, guide cabins, and the first telephone, water and sewer lines.

**The transfer.** The case for handing the cave to Montana came down to money. In a letter of April 5, 1937, printed in the Senate committee report on the bill, Acting Secretary of the Interior Charles West explained that "the State of Montana has signified its desire to administer the tract … as a State park, and as the Congress has not appropriated funds for the administration thereof as a national monument, it appears desirable that said tract be transferred to the State of Montana." He added that local interests and the advisory board of Montana Grazing District No. 5 favored the change, and the Bureau of the Budget had no objection. Representative James F. O'Connor of Montana made the same point to the House on August 16, 1937: "The United States Government has made no appropriation to take care of this territory, but the State of Montana has." Asked whether Montana would come back later asking for money, he answered, "Oh, no. We are simply asking to be allowed to take care of it."

The bill was S. 1216, introduced by Senator Burton K. Wheeler of Montana. It passed the Senate without debate on June 28, 1937, and the House on August 16. The Act of August 24, 1937 (50 Stat. 746–747) "authorized and directed" the Secretary of the Interior to convey the land to Montana "to be used for the purposes of a public park and recreational site." The grant ran well beyond the old monument, through six sections in two townships, about 1,500 acres by O'Connor's count, and the United States kept all mineral deposits. Montana would lose the land if it failed to make it a park within five years, let it go unused as a park for any five years in a row, or put any part of it to another use. In that case the Secretary could "assume jurisdiction" of the original 160 acres "for national-monument purposes." Interior had asked for that clause because the land had come back from the railway for monument use. The monument ended as a consequence of the transfer, not through any word of abolition.

**Montana's park.** Montana kept its end of the bargain. A 1941 state survey counted the park's receipts from September 10, 1939, "when the cave was opened to the public," and the park was dedicated on May 4, 1941. A driving rain canceled the ceremony, but some three thousand people drove the new road up anyway. The name took a while to settle. The 1939 WPA guide to Montana still sent travelers to "LEWIS AND CLARK CAVERN (guides), a National Monument in Jefferson Canyon," noting that it "is known locally as Morrison Cave." That same 1941 survey called it "Morrison Cave state park," and the 1942 state highway map listed "Morrison Cave State Park, marvelous limestone cavern," even as postcards from about 1941 were already captioned "Lewis and Clark Caverns State Park."

Lewis and Clark Caverns is counted as Montana's first state park: the state's own acquisition list dates it to October 18, 1935, and the next-earliest park to December 1940, though Montana had no state park system until the 1940 legislature created one. Today the park covers 3,015 acres, by Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks' figure.

**The one that left.** Lewis and Clark Cavern is the only one of Roosevelt's eighteen national monuments that left federal ownership. Six others were abolished on paper when Congress made national parks or historical parks of the same ground, and every acre stayed federal. Wheeler National Monument was abolished outright in 1950, but its 300 acres went back into the Rio Grande National Forest, where they remain as the Wheeler Geologic Area. A short-lived federal monument, proclaimed on what turned out to be a railroad's land, became one of Montana's best-known attractions.

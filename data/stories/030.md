## At a glance
On May 11, 1908, Roosevelt reserved a 160-acre square of Montana hillside around "an extraordinary limestone cavern" in Jefferson County, using the Antiquities Act to protect a cave "of great scientific interest." The monument carried the name of Lewis and Clark, who never entered the cave. It is the only one of Roosevelt's eighteen national monuments that later left federal ownership, and today it is Lewis and Clark Caverns State Park, Montana's first state park.

## Background
Proclamation 807 (35 Stat. 2187–2188) set aside a compact square, a half-mile on a side and centered on the cave's main entrance, in the hills above the Jefferson River. The spot was "one mile more or less northeasterly from Limespur, a station and post office on the Northern Pacific railroad." The cave sits high on the mountainside: the Park Service later put the entrance "about 1,300 feet above the river and about 500 feet below the rim of Cave Mountain."

Like [Jewel Cave National Monument, South Dakota](story:29) three months earlier, this proclamation leaned on the "scientific interest" reach of section two of the Antiquities Act to protect a cave and its formations. It described the reserved ground in words rather than on a map, because no one had surveyed the land yet. The proclamation places the cave "upon unsurveyed land in township one North, range two West of Montana Meridian" and reserves "one hundred and sixty acres of land in square form with side lines running north and south and all sides equidistant from the main entrance to said cavern." No diagram was bound with it; with no survey lines, there was nothing to draw.

The warning clause is short and pointed. No one was to "appropriate, injure, or destroy any of the natural formations in the cavern," nor "locate or settle upon any of the lands reserved." Secretary of State Elihu Root countersigned. The *Statutes at Large* lists the document as "Setting aside Lewis and Clark Cavern National Monument, Montana."

**The name.** Meriwether Lewis and William Clark never entered the cave and never mentioned it. The site overlooks the stretch of the Jefferson the Corps of Discovery ascended on August 1, 1805, and came back down on July 12, 1806. None of the expedition's journalists mention a cave for those days. (On August 1, 1805, only Clark's canoe party actually ran the river past it; Lewis and three others went overland north of the river.) The name honored the association, not a visit. The proclamation itself never mentions the expedition and gives no reason for the name.

The cavern went by other names locally. It had been called Limespur Cave and Shoshone Cave, and for decades it was best known as Morrison Cave.

## Historical context
The monument was the work of the General Land Office. Its annual report for 1908 says the President, "by formal proclamation prepared in this Bureau," had created eight national monuments out of unreserved public land, Lewis and Clark Cavern among them. The same report allowed itself a moment of admiration: the cavern "is a cave whose beauty is probably second to no other in the United States." Seven weeks after the signing, Louis L. Sharp, chief of the Land Office's field division, was named the monument's supervisor. No local petition, letter of recommendation, or individual sponsor has turned up. The monument appears to have come out of the Land Office itself.

By 1908 the cave was no secret in Jefferson County. Local men knew of it in the 1890s, though accounts disagree about who found it and when. It opened to visitors about 1901. The earliest newspaper notice is "The Wonders of Limespur Cave," in the *Anaconda Standard* of December 8, 1901.

The man who turned it into an attraction was Dan A. Morrison of Whitehall, who managed a lime quarry owned by the Northern Pacific Railway. Morrison enlarged the entrance, built some 2,000 wooden steps down into the passages, and promoted the cave widely. In 1905 he filed a mineral claim on it, hoping to own it outright.

That set up a tangle typical of the West in those years: railroad land grants, a promising tourist cave, and a small federal reservation drawn on paper before anyone had run the survey lines to find out whose ground it was. Roosevelt reserved the cave as public land, "subject to any valid existing rights." The proclamation never mentions the railroad.

## Political impact
The proclamation made little noise. Nothing about it appeared in the digitized Montana papers until July 1, 1908, when the *River Press* of Fort Benton put a Helena dispatch on its front page under the headline "Montana's Mammoth Cavern." The story reported Sharp's appointment, walked readers through the cave chamber by chamber, and offered practical advice: the only safe way in was to tie a spool of wire at the entrance and unreel it as you went. It added that the cave "has never been advertised by the Northern Pacific, whose trains pass the place daily." Two days later the *Whitefish Pilot* noted that Montana had a cave comparing favorably with Kentucky's Mammoth Cave, and that Roosevelt had just set it aside under the antiquities act.

Both notices were admiring, and no opposition has been found. That silence should be read carefully, though. The surviving digitized papers for 1908 Montana are country weeklies. The Butte, Helena, Anaconda and Great Falls dailies that would have carried the news the next morning have not been searched.

The monument was tiny, remote, and threatened no one's livelihood. But because the land was still unsurveyed, the reservation was provisional in a way the proclamation could not yet see.

## Legacy
**The railroad's land.** When the General Land Office finally surveyed the tract, it found that the 160 acres lay wholly within the Northern Pacific Railway's land grant. The government had proclaimed a monument on ground it did not own. Morrison learned the same thing when he tried to secure title under the mining laws. As Senator Thomas J. Walsh of Montana told the Senate in 1929, "in his endeavor to do so it was disclosed that the property really belonged to the Northern Pacific Railroad Co. under its grant." There was no lawsuit, just a title problem discovered in a land-office filing.

The railway resolved it. By a quitclaim deed dated February 14, 1911, it gave the land to the United States "for the purpose of maintaining thereon the said Lewis and Clark Cavern National Monument," on the condition that the land would "immediately revert to the grantor" if the monument was ever abandoned. In other words, the land would go back to the railway, not to the government. President Taft's Proclamation 1123 of May 16, 1911, confirmed the same 160 acres on the new survey. This time a General Land Office plate was bound with it, a neat illustration of what "unsurveyed" had meant three years before.

Congress eventually paid Morrison. A private act of February 4, 1929, granted Dan A. Morrison of Whitehall $5,000 "in full reimbursement of and remuneration for all expenses incurred and services performed in all matters touching the discovery, exploration, and development of the cavern." Walsh said Morrison had spent "about $15,000 or $20,000" on it.

**A neglected monument.** The cave was hard to reach and had no money behind it. A 1930 Park Service account places it about five miles off the Yellowstone Trail near Cardwell, reached by "a poor road [that] ascends part way up the mountain," and then a trail "requiring a climb of about 45 minutes." There were no lights and no rangers. The cave was closed for years "on account of vandalism and lack of funds to put in a lighting system and ranger protection."

In 1934, according to the National Register nomination, Governor Frank Cooney asked the federal government to improve access and develop the monument for visitors. The Civilian Conservation Corps changed that between 1935 and 1941. Company 574, about two hundred men at Camp Cavern near Whitehall, cleared tons of bat guano, surveyed the cave, built rock stairways, drove a 538-foot exit tunnel, put in the first electric lighting, and built the 3.2-mile road up Greer Gulch, along with a lodge, cabins, and the first telephone, water and sewer lines.

**The transfer.** The case for handing the cave to Montana came down to money. The State of Montana told the Interior Department it wanted to run the site as a state park, and Interior agreed, because Congress had never funded the monument. Representative James F. O'Connor of Montana put it to the House on August 16, 1937: "The United States Government has made no appropriation to take care of this territory, but the State of Montana has." Asked whether Montana would come back later asking for money, he answered, "Oh, no. We are simply asking to be allowed to take care of it."

The bill was S. 1216, introduced by Senator Burton K. Wheeler of Montana. The Act of August 24, 1937 (50 Stat. 746–747) "authorized and directed" the Secretary of the Interior to convey the land to Montana "to be used for the purposes of a public park and recreational site." The grant ran well beyond the old monument, about 1,500 acres in all, and the United States kept the mineral rights. If Montana failed to use the land as a park, the Secretary could declare it forfeit and "assume jurisdiction" of the original 160 acres "for national-monument purposes." Interior asked for that clause because the land had come back from the railway for monument use. The monument ended as a side effect of the transfer, not through any formal abolition.

Montana kept its end of the bargain. The state dedicated the park on May 4, 1941. Rain canceled the ceremony, but some three thousand people drove the new road up anyway. Even then the state was still calling it "Morrison Cave State Park" in 1942, although postcards were already using "Lewis and Clark Caverns" by 1941.

Lewis and Clark Cavern is the only one of Roosevelt's eighteen national monuments that left federal ownership. Several others were abolished on paper when Congress made national parks of the same ground, and Wheeler National Monument was abolished outright in 1950, but its land stayed federal. Today Lewis and Clark Caverns State Park covers 3,015 acres and is counted as Montana's first state park. A short-lived federal monument, proclaimed on what turned out to be a railroad's land, became one of the state's best-known attractions.

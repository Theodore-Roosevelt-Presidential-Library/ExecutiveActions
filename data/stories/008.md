## At a glance
On December 8, 1906, Theodore Roosevelt signed three proclamations under the brand-new Antiquities Act: El Morro in New Mexico, Montezuma Castle in Arizona, and Petrified Forest in Arizona. After [Devils Tower](story:2), they were the second, third, and fourth national monuments. They are the kind of places the law was written for, and all three are still protected today.

## Background
The three proclamations share a single day and a common form. Each opens by reciting section two of the Antiquities Act, including its limit to "the smallest area compatible with the proper care and management of the objects to be protected." Each was countersigned by Secretary of State Elihu Root, and each made a General Land Office map "a part of this proclamation," with the acreage printed on its face.

- **El Morro (Proclamation 695), 160 acres.** It protected "the rocks known as El Morro and Inscription Rock in the Territory of New Mexico," which were "of the greatest historical value."
- **Montezuma Castle (Proclamation 696), 160 acres.** It protected "the prehistoric structure known as Montezuma's Castle in the Territory of Arizona," "of the greatest ethnological value and scientific interest."
- **Petrified Forest (Proclamation 697), 60,776.02 acres.** It protected "the mineralized remains of Mesozoic forests, commonly known as the 'Petrified Forest,'" in Gila and Apache counties, "of the greatest scientific interest and value."

Devils Tower had been first, in September 1906. Nothing was proclaimed between then and December 8, and [Chaco Canyon](story:5) followed in March 1907 as the fifth. Roosevelt proclaimed eighteen national monuments in all. The three December proclamations were issued the same day; no record shows the order in which they were signed.

## Historical context
These were the cases the Antiquities Act was meant for. It is worth noticing, though, that none of the three proclamations names a threat. Each speaks of value: historical, ethnological, scientific. The danger was real, and it moved the people who pushed for these reservations, but it shows up in letters, field reports, and the popular press, not in the documents themselves. (The law's own phrase, worth quoting whole, is "other objects of historic or scientific interest.")

**El Morro** is a bluff of soft Zuni Sandstone, and travelers had been carving into it for centuries. The most famous inscription is Juan de Oñate's of April 16, 1605, which the National Park Service reads as "Paso por aqui el adelantado Don Juan de Onate del descubrimiento de la mar del sur a 16 de Abril de 1605." Around it are more than 2,000 inscriptions, petroglyphs, and pictographs, from Ancestral Puebloan marks through Spanish, and on to American surveyors, soldiers, and emigrants. The concern was people, not weather. Charles F. Lummis wrote in 1892 that "some of these late-comers have been vandals, and have even erased the names of ancient heroes to make a smooth place for their 'John Jones,'" and that "the government should protect it, as it would be protected in any other civilized land."

**Montezuma Castle** is a five-story, twenty-room Southern Sinagua cliff dwelling, built in the early 1100s and empty by the early 1400s. It is neither Aztec nor a castle. The name went first to nearby Montezuma Well in 1864, given by a militia expedition under King S. Woolsey; Henry Clifton wrote in the *Arizona Miner*, "They were probably built by the Aztecs. We gave the name of Montezuma to the well." The Castle picked up the name in print in 1890, from army surgeon Edgar A. Mearns. Here the damage was documented firsthand. Lummis warned in January 1897 that "heedless relic-hunters have so undermined the walls that some of them are in danger of falling; and when the process begins, the whole castle will go very fast." General Land Office Special Agent George F. Wilson, inspecting on July 25, 1904, reported that "in one of the upper rooms a charge of dynamite was used to break down an inner wall, in the search for relics." Mearns himself had carried off several thousand specimens, Richard Wetherill dug burials there in 1896, and in the summer of 1897 the Arizona Antiquarian Association raised about $150 to shore the building up.

**Petrified Forest** was the industrial case, measured in railroad cars. Lester F. Ward reported to the U.S. Geological Survey on December 12, 1899 that his teamster "was employed for a long time hauling these trunks out of the upper forest to Carrizo station," and that "many carloads of it were shipped to the East." Arizona's territorial memorial of 1895 complained that "carloads of the limbs and smaller pieces are being shipped away to be ground up for various purposes," and described "blasting them in pieces in search of crystals." Drake & Co. of Sioux Falls cut the wood into table tops and mantels, with Tiffany & Co. among the buyers.

The best-known threat had already faded by 1906. The Armstrong Abrasive Company filed claims on 602.10 acres in December 1895, shipped machinery to Adamana in November 1896, and finished a stamp mill by January 1897 to grind the wood into abrasive. By the best account the mill never ran, beaten by cheaper Canadian emery, and the venture was dead by the end of 1897. Ward's argument for protection looked ahead rather than back: "there is always the danger that some powerful mercantile interest may do its destructive work." The push for a reservation came from that 1895 memorial and from Representative John F. Lacey's park bills of 1900, 1902, 1903, and 1905, each passed by the House and lost in the Senate.

## Political impact
The three monuments met no opposition that has been found, and barely any notice. No Arizona or New Mexico paper reported them in December 1906. The first word came in an Associated Press dispatch from Los Angeles on January 2, 1907, reprinted as filler across the territories, with the counties wrong. El Morro's first local notice appeared in the *Santa Fe New Mexican* on February 13, 1907, under the name "Inscription Rock." Montezuma Castle seems to have gone unreported in Arizona entirely. New Mexico's papers approved, and used the new monuments to lobby for more. Around Petrified Forest, ordinary business carried on: in March 1907 the *Holbrook Argus* reported Adamana outfitters hauling "about one hundred eastern tourists to and from the petrified forest."

Petrified Forest also became one of the first real tests of the "smallest area" limit. Taft's Proclamation 1167 of July 31, 1911 found that the monument had been shown, "through a careful geological survey of its deposits of mineralized forest remains, to reserve a much larger area of land than is necessary to protect the objects for which the Monument was created, and therefore the same should be reduced in area to conform to the requirements of the act authorizing the creation of National Monuments." The cut left about 25,626 acres. The *Arizona Republican* credited "the reports of the experts," and nothing in the record points to settler pressure. Arizona at the time was asking for better access to the monument, not its removal.

## Legacy
All three remain under the National Park Service. Petrified Forest became a national park in two steps: Congress authorized the change on March 28, 1958, and the park was established on December 9, 1962. The Petrified Forest National Park Expansion Act of 2004 authorized a larger boundary of roughly 125,000 acres on a willing-seller basis. Today Petrified Forest National Park covers 221,390.21 acres, El Morro National Monument 1,278.72 acres, and Montezuma Castle National Monument 1,015.52 acres, the last in two detached pieces after Montezuma Well was added in 1943. Chaco has been Chaco Culture National Historical Park since 1980.

Their founding purpose, stopping the removal and destruction of things that cannot be replaced, is still their core job. The parks still police the theft of antiquities and of petrified wood that is more than 200 million years old.

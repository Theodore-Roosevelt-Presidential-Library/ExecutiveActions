## At a glance
On November 17, 1903, Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed that Cuba gave American authors copyright protection on the same terms as its own citizens. Under the copyright law of 1891, that finding opened U.S. copyright to Cuban authors. It was a small, routine step, signed in the middle of the much larger fight over trade with the young Cuban republic. The other half of the bargain, protection for Americans in Cuba, had been put in place by the U.S. military government a few years earlier.

## Background
Proclamation 510 did not create any new power. It carried out a step that Congress had handed to the President. Section 13 of the Copyright Act of March 3, 1891, often called the Chace Act, extended U.S. copyright to foreign authors, but only on a reciprocal basis. The act would apply to a foreign country's citizens only "when such foreign state or nation permits to citizens of the United States of America the benefit of copyright on substantially the same basis as its own citizens," or when that country belonged to an international copyright agreement the United States could join. And "the existence of either of the conditions aforesaid shall be determined by the President of the United States by proclamation."

Roosevelt's proclamation recited that "satisfactory official assurances have been given that in Cuba the law permits to citizens of the United States the benefit of copyright on substantially the same basis as to the citizens of Cuba," and declared "that the first of the conditions specified in section 13 of the act of March 3, 1891, now exists and is fulfilled in respect to the citizens of Cuba." Secretary of State John Hay countersigned it.

From then on, Cuban authors could secure American copyright on the same terms as other foreigners. That included the 1891 act's manufacturing requirement: a foreign author's book had to be printed from type set in the United States to qualify.

The proclamation opened the American half of the relationship only. The Cuban half was already in place, and the United States had put it there. During the American military occupation, Order No. 119 of March 19, 1900, issued over the name of Adna R. Chaffee as chief of staff, extended to authors of foreign works the protection of Spain's intellectual-property law of 1879. Order No. 55 of February 13, 1901, simplified the paperwork for registering foreign works. Order No. 160 of June 13, 1901, issued on instructions from the Secretary of War, directed that copyrights registered with the Librarian of Congress be protected in Cuba. On February 25, 1903, the new Cuban republic told the international copyright bureau at Berne that these orders were still in force. The Cuban law that Roosevelt certified in November 1903 was, in large part, the occupation's own work, carried forward by the republic.

## Historical context
The Republic of Cuba was new. Tomás Estrada Palma had been inaugurated on May 20, 1902, at the end of the American military occupation, and 1903 was crowded with agreements between the two countries. February brought the naval-station lease that became Guantánamo. May 22 brought the Treaty of Relations that wrote the Platt Amendment into a treaty. The biggest item was trade. A commercial convention signed at Havana on December 11, 1902, promised Cuban goods a 20 percent cut from American tariff rates, and it needed an act of Congress to take effect.

Roosevelt called Congress into special session on November 9, 1903, expressly to approve that convention (Proclamation 506). Proclamation 510 was signed eight days into the session, while the trade bill was still pending. The bill did not become law until December 17, 1903, after the regular session had opened. The day after he countersigned the copyright proclamation, John Hay signed the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty with the new Republic of Panama — a fair measure of how small a matter Cuban copyright was on his desk that week.

The copyright finding itself followed a well-worn path. Since 1891, Presidents had certified one country after another: Belgium, France, Great Britain and its possessions, and Switzerland in the first proclamation of July 1, 1891; then Germany, Italy, Denmark, Portugal, Spain, Mexico, Chile, Costa Rica, and the Netherlands through 1899. Cuba came next. Norway (1905) and Austria (1907) followed during Roosevelt's presidency, while China and Japan were covered by treaty instead. The relationship with Spain, interrupted by the war of 1898, had been restored in late 1902 by an exchange of diplomatic notes. The United States was moving toward honoring foreign authors' rights, but cautiously, country by country, on strictly reciprocal terms, and with the manufacturing clause protecting American printers.

## Political impact
No controversy over the proclamation has been found. It was a mechanical step the 1891 law assigned to the President, and it was overshadowed by the far larger Cuban questions of the moment: the Platt Amendment relationship and the trade bill then before the special session of Congress (see [The Convening Proclamations of 1903](story:45)).

It was not announced, but word got out. A Washington dispatch of November 19, printed in the *San Francisco Call* the next day under the headline "Copyright Laws Extend to Cuba," reported that "Senor Don Gonzales de Quesada, the Cuban Minister, to-day called upon Secretary Hay regarding the application of United States copyright laws to Cuban authors," and that "although the document had not been made public, President Roosevelt several days ago issued a proclamation." That shows the Cuban minister pressing the matter around the time of the signing, though not who started it. In California's digitized papers, it was the only item on Cuban copyright for the rest of 1903.

The people the proclamation concerned took note too. The Berne copyright union's official monthly, *Le Droit d'Auteur*, reported it in its December 15, 1903, issue, noting that Roosevelt had signed it, John Hay had countersigned it, and its text matched the earlier proclamations extending the 1891 law to other countries. In short, the proclamation got a short wire notice and a line in the international copyright press, not public debate.

## Legacy
The proclamation has no separate legacy beyond the copyright relationship it opened, and that relationship was later rebuilt on new foundations. After Congress passed a new Copyright Act in 1909, Cuba was named again in a proclamation covering several countries on April 9, 1910, and in a proclamation of November 27, 1911, that took effect from May 29, 1911.

From there, the two countries' copyright relations moved to multilateral treaties:

- the **Buenos Aires Convention**, in effect for Cuba from May 28, 1913;
- the **Universal Copyright Convention**, from June 18, 1957;
- and, on the American side, the comprehensive copyright law of 1976.

The United States joined the Berne Convention on March 1, 1989, under the Berne Convention Implementation Act of 1988.

Proclamation 510 survives as one entry in the long list of proclamations issued under the Chace Act, alongside those Roosevelt signed for [Norway](doc:proc-577) in 1905 and [Austria](doc:proc-774) in 1907. It is a reminder that international copyright, now handled by multilateral treaties, once had to be certified one country at a time, by name.

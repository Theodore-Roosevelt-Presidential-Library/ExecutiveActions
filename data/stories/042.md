## At a glance
In 1903 Theodore Roosevelt signed ten proclamations that set aside, enlarged, or rebuilt forest reserves: public land closed to settlement and held for its timber and its water. Nine were on the mainland, in Utah, Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, and California. The tenth reserved the forested slopes of Puerto Rico's Luquillo Mountains, the first tropical forest reserve created by a U.S. president and the forest known today as El Yunque. Most drew little national notice, but in Utah's Sanpete County one set off a sharp fight over sheep and water.

## Background
Each of these proclamations took a block of the public domain out of reach of homesteaders, miners, and timber buyers and kept it in federal hands. Seven of them — Manti, Logan, Lewis and Clarke, Pocatello, Aquarius, Payson, and Highwood Mountains — cited in their own text "section twenty-four of the Act of Congress, approved March third, eighteen hundred and ninety-one." That section of the General Revision Act, known as the "Creative Act," let the President reserve forested public land by proclamation. Each used the same "public good would be promoted" language and the same warning against occupying the land that appeared in [The Forest Reserves of 1902](story:33).

The ten, in order of signing:

- **Luquillo** (Proc. 495, Jan. 17; Puerto Rico) — a boundary enclosing about 65,950 acres
- **Yellowstone** (Proc. 496, Jan. 29; Wyoming and Montana) — 8,329,200 acres. Its official title reads *Enlargement of the Yellowstone National Reserve*, with no "Forest."
- **Manti** (Proc. 499, May 29; Utah) — 584,640 acres
- **Logan** (Proc. 500, May 29; Utah) — 182,080 acres
- **Lewis and Clarke** (Proc. 501, June 9; Montana) — 4,670,720 acres
- **Pocatello** (Proc. 505, Sept. 5; Idaho) — 49,920 acres
- **Aquarius** (Proc. 507, Oct. 24; Utah) — 639,000 acres
- **Payson** (Proc. 509, Nov. 5; Utah) — enlarged from 86,400 to 111,600 acres
- **Highwood Mountains** (Proc. 511, Dec. 12; Montana) — 45,080 acres
- **Santa Barbara** (Proc. 512, Dec. 22; California) — 1,838,323 acres

The acreages are the General Land Office's own estimates.

Three of the ten were really consolidations, not the simple new reserves their titles suggest. The Yellowstone proclamation declared that "the proclamations heretofore issued respecting said forest reserves are hereby superseded, and The Yellowstone Forest Reserve is hereby established in place thereof." It folded the Yellowstone and Teton reserves in Wyoming and the Absaroka in Montana into one reserve, with additions. The Lewis and Clarke proclamation replaced two 1897 reserves, the Lewis and Clarke and the Flathead. (The proclamation spells the name with a final "e," which the government kept until 1907.) The Santa Barbara proclamation merged the Pine Mountain and Zaca Lake reserve of 1898 with the Santa Ynez reserve of 1899. Because they rebuilt existing reserves, the Yellowstone and Santa Barbara proclamations cited the Organic Act of June 4, 1897, alongside the 1891 law.

All of these reserves were run by the General Land Office in the Interior Department.

**Luquillo stands apart.** It was not made under the 1891 forest law at all. Puerto Rico had come to the United States from Spain in 1898, and a special act of July 1, 1902, gave the President one year to reserve public lands and buildings on the island "for military, naval, light-house, marine-hospital, post-offices, custom-houses, United States courts, and other public purposes." It was not a forestry law. Roosevelt used that general power, declared the tract "a Public Forest Reservation," and signed with five months to spare. The proclamation said so in its first lines:

> Whereas, it is provided by section one of the Act of Congress, approved July first, nineteen hundred and two, entitled, "An Act Authorizing the President to reserve public lands and buildings in the island of Porto Rico for public uses, and granting other public lands and buildings to the government of Porto Rico, and for other purposes" …

It closed in the familiar way: "Warning is hereby expressly given to all persons not to occupy or use the lands reserved by this proclamation. The reservation hereby established shall be known as The Luquillo Forest Reserve."

The reserve's boundary was a rectangle drawn by latitude and longitude around the Luquillo Mountains in the island's northeast. It enclosed roughly 65,950 acres, but the proclamation reserved only "public lands, not heretofore appropriated or reserved" inside it. John C. Gifford, writing for the Bureau of Forestry in 1905, judged that "Probably less than 30 per cent is Government land unclaimed by private owners. The amount of Federal forest land, roughly estimated, is 20,000 acres." A survey in 1916 found about 12,400 acres.

## Historical context
The thinking behind the mainland reserves was the thinking behind the whole conservation program in these years, told more fully in [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9). People feared a coming "timber famine." They distrusted speculators and monopolists who might corner timber and range. Above all, they believed forested headwaters had to stay in public hands to protect the streams that farms, towns, and the new federal irrigation projects of [The Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21) depended on. Roosevelt would put the case plainly in his Seventh Annual Message of 1907, calling the exhaustion of natural resources "the fundamental problem which underlies almost every other problem of our National life." In 1903 that argument was already the working rule.

The map shows it. The Utah and Montana reserves were drawn around the high, timbered plateaus and mountain fronts — the Wasatch Plateau above Manti, the Bear River Range above Logan, the Aquarius Plateau, the Highwood Mountains — that fed the irrigated valleys below.

Luquillo had a longer history. Under Spain, the Sierra de Luquillo had been state forest land. An 1870 inventory already listed "monte Sierra de Luquillo" and "Yunque" among the island's *montes del Estado*. A royal decree of April 21, 1876, approved a forestry code for Cuba and Puerto Rico that told the island's forest inspector to catalogue public forests that should be kept from sale. Under that code, Puerto Rico's forest service catalogued the Sierra de Luquillo in 1877 and measured it at 10,057.7194 hectares in 1885. The 1876 decree did not name Luquillo, and no Spanish order ever created a Luquillo reserve. The land passed to the United States as former Crown property. As Louis Murphy of the Forest Service later put it: "Upon the transfer of the island to American sovereignty the Spanish Crown lands in the Sierra de Luquillo became the property of the Federal Government." Roosevelt's proclamation brought that land into the American reserve system, on the belief that the island's timber, and above all its water, were public goods worth guarding.

## Political impact
Like the 1902 reserves, the 1903 proclamations drew little national attention. Making forest reserves was by now a routine use of a settled power, years before the 1907 fight over the "midnight" reserves made the President's authority a national quarrel. Most of the ten earned a line or two of wire copy and no editorials. Luquillo barely registered on the mainland.

Locally, though, several of them mattered a great deal, and not always in the way one might expect.

**Manti set off a real fight.** By early February 1904, Sanpete County wool growers were meeting in convention at Manti and cattlemen at Ephraim. The towns of Gunnison and Mayfield petitioned the Secretary of the Interior on behalf of "a population of about 3,000 souls… solely dependent on the waters of Twelve Mile creek." The *Salt Lake Herald* ran the story under the headline "RIGHTS OF PEOPLE AS AGAINST SHEEP" (Feb. 22, 1904). Manti residents sent a counter-petition, and the *Deseret Evening News* reported the claim that the federal order "would cause the destruction of 200,000 sheep" (Feb. 17, 1904). After Senators Reed Smoot and Thomas Kearns stepped in — Smoot going to Gifford Pinchot personally — Interior raised the grazing limit from 175,000 head to 300,000. The *Salt Lake Tribune* called it "AN EXCELLENT COMPROMISE" (Feb. 8, 1904).

**Pocatello** arrived with a conflict already under way. In September 1903 a wire story that ran coast to coast reported a federal lawsuit against southeastern Idaho stockmen, a sitting state senator among them, for grazing 28,000 sheep on the Portneuf headwaters and fouling Pocatello's water supply. When a syndicated survey of Idaho protest against reserves appeared in March 1904, it made an exception for this one: "this latter, however, is to protect the water supply of Pocatello and a necessity existed for its creation."

**Santa Barbara** cheered. The *Morning Press* of December 23, 1903, the day after the signing, announced: "Proclamation Signed by the President Means Much For Santa Barbara. Insures For All Time A Sufficient Water Supply For This City — Efforts of Senator Bard and Others Successful After Many Failures." Working from a telegram sent by Senator Thomas R. Bard, the paper reported that the Rancho Los Prietos y Najalayegua, owned by J. L. Washburn of Duluth and the Santa Barbara Water Company, was coming into the reserve after "over two years" of effort in Washington. The request had twice been turned down in the Interior Department before it was "brought to the personal attention of the President," who sent it back for more study. Forest officers, and finally Pinchot himself, examined the tract and urged that it be taken in. According to the paper, the ranch was to be exchanged for other government land, "timber and mineral producing lands excluded." Its value lay in "securing the water shed of the river and also the various reservoir and dam sites necessary to the construction of the city water system." The *Los Angeles Herald* carried the news the same day under "44,000 ACRES ADDED TO FOREST RESERVE." Those first reports took the proclamation as an addition to the old reserves and did not yet grasp that it merged them.

Two more cut against the usual picture of Western resistance. In Montana, a petition *asking* for a supervisor to be appointed for the Highwood Mountains reserve was "being generously signed" (*River Press*, Fort Benton, June 24, 1903). And in March 1904 the *Salt Lake Tribune* reported that "persons living on and adjacent to the Aquarius forest reserve in this county are well satisfied with the reserve."

The 1903 reserves, then, did not start a national argument. The quarrels they did start were local, and they were about grazing permits and water, not about whether the President had the power to make reserves at all.

## Legacy
In February 1905 the reserves passed from the General Land Office to Gifford Pinchot's new Forest Service, and in 1907 Congress renamed every "forest reserve" a "national forest." All ten of the 1903 reserves survive inside today's national forests, though their names and boundaries have shifted many times:

- **Manti** → **Manti-La Sal National Forest.** The La Sal was folded into the Manti in 1949 and the combined forest renamed in 1950. It is the only hyphenated national forest that is a single proclaimed forest rather than two forests sharing an administration.
- **Logan** → **Uinta-Wasatch-Cache National Forest.** Logan first became part of the Bear River Forest Reserve in 1906; the Bear River was divided in 1908, and its eastern part became the Cache National Forest. When the Cache was split in 1973, the Utah portion holding the original Logan lands went to what is now the Uinta-Wasatch-Cache.
- **Lewis and Clarke** → **Helena–Lewis and Clark National Forest**, administratively combined in 2015.
- **Santa Barbara** → **Los Padres National Forest**, renamed in 1936.
- **Pocatello** → **Caribou-Targhee National Forest**, by way of the Cache (1915) and two later orders that moved its lands to the Caribou in 1939 and 1942.
- **Aquarius** → **Dixie National Forest.** Renamed the Powell in 1908, it was abolished by a 1945 order, effective October 1944, and all its lands joined to the Dixie, which still has a Powell Ranger District. The northern part is managed today by the neighboring Fishlake.
- **Payson** → **Uinta-Wasatch-Cache National Forest**, by way of the Nebo National Forest (1908) and the Uinta (1915).
- **Highwood Mountains** → **Helena–Lewis and Clark National Forest**, by way of the Jefferson National Forest of Montana (1908–1932). The Highwood Mountains are still listed among the ranges in the forest.
- **Yellowstone** was divided in 1908 among seven forests. Its living descendants are the **Shoshone, Bridger-Teton, Caribou-Targhee, Custer Gallatin, and Helena–Lewis and Clark** National Forests.

Most of the modern hyphenated names are administrative combinations; the forests underneath them remain separately proclaimed.

The Luquillo reserve has the most distinctive afterlife. It became the **Luquillo National Forest** under the 1907 renaming act, the **Caribbean National Forest** by executive order in 1935, and the **El Yunque National Forest** by Executive Order 13428 in 2007. That last change was made by executive order, not by Congress.

El Yunque remains the only tropical rainforest in the National Forest System, and both qualifiers matter: the Forest Service has held other tropical ground, including tropical dry forest on St. Croix, and the nation has other tropical federal lands in its national parks and wildlife refuges. The forest is also a refuge for species found nowhere else, among them the Puerto Rican parrot, listed as endangered since 1967. El Yunque still holds a small wild flock.

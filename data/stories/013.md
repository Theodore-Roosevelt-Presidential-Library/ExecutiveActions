## At a glance
On February 11, 1904, two days after Japanese torpedo boats struck the Russian fleet at Port Arthur, Roosevelt proclaimed the United States neutral in the Russo-Japanese War. The proclamation warned Americans against fighting for either side and kept both navies from using American harbors as bases. That carefully kept neutrality later let Roosevelt offer his good offices to end the war, and the peace signed at Portsmouth in 1905 earned him the Nobel Peace Prize.

## Background
The proclamation set no new policy, and the proof is specific. Its working rules were copied almost word for word from President Grant's proclamation of October 8, 1870, issued during the Franco-Prussian War. The twenty-four-hour clause is identical. The coaling clause differs in exactly one way: Grant had written "the nearest European port of her own country," and Roosevelt dropped "European," as he had to, since one of the warring powers was Japan. That single missing adjective was the whole of the 1904 change.

Otherwise, the proclamation invoked a law already on the books, the Neutrality Act of April 20, 1818. It spelled out, for every American and every foreign subject on American soil, what that law forbade. No one could accept a commission in either fighting force, enlist in it, fit out or arm a warship for it, or launch "any military expedition or enterprise" against Russia or Japan from United States territory.

The rules for the water carried the real force:

- **No lying in wait.** From February 11, a warship of either side that used American waters "for the purpose of preparing for hostile operations, or as posts of observations upon" the other side's ships would be regarded as "unfriendly and offensive."
- **The twenty-four-hour rule.** Beginning "from and after the fifteenth day of February instant," once any vessel of one belligerent left an American port, no warship of the other could follow it out for at least twenty-four hours. The departing vessel could be a warship, a privateer or "a merchant ship." That was the point of the rule: to keep raiders off merchant ships.
- **Short stays.** A warship entering a U.S. port had to "depart and put to sea within twenty-four hours," unless weather, provisioning or repairs required more time. When ships of both sides lay in the same harbor, their departures were to be arranged "as to afford the opportunity of leaving alternately."
- **Limited coal.** A warship could take on "so much coal only as may be sufficient to carry such vessel, if without any sail power, to the nearest port of her own country." A ship that could also sail got half that. She could not coal in American waters again for three months unless she had reached a port of her own country in the meantime or received special permission.

The proclamation also restated a principle the United States and Russia had agreed to in an 1854 convention: "That free ships make free goods." It summed up its own doctrine in one line: "whatever privileges shall be accorded to one belligerent within the ports of the United States, shall be, in like manner, accorded to the other."

## Historical context
The war opened with a Japanese surprise attack on the Russian squadron at Port Arthur on the night of February 8–9, 1904. Japan had broken off relations on February 6 and declared war on February 10, after the attack. On February 9 (January 27 in the Russian calendar) the Tsar issued a manifesto to his own subjects announcing the war. It became the first great-power war of the twentieth century, a contest over Manchuria and Korea between a European great power and a rising Asian one. More than two and a half million men were mobilized, and the single battle of Mukden involved more than half a million.

The proclamation was not the first American move. On February 10, Secretary of State John Hay sent a circular note to Russia, Japan, China and the other powers that had signed the Protocol of Peking. He asked that "the neutrality of China and in all practicable ways her administrative entity shall be respected by both parties, and that the area of hostility shall be localized and limited as much as possible." Protecting China came first. Closing American ports to the war came second.

Behind American neutrality law in this period stood the memory of the *Alabama* claims. During the Civil War, Confederate commerce raiders built in Britain had wrecked Union shipping. The *Alabama* was built by John Laird Sons at Birkenhead and sailed from Liverpool. In 1872 an international tribunal at Geneva ruled, four votes to one, that Britain had "failed, by omission, to fulfil the duties prescribed," and awarded the United States $15,500,000 in gold.

The order of events matters here. The prohibitions in the 1818 law were written decades before the *Alabama* existed, so they could not have been aimed at it. What the *Alabama* changed was the cost of failing to enforce such laws. Britain had a law of its own, and the tribunal refused to accept that the law was the problem. What a neutral owed was "due diligence," a principle carried forward in 1907 into Hague Convention XIII, at the conference Roosevelt had set in motion. Having a neutrality law was not enough. A neutral government had to enforce it.

Roosevelt's private sympathies were not neutral. He admired Japan's discipline and daring and was wary of Russian expansion in the Far East. Yet he did not want either side to win so completely that it could dominate northeast Asia. In June 1904 he wrote to Sir Cecil Spring Rice that if Russia won, "she will organize northern China against us and rule us absolutely out of all the ground she can control," and that if the Japanese "win out it may possibly mean a struggle between them and us in the future." A year later, on June 16, 1905, he described to Spring Rice the outcome he hoped for: "at the same time each power will be in a sense the guarantor of the other's good conduct." The proclamation kept all of this out of view. It committed the government to strict impartiality and left Roosevelt free to work toward the balanced peace he wanted.

## Political impact
At home a neutrality proclamation at the start of a foreign war was routine, and this one stirred little debate. American sympathy ran strongly toward Japan. Among American Jewish organizations and financiers it ran especially strong after the Kishinev pogrom of April 1903, and Jacob Schiff of Kuhn, Loeb underwrote a large share of Japan's war loans. But nobody was asking the United States to fight. The proclamation mattered most for what Roosevelt later did with the neutral standing it protected.

The war ground on. Port Arthur surrendered on January 2, 1905, after about five months under siege. Russia's armies were beaten at Mukden between February 20 and March 10, and on May 27–29 Russia's Baltic fleet was destroyed as a fighting force at Tsushima. Both sides were exhausted. Japan, short of money and men despite its victories, was ready for an outside hand.

On June 8, 1905, Roosevelt sent the same note to both capitals. It did not offer to mediate. It suggested that talks "be conducted directly and exclusively between the belligerents — in other words, that there may be a meeting of Russian and Japanese plenipotentiaries or delegates without any intermediary." The President was "entirely willing to do what he properly can" in "arranging the preliminaries as to the time and place of meeting." Japan accepted on June 10 and Russia, more cautiously, on June 12. Because the United States had stayed carefully outside the war, Roosevelt could offer his good offices on terms both sides would take. That summer four envoys came to the United States: Sergei Witte and Roman Rosen for Russia, and Komura Jutarō and Kogorō Takahira for Japan.

## Legacy
Roosevelt received both delegations aboard the presidential yacht *Mayflower* off Oyster Bay on August 5, 1905. The conference met in the newly finished General Stores Building, now Building 86, at the Portsmouth Navy Yard on Seavey Island in Kittery, Maine. The treaty takes its name from Portsmouth, New Hampshire, across the Piscataqua River, where the delegates stayed at the Hotel Wentworth in New Castle and crossed to the yard each morning. Sessions opened August 9, the two sides reached agreement on August 29, and the Treaty of Portsmouth was signed in Building 86 on September 5, 1905, ending the war.

Roosevelt's personal diplomacy kept the talks alive when they nearly collapsed over Russia's refusal to pay an indemnity. It earned him the 1906 Nobel Peace Prize, the first Nobel Prize of any kind awarded to an American and the first Peace Prize to a sitting head of state. The citation honored him "for his role in bringing to an end the bloody war recently waged between two of the world's great powers, Japan and Russia." The prize was presented on December 10, 1906, in Christiania, where the American minister, Herbert H. D. Peirce, accepted it for him. Roosevelt gave his Nobel lecture in person on May 5, 1910, a year after leaving office.

The award was controversial. Norway's political Left called Roosevelt a "military mad" imperialist, and the committee's own adviser, Halvdan Koht, filed a long and critical report. Roosevelt would not keep the money. He held the $36,734.79 in trust for an industrial-peace foundation, and when it had grown to $45,482.83, untouched, he asked Congress to release it for wartime relief charities.

The peace itself held up less well than the honor. The treaty contained no indemnity. On August 29 Japan had dropped its demand for one in exchange for the southern half of Sakhalin, when the Japanese public had expected the whole island. Anger spread across Japan before the treaty was even signed. On September 5, already September 6 in Tokyo, a crowd of some thirty thousand broke through police barricades at Hibiya Park, and three days of rioting followed. Seventeen people were killed. Martial law lasted until November 29. Japanese resentment of the American-brokered peace fed a longer cooling between the two Pacific powers.

The detailed ship-by-ship neutrality law behind the 1904 proclamation has since disappeared. Hague Convention XIII carried these rules forward in 1907, but two world wars and the collective-security system of the United Nations Charter eventually left no place for a twenty-four-hour rule. What lasted was the role the proclamation made possible: an American president serving as the indispensable go-between in other nations' wars. That was new in the country's history.

## At a glance
On May 6, 1907, Roosevelt signed two nearly identical proclamations creating Cinder Cone and Lassen Peak national monuments out of the Lassen Peak National Forest in northern California. Both were set aside for science, as illustrations "of special importance in tracing the history of the volcanic phenomena of that vicinity." They were the first monuments carved out of a national forest. Seven years later, the supposedly finished mountain erupted.

## Background
Both proclamations rested on section two of the Antiquities Act of 1906, which let the president protect "objects of historic or scientific interest" on federal land. Both protected the same kind of object: volcanoes.

- **Lassen Peak (Proclamation 754)** is a dacitic lava dome that the U.S. Geological Survey calls "one of the largest lava domes on Earth," rising more than 2,000 feet above its surroundings. The proclamation says it "marks the southern terminus of the long line of extinct volcanoes in the Cascade Range from which one of the greatest volcanic fields in the world extends."
- **Cinder Cone (Proclamation 753)**, about ten miles to the northeast, is a hill of ash and cinders. Its monument took in "the adjacent area embracing a lava field and Snag Lake and Lake Bidwell."

The surrounding forest had been created as the Lassen Peak Forest Reserve by Proclamation 564 of June 2, 1905. In March 1907, Congress renamed all forest reserves "national forests," so the 1907 proclamations correctly use the new term. Each proclamation reserved its land "from appropriation and use of all kinds under all of the public land laws." Each also carried a clause settling how the monument and the forest would share the ground:

> The reservation made by this proclamation is not intended to prevent the use of the lands for forest purposes under the proclamation establishing the Lassen Peak National Forest, but so far as the two reservations are consistent they are equally effective. In all respects in which they may be inconsistent the National Monument hereby established shall be the dominant reservation.

That "dominant reservation" clause is a genuine first. None of the earlier monument proclamations (Devils Tower, El Morro, Montezuma Castle, Petrified Forest and Chaco Canyon) has it, because none of those sites lay in a forest reserve. The Park Service's standard history of the Antiquities Act credits the device to the [Grand Canyon](story:3) proclamation of January 1908, but the Lassen proclamations used it word for word eight months earlier. Both monuments stayed with the Forest Service, which already managed the land around them.

Neither proclamation gives an acreage. Each boundary is defined only by "the diagram forming a part hereof," a Forest Service map bound into the *Statutes at Large*. The Interior Department's compilations of 1911 and 1912 give estimated areas of 5,120 acres for Cinder Cone and 1,280 acres for Lassen Peak. Lassen Peak's share was two square-mile sections of land.

The idea did not start with Roosevelt. It came up through the Forest Service. On February 22, 1907, Lassen Peak National Forest supervisor Louis A. Barrett wrote to Chief Forester Gifford Pinchot, urging that the points of interest near the peak be protected as national monuments, not as a national park. Monument status would let him choose exactly what to protect. It would sit better with local people because it would not block timber cutting or grazing. And forest officers could manage it at little extra cost.

On April 1, Secretary of Agriculture James Wilson forwarded Barrett's reports to the Interior Department. He wrote that they showed "the importance of the objects being protected from appropriation of all kinds as soon as possible," and he left the door open to a national park later: "in that event, the Monuments would simply be superseded by the Park." The General Land Office's commissioner, Richard A. Ballinger, agreed on April 9. On May 4, Interior Secretary James R. Garfield sent Roosevelt the draft proclamations, noting that "the creation of these National Monuments is urged by the people of California familiar with the objects sought to be preserved." Roosevelt signed two days later.

## Historical context
These two proclamations belong with Devils Tower, not with the Southwest's ancient ruins. Congress had passed the Antiquities Act mainly to stop the looting of prehistoric sites, and its backers had presented it as a small tool. Roosevelt read its "scientific interest" clause to include natural science as well as archaeology, geology included. The Lassen monuments are a clear example.

The justification was scientific and educational. Cinder Cone's proclamation called the area "of great scientific interest, as illustrations of volcanic activity which are of special importance in tracing the history of the volcanic phenomena of that vicinity." That wording came straight from Wilson's letter, which was summarizing J. S. Diller's 1895 *Lassen Peak Folio* for the Geological Survey. The government was not reserving these places as scenery for tourists or as wilderness for its own sake. It was keeping a natural laboratory intact for the study of how volcanoes work.

The proclamations' word "extinct" needs a close reading. It describes the "long line" of Cascade volcanoes. The text never actually says that Lassen Peak itself is extinct, though the implication is there. Most writers of the day did treat Lassen as finished. But Diller, who had mapped the district, had written the opposite twelve years earlier: "That volcanic activity is not yet extinct in the Lassen Peak district is shown by the presence of numerous solfataras and hot springs. At Bumpass's Hell, near the southern base of the peak, there are boiling mud pools and vigorous, solfataric action."

## Political impact
There was no fight, and the reason goes beyond the remote location. Barrett had chosen the monument route precisely because it would not touch timber or grazing, and Wilson had made the same case to the Interior Department. No rancher, logger or miner lost anything, and no lawsuit followed. On the ground, little changed in who was in charge: the General Land Office's annual report for 1908 stated the rule plainly, "The monuments within national forests are under the jurisdiction of the Forest Service, Department of Agriculture."

Congress could not have objected at the moment. It had adjourned on March 4, 1907 and did not meet again until December 2, so both proclamations were signed and reported entirely during the recess. Nor did a protest come when Congress returned. The printed indexes to the *Congressional Record* for both sessions of the 60th Congress, running from December 1907 to March 1909, have no entry for Lassen or Cinder Cone. The same index does list "Remarks by Mr. Smith of Arizona on Grand Canyon of Colorado," exactly the kind of western complaint a Lassen protest would have looked like.

The news traveled slowly, and not far. The first notice found came on May 29, when the *Sacramento Daily Union* reported that the proclamation "was received yesterday by State Forester Lull," twenty-three days after Roosevelt signed it. From Red Bluff and Quincy the story spread through the Sacramento Valley for about a month:

- The *Feather River Bulletin* of May 30 printed the Forest Service boundary map.
- The *Sacramento Daily Union* and the *Marysville Daily Appeal* both read the map and placed the Lassen Peak monument in sections 34 and 35 of its township.
- The *Weekly People's Cause* of June 8 printed the full text of the Cinder Cone proclamation on its front page.
- The *Red Bluff Daily News*, *Chico Record*, *Colusa Daily Sun*, *Chico Weekly Enterprise* and others ran items through early June.
- A syndicated paragraph noting that State Mineralogist Lewis E. Aubury had received the proclamation ran in at least five small papers, from the *Newcastle News* of June 19, 1907 to the *Cloverdale Reveille* of November 21, 1908, eighteen months after the signing.

One paper got it wrong. The *Stockton Independent* of June 14, reprinting the *Oakland Tribune*, told readers that "Cinder Cone seems to have been erroneously located in the same reserve" and that the proclamation "probably refers to the mountain of volcanic cinder located at the base of Mt. Shasta in Siskiyou county." Beyond the valley there was a single short paragraph in the *San Francisco Call* of June 9, under the headline "PRESIDENT ESTABLISHES NATIONAL MONUMENTS," and nothing east of the Sierra. Roughly a dozen and a half California papers carried the news. Northern California noticed; the rest of the country did not.

Two volcanic monuments in the Cascades were a small matter on their own. Combined with everything else Roosevelt signed in 1907, each was one more assertion that a president could set aside public land with his own signature. Together they added to a growing unease in the West, already loud over the forest reserves and the [coal-land withdrawals](story:27), that the public domain was being closed off from Washington without Congress in the room. That argument over executive power would boil over by the end of his term.

## Legacy
### The mountain wakes
On May 30, 1914, a steam explosion broke out at Lassen's summit, and over the next year some 180 more blasted out a crater nearly a thousand feet across. The eruptions peaked in May 1915 with two events. Late on the evening of May 19, an explosion shattered the lava dome that had risen in the crater and sent a half-mile-wide avalanche of snow and rock down the mountain's flank. The melting snow turned it into a mudflow that ran miles down Lost Creek. At about four in the afternoon on May 22, the peak blew again, throwing a column of ash and gas more than 30,000 feet high. Part of it collapsed into a flow of hot ash and gas that devastated three square miles. Fine ash fell as far away as Elko, Nevada, some 280 miles east. The Park Service calls the scoured ground the Devastated Area and credits it to both May 1915 eruptions.

According to the USGS, the May 22 explosion "was the most powerful in a 1914-17 series of eruptions that were the last to occur in the Cascades before the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens." The Park Service puts it another way: before 1980, "Lassen Peak was the only Cascade volcano to have erupted in the 20th century."

The best pictures came from Benjamin Franklin Loomis (1857–1935), a former shake cutter who ran a store, a hotel, a freighting business and a lumber mill at nearby Viola, and took photographs on the side. The USGS credits him with "the most complete and best documented photographic record" of the eruptions. His famous six-exposure sequence shows the eruption of June 14, 1914. The Park Service says his photographs "brought national attention to the area and supported the campaign to establish Lassen Volcanic National Park."

### From monuments to a national park
That park campaign actually came first. Representative John E. Raker of California introduced a bill for a "Peter Lassen National Park" on February 6, 1912, more than two years before the first eruption. Bills followed later in 1912, in April 1913, and on December 6, 1915. The earlier ones had died in committee twice. The Park Service's administrative history calls the timing of the eruptions "truly fortuitous," with the news and photographs "breathing new life into Raker's effort."

Not everyone in the government wanted a park. Agriculture Secretary David F. Houston filed a report against the final bill in April 1916, and Chief Forester Henry Graves had dismissed most park proposals as "simply local advertising and boosting schemes." The sticking point was grazing.

Congress established Lassen Volcanic National Park by the act of August 9, 1916 (ch. 302, 39 Stat. 442). The act reserved the land inside its boundary "as a public park or pleasuring ground." Its title places the park "in the Sierra Nevada Mountains," which is geologically wrong; Lassen is the southernmost volcano of the Cascades. The act never mentions either monument. It simply drew the park boundary around both, taking in the two sections where the Lassen Peak monument lay, and absorbed them. Wilson had predicted exactly this in 1907, when he wrote that a park would mean "the Monuments would simply be superseded by the Park."

### Cinder Cone's age
Even Cinder Cone's age has a twist. Current USGS work dates its eruptions to about 1650, using the rock's magnetism and a radiocarbon range of A.D. 1630–1670. A tree-ring year of 1666 is often cited, though the scientists who proposed it call the evidence ambiguous. J. S. Diller had reached nearly the same answer in 1891. Rejecting a popular date of 1850–51, he wrote that "the geologic evidence clearly demonstrates that it must have occurred long before the beginning of the present century," and placed the eruption about two hundred years back. In 1937 R. H. Finch brought back the 1851 date, and modern work has proved Diller right again. A correct nineteenth-century conclusion was overturned and then restored.

### A volcano still watched
Today the USGS California Volcano Observatory watches the Lassen Volcanic Center with seismic, ground-movement and gas monitors. The Geological Survey states that "it is almost certain that the Lassen area will experience volcanic eruptions again." Its 2018 national assessment rates Lassen one of eighteen U.S. volcanoes with a very high threat level. It ranks 11th of 161, between Mount Spurr and Augustine Volcano, and in California only Mount Shasta ranks higher. Its standing alert level is Normal, aviation color code Green.

A mountain reserved in 1907 as the end of a line of extinct volcanoes became, seven years later, the clearest proof in the Lower 48 that such mountains are not finished.

## At a glance
Roosevelt closed out 1903 with twenty-one executive orders. Most let one named person be hired or rehired outside the usual civil-service rules, and a few of those quietly staffed the new Bureau of Corporations. Three orders mattered more: a complete set of passport rules signed at Oyster Bay, a ban on consuls using official letterhead for private letters, and a naval reservation covering all of Kiska Island in the Aleutians.

## Background
Fifteen orders were single-purpose personnel actions: appointments without examination, reinstatements past the one-year limit, a transfer, and extensions of temporary jobs (EOs 223, 224, 225, 226, 227, 229, 231, 232, 233, 234, 236, 237, 238, 239, 242). The people ranged from Government Printing Office sewers and a Treasury money teller to the consulting engineer of Washington's new filtration plant (Allen Hazen, EO 231) and the new superintendent of the Government Hospital for the Insane (Dr. William A. White, EO 232). Most began as a request from a department head or a recommendation from the Civil Service Commission.

A few of these files show the human side of the paperwork:

- **EO 224.** Sarah McLanahan had worked twelve years as a sewer before leaving the Government Printing Office on her marriage in 1894. Widowed in June 1903 with three children to support, she was reinstated on the Commission's recommendation, her "previous efficient service attested by the Public Printer."
- **EO 234** was never a formal order at all. It was a letter from the President's secretary approving Emma J. Rawlings's reinstatement as a Treasury money teller, which the Commission "regarded … as an Executive Order."
- **The Bureau of Corporations.** Three orders (EO 223, which included E. Dana Durand as a special examiner, and EOs 236 and 239) named special examiners and special agents without examination for the trust-investigating bureau created that February in the new Department of Commerce and Labor.

The rest of the routine business: EO 222 let Civil War veterans compete for unskilled-labor jobs without the age test; EO 230 struck an exception from Schedule A; and EO 228, of August 1, extended the Spanish Treaty Claims Commission "for and during six months from and after the second day of September, nineteen hundred and three," that is, to March 2, 1904. The 1901 law creating the commission allowed extensions of no more than six months at a time.

**The passport rules.** EO 235, signed September 12 at Oyster Bay, where Roosevelt was spending the summer, gathered the whole passport procedure into sixteen numbered rules: who could issue a passport, who was entitled to one, and what an applicant had to prove. It was the only order of the group issued from Oyster Bay. It replaced the seventeen rules of July 1902 ([The Passport Orders of July 1902](story:79)) and carried out the same law, the act of June 14, 1902, which limited passports to "those owing allegiance, whether citizens or not, to the United States."

The 1903 rules were more than a restatement. Four things were new: the requirement in rule 4 that "a person of the Chinese race, alleging birth in the United States" supply affidavits from at least two witnesses with personal knowledge of the birth; the statement in rule 7 that "A married woman's citizenship follows that of her husband so far as her international status is concerned"; a note on applicants abroad in rule 1; and a note in rule 11 excluding governesses and tutors from "servant." The 1902 rule on visés was dropped. The rejection rule became a discretionary "right," and the old ground of refusal for anyone "unable or unwilling to comply with the rules" disappeared. The rules also carried forward the 1902 provision for residents of the island possessions who owed allegiance without being citizens.

**Consuls and Kiska.** EO 240, of December 5, barred consular officers from using paper "bearing any heading or device indicating their official position" in private letters, or signing themselves as consular officers; the State Department passed the rule to its consuls by circular two days later. EO 241, of December 9, reserved all lands on Kiska Island, Little Kiska Island, and adjacent islets for naval purposes. It was not the first naval reservation there: an order of June 13, 1902 had already reserved "certain lands on Adakh and Kyska Islands, Alaska … for naval purposes." EO 241's wording, "all lands," suggests it enlarged that reservation, but its text has not been found. Unlike Midway earlier in the year ([The Executive Orders of Early 1903](story:81)), which went wholly to the Navy in one stroke, Kiska was reserved piecemeal.

## Historical context
In 1903 a United States passport was not required by law for leaving or entering the country. It was a discretionary document, one the Secretary of State "has the right in his discretion to refuse." The rules were concerned less with security than with proving who was, and was not, entitled to the government's protection abroad.

Two provisions fix the rules firmly in their moment. The extra hurdle for Chinese-American applicants came out of the Chinese Exclusion regime, which Congress had continued "until otherwise provided by law" seventeen months earlier (Act of April 29, 1902). That regime treated Chinese-American claims of citizenship with systematic suspicion. The rule that a married woman's citizenship followed her husband's reflected the law of the day on married women's status.

The insular provisions carried forward the aftermath of 1898. Under the Treaty of Paris and the laws that followed, the people of Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Guam owed allegiance to the United States without being United States citizens.

Kiska belonged to the Navy's effort, after 1898, to secure coaling and station sites along the routes to Asia. Seven weeks after the order, the *Army and Navy Journal* reported that the Navy's General Board had recommended "a coaling station in the Aleutian Islands at Kiska," with a coal depot of "an initial capacity of 20,000 tons, later to be increased to 100,000 tons," because Kiska's "proximity to the great circle route gives it great superiority over Dutch Harbor." Secretary Moody, "while appreciating the importance" of the station, was "not prepared to approve" the board's proposed $1,500,000. In the spring of 1904 the gunboat *Petrel* was detached for "special service at Kiska," and Navy colliers and the Pacific Squadron were bound for the island that May and June.

## Political impact
None of these orders is known to have provoked controversy; most were internal administrative acts. How the press received them is an open question. A search of California papers found no coverage of the new passport rules, the Kiska reservation, or Dr. White's appointment, and the *Army and Navy Journal* of December 1903 carries no notice of the Kiska order. Eastern papers have not been searched, and some of these acts, such as Dr. White's appointment, the Bureau of Corporations hires, and the Chinese-race passport clause, may well have been reported there.

## Legacy
EO 235 sits in a long chain of passport rules. It was replaced in 1907 by a new set that added further requirements for Chinese applicants: duplicate filing, photographs, and a declaration of the port of reentry ([The Executive Orders of Summer 1907](story:93)). Its most striking provisions were later repudiated. Chinese Exclusion was repealed by the Magnuson Act of December 17, 1943. The doctrine that a wife's citizenship followed her husband's was broken by the Cable Act of 1922, though not cleanly: that act still stripped citizenship from any woman who married "an alien ineligible to citizenship," which in practice meant Asian men, until 1931.

The modern passport came later: the Passport Act of 1926, a wartime passport requirement from 1918, emergency requirements from 1941, and a standing peacetime requirement only in 1978, when Congress made it unlawful "for any citizen of the United States to depart from or enter, or attempt to depart from or enter, the United States unless he bears a valid passport," subject to exceptions the President may authorize.

Kiska gained a grim significance in the Second World War. Japanese forces landed there in June 1942, as they did on Attu. The garrison slipped away under cover of fog in late July 1943, and the Allied force that landed on August 15 found the island empty. Attu and Kiska, the western Aleutians Japan held in 1942–43, were the only ground in North America occupied by an enemy during the war.

Most of the other orders were spent when the appointment was made. The exception is Dr. William A. White, who led the Government Hospital for the Insane (St. Elizabeths) until 1937 and became a leading figure in American psychiatry.

## At a glance
On January 11, 1908, Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed the Grand Canyon a national monument, about 818,560 acres, slightly larger than Rhode Island. The Antiquities Act asked for "the smallest area compatible" with protecting an object; Roosevelt treated the entire canyon as the object. The proclamation held, the canyon became a national park in 1919, and the fight it set off ran for nearly two decades.

## Background
The proclamation itself names no acreage. It reserves the tracts "shown as the Grand Canyon National Monument on the diagram forming a part hereof." The 818,560-acre figure, exactly 1,279 square miles, is the National Park Service's.

The 1908 act capped a long, layered effort. President Benjamin Harrison had set the surrounding land aside as the Grand Cañon Forest Reserve by proclamation on February 20, 1893, under the Forest Reserve Act of 1891. Harrison knew Congress well on this subject: as a senator from Indiana he had introduced Grand Canyon park bills in 1882, 1883, and 1886, and all three died. The canyon kept getting protected by executive action because Congress would not act.

Roosevelt had added a layer of his own. By proclamation of November 28, 1906, under a separate act of Congress passed that June "For the protection of wild animals in the Grand Canyon Forest Reserve," he made the forest land north and west of the Colorado River, the Kaibab Plateau, a national game preserve.

A forest reserve, though, did not stop prospectors from staking mining claims or speculators from fencing off the best viewpoints, and bills to make the canyon a park kept stalling. The 1908 proclamation closed the gap. It reserved the land "from appropriation and use of all kinds under all of the public land laws, subject to all prior valid adverse claims." New mining claims and settlement were shut out; claims that were already valid were saved. And where the monument overlapped the forest, the proclamation declared, "the National Monument hereby established shall be the dominant reservation." In plain terms, protection now outranked extraction.

## Historical context
Roosevelt had stood on the South Rim on May 6, 1903, during a long Western tour. He arrived at half past nine in the morning, left at six that evening, and spoke from the balcony of a rim hotel to a crowd of about eight hundred: the territorial governor, men from his old Rough Rider regiment, local residents, schoolchildren whose diplomas he handed out, and Native people, to whom he addressed part of the speech. He had just praised the Santa Fe Railway for deciding not to build its hotel on the canyon's brink, and his best-known words that day followed directly from that. They are, at heart, an instruction not to build. In the authorized text of his addresses, he said:

> I hope that you will not have a building of any kind, not a summer cottage, a hotel, or anything else, to mar the wonderful grandeur, the sublimity, the great loneliness and beauty of the canyon. Leave it as it is. You can not improve on it. The ages have been at work on it, and man can only mar it. What you can do is to keep it for your children, your children's children, and for all who come after you, as one of the great sights which every American if he can travel at all should see.

A stenographer's version printed by the Flagstaff *Coconino Sun* on May 9, 1903, differs slightly: "Man cannot improve on it; not a bit," and "for your children and your children's children." Both texts end the same way: "one of the great sights," not "the one great sight."

That plea ran straight into reality on the rim. The Santa Fe had reached the canyon on September 17, 1901, with a sixty-four-mile branch from Williams, and tourism was booming. Entrepreneurs raced to control access. Chief among them was Ralph Cameron, a local prospector turned businessman, who had staked mining claims across the most valuable ground, including the head of the Bright Angel Trail, where he charged a one-dollar toll from 1903 until Coconino County took the trail over in 1912. He used the mining laws less to dig ore than to lock up real estate at the canyon's gateway.

Arizona was still a territory, its mining and commercial interests were wary of federal control, and park bills went nowhere. Blocked in Congress and unwilling to watch the rim be carved into private claims, Roosevelt reached for the one tool that needed no one's approval but his own: the Antiquities Act, passed less than two years before. He had first used it on [Devils Tower National Monument, Wyoming](story:2).

## Political impact
The proclamation was daring because of its size, and opponents seized on that. Ralph Cameron refused to yield, kept collecting tolls, and pressed his claims, and the fight became a years-long test of whether a president could really wall off so much land with a stroke of the pen.

It reached the Supreme Court. In *Cameron v. United States*, decided April 19, 1920, a unanimous Court speaking through Justice Van Devanter ruled against Cameron. His Cape Horn claim, the Court held, rested on no adequate discovery of minerals made before the reservation, and "a discovery made later can confer no rights upon the claimant." Cameron lost not because the proclamation erased his claim, but because his claim had never been valid. The Court also brushed aside, in a few sentences, his argument that there had been no authority to create the monument at all: the Grand Canyon, as the proclamation said, "is an object of unusual scientific interest." The Court did not take up the Act's "smallest area" limit.

Cameron was not finished. He won election to the U.S. Senate from Arizona in 1920 and spent his single term fighting the National Park Service and its director, Stephen Mather. He tried and failed to have Mather removed. In February 1922 he told the Senate that developing the canyon would serve "thirty or forty millionaires," and he held up the park's money for months against Arizona's own Carl Hayden and Henry Ashurst. The park's appropriation fell from $100,000 for fiscal 1922 to $75,000 for fiscal 1923. In 1926, after the General Land Office invalidated the last of his claims, he created a congressional committee to investigate the Interior and Agriculture departments, made himself chairman, and aimed it largely at Mather. That November he lost his seat in the general election to Carl Hayden, 44,591 to 31,845, after a six-part *Los Angeles Times* series alleged he had salted mining claims in the canyon.

The episode showed both the reach and the limits of Roosevelt's approach: the proclamation held, but the backlash ran for two decades.

## Legacy
Congress made the Grand Canyon a national park by an act President Woodrow Wilson signed on February 26, 1919, folding the monument's lands into the park. It was the outcome Roosevelt had wanted all along, reached after the monument had held the line for eleven years. The canyon became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979 and draws four to five million visitors a year; the National Park Service counted 4,919,163 recreation visits in 2024.

*Cameron v. United States* is still cited whenever a president's monument authority is challenged. The 1920 Court answered only whether the canyon qualified as an object of scientific interest; later courts have read the case as a foundation for broad presidential discretion under the Act. The Grand Canyon designation remains the classic example of the Antiquities Act used boldly, a line that runs to modern, contested designations such as the Baaj Nwaavjo I'tah Kukveni-Ancestral Footprints of the Grand Canyon National Monument, established on August 8, 2023, across 917,618 acres. Its name comes from two tribes whose ancestral lands it covers: Havasupai *baaj nwaavjo*, "where Indigenous peoples roam," and Hopi *i'tah kukveni*, "our ancestral footprints." That proclamation withdrew the land from new mining claims and mineral leasing, aimed principally at uranium, subject to valid existing rights.

More than a century on, Roosevelt's "Leave it as it is" has become shorthand for the American idea that some places are worth more kept whole than used up.

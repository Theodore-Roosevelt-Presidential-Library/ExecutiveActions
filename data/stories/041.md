## At a glance
On October 29, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt set aside Thursday, November 27 as a national day of thanksgiving. Where his first Thanksgiving proclamation had been written in the shadow of McKinley's death, this one was serene: "The year that has just closed has been one of peace and of overflowing plenty." Newspapers across the country ran it, often on page one and often in full.

## Background
This is Roosevelt's second Thanksgiving proclamation, naming the last Thursday of November as the day of thanks. It carries on the yearly ritual described in [Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20): each autumn the President named a day and asked the country to pause and give thanks. That chain was unbroken since Lincoln's proclamation of October 3, 1863. Before then, after Washington's proclamation of 1789, the observance had come and gone for decades. And Lincoln fixed no permanent date — Andrew Johnson named the first Thursday of December in 1865, and Grant named November 18 in 1869. What Roosevelt inherited was a habit, on a date set by custom alone.

The 1901 proclamation was written seven weeks after McKinley's death. The 1902 message is calm and confident, with the frank boast that "Rarely has any people enjoyed greater prosperity than we are now enjoying."

Its long middle passage deserves a close look. Before it counts blessings, it reckons with a century and a quarter of hard years, when "the country was menaced by malice domestic or foreign levy, when the hand of the Lord was heavy upon it in drouth or flood or pestilence, when in bodily distress and anguish of soul it paid the penalty of folly and a froward heart." The present plenty arrives as something struggled toward, not simply received: "Nevertheless, decade by decade, we have struggled onward and upward." And it closes with a test of gratitude: "we seek to praise Him not by words only but by deeds, by the way in which we do our duty to ourselves and to our fellow men."

A few words may trip a modern reader. "Drouth" was a standard spelling of *drought*, and "froward" means stubbornly perverse. Two phrases are borrowed without credit: "malice domestic or foreign levy" adapts *Macbeth* ("Malice domestic, foreign levy, nothing / Can touch him further"), and "a froward heart" is the language of the King James Psalms ("A froward heart shall depart from me," Psalm 101:4). Some newspaper reprints of the day were not faithful — *Leslie's Weekly* set "drought" and added a comma after "malice" — so the *Statutes at Large* text is the one to quote.

## Historical context
The tone reflects a good year for the administration, though not a tidy one. On July 4, 1902, Roosevelt had proclaimed the Philippine insurrection at an end, but that proclamation excepted "the country inhabited by the Moro tribes," where American troops were still fighting ([Pardon and Amnesty in the Philippines, July 4, 1902](story:34)).

The anthracite coal strike, which had shut the eastern hard-coal mines since May, had ended only six days before the proclamation was signed. Roosevelt named a commission to investigate on October 15–16. The miners' convention at Wilkes-Barre voted unanimously on October 21 to go back to work and accept the commission's judgment, and work resumed on Thursday, October 23. The commission's award would not come until March 1903. So when Roosevelt wrote of peace and plenty, the strike was over but its settlement was still pending ([The Anthracite Coal Strike and the Coal Strike Commission, 1902](story:11)).

The economy was strong, and the proclamation was signed six days before the November 4 midterm elections. Its language — thanks to "the Giver of Good," praise offered "not by words only but by deeds" — was the civic-religious rhetoric the office was expected to supply, pitched to a nation that knew the yearly observance well.

## Political impact
The proclamation was a wire story the day it was signed, and it ran widely, often on page one and often in full. The *Stockton Mail* had it on page one on October 29 itself. On October 30 it ran in the *San Bernardino Sun*, the *Morning Echo*, Riverside's *Enterprise*, the *Los Angeles Herald*, the *Petaluma Daily Morning Courier* and the *Santa Barbara Weekly Press*, and on page one of South Dakota's *Mitchell Daily Republican*. Nebraska's *Loup City Northwestern* put it on page one on November 7.

Headline writers seized on the prosperity line: "People Should Return Heartfelt Thanks For Unbounded Prosperity" (San Luis Obispo *Morning Tribune*, October 31) and "YEAR OF PEACE AND PLENTY" (*Santa Barbara Weekly Press*). The phrase "overflowing plenty" turns up in 21 items in California newspapers between October and December 1902. Two of them, in the *Ventura Daily Post* and the *Sonoma Valley Expositor*, are advertisements, with the President's words reprinted in the ad columns.

The one editorial found read the document the other way. The *San Francisco Call*'s "Now for Thanksgiving" (November 7, 1902, p. 6), written just after the election, admitted that in the heat of the campaign the President's proclamation had drawn little attention. What struck the editors was its piety: Roosevelt, they noted, said comparatively little about the country's material prosperity, the subject earlier presidents had leaned on at Thanksgiving. The *Call* quoted the "froward heart" passage and the "peace and overflowing plenty" sentence, and used the coming holiday to urge members of the rival parties to mend old friendships.

On Thanksgiving Day itself, *Leslie's Weekly* (November 27, 1902) gave its cover to a photograph captioned "PRESIDENT ROOSEVELT SIGNING HIS THANKSGIVING PROCLAMATION," by R. L. Dunn, with the full text beneath.

No paper found set the prosperity line against the recent coal crisis; a search of California papers for late October and November 1902 turned up only sermon reports and features. The coverage found is mostly Californian, with scattered papers elsewhere, and eastern editorial opinion has not been sampled. The proclamation carried no policy, and no objection to it has been found.

## Legacy
Like every annual Thanksgiving proclamation, this one has no independent legacy; it matters as one link in the continuous chain that runs back to Lincoln's of 1863 ([Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20)). The date went on resting on custom, usually the last Thursday of November, until Franklin Roosevelt moved the holiday a week earlier in 1939, 1940 and 1941. A joint resolution of Congress approved December 26, 1941, then fixed Thanksgiving on the fourth Thursday of November.

The Dunn photograph survives in the Library of Congress. The Theodore Roosevelt Center's catalog record for that print ("President Roosevelt signing his 1902 Thanksgiving Proclamation act," record o306954) dates it December 3, 1902. That cannot be the date of the event, since the picture was on the cover of *Leslie's Weekly* on November 27 and the proclamation is dated October 29; it is most likely a copyright-deposit date. Whether the picture shows the actual signing or a later pose is not established. The safest caption is the one *Leslie's* used, without a date.

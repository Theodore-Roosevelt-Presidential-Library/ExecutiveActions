## At a glance
Between October 31 and December 27, 1901, Roosevelt signed the thirteen orders that open his presidency in the numbered executive-order series, EOs 141 through 153. They are not his first orders; five unnumbered ones came earlier. Nearly all deal with the civil service. Some tighten the merit system, some begin bringing the rural free-delivery mail service under it, and a few make one-time exceptions so a particular job could be filled without an examination.

## Background
None of these orders carried a number when it was signed. The numbers come from a State Department series applied after the fact, beginning in 1907. Most of the thirteen amend the Civil Service Rules of May 6, 1896, as revised by McKinley in 1899, which governed the "classified" federal workforce under the Pendleton Act of 1883. In the same eight weeks Roosevelt also signed at least eight unnumbered orders, mostly Navy pay rules and land set aside for the Navy and the Lighthouse Board.

The orders fall into a few groups:

- **Who was covered (144, 145, 147).** EO 144 put civilian field workers of the Army's Quartermaster, Medical, Ordnance and Engineer Departments (packers, foremen, forage masters, lock and dam tenders and dozens of other trades) back under the rules, reversing part of McKinley's 1899 order. EO 145 let an Indian agent whose post was abolished, when a school superintendent took over the agency, become a classified employee "upon such test of fitness as may be satisfactory to the Secretary of the Interior and the Civil Service Commission," with no competitive exam. EO 147 brought "the rural free-delivery service" into the Post-Office Service, though the carriers themselves were not to be classified until rules for appointing them took effect.
- **Transfers and short registers (146, 150, 151).** EO 146 changed one word: the apportionment rules "shall" be waived on an appointing officer's certificate became "may" be waived, and only with reasons given "in detail" and the Commission's approval. EO 150 barred transfers before six months of actual service. EO 151 let officers appoint from a register with only one or two eligible names instead of the usual three.
- **Rural carriers' ages (152, 153).** EO 152 gave rural letter carriers their own age range, 17 to 55, against 18 to 45 for other post-office jobs. EO 153 waived the limits entirely for honorably discharged Civil War and Spanish-American War veterans.
- **Enforcement (148, 149).** EO 148 gave the Commission teeth: if someone was holding a job in violation of the rules, it could certify the fact to the department head and, if the person was still on the rolls thirty days later, to the officers who paid the salary, who then had to stop paying. EO 149 made it "the duty of every officer and employee in the public service" to give the Commission information and sworn testimony.
- **One-time exceptions (141, 142, 143).** Each "Special Rule" let a single vacancy be filled without an examination: a check-signing clerk at the Philadelphia pension agency, the steward of the White House, and a laborer serving as coachman to the Assistant Secretary of the Navy. For the first, the Commissioner of Pensions had asked on October 21 that the President make "a special exception in this instance," after the Philadelphia pension agent asked to choose his own clerk.

Most of the orders took effect when signed. EO 147 deliberately waited on the carriers' appointment rules. None needed an act of Congress.

## Historical context
Roosevelt is the only president who had served as a U.S. Civil Service Commissioner. He held the job from 1889 to 1895, appointed by Benjamin Harrison and kept on by Grover Cleveland, and spent those years in open fights with postmasters and with Harrison's Postmaster General, John Wanamaker, over dodges around the Pendleton Act. In 1892 the papers reported him calling a post-office inspectors' report against him "slanderous falsehoods."

Eight days before the December 11 enforcement orders, Roosevelt laid out his view in his first annual message to Congress (December 3, 1901): "The merit system of making appointments is in its essence as democratic and American as the common school system itself. It simply means that in clerical and other positions where the duties are entirely non-political, all applicants should have a fair field and no favor, each standing on his merits as he is able to show them by practical test." The same message looked ahead to orders like EO 144: "In other cases, as where laborers are employed, a system of registration undoubtedly can be widely extended." Several of these orders follow that program, tightening enforcement (148, 149), closing transfer loopholes (146, 150), and restoring the Army field trades to the rules (144).

The same weeks show the other side of the job. A president has a government to staff. The special rules let him fill three particular posts without waiting on an exam, and EO 145 let sitting Indian agents, who were political appointees, slip into the classified service on a non-competitive test. Extend the merit system, but keep the practical power to make particular appointments: both impulses ran side by side through Roosevelt's civil-service record, and both show up in this first cluster.

The rural carriers did come under the merit system on February 1, 1902. The Commission counted 342 rural free-delivery clerks, inspectors, special agents and messengers classified on November 27, 1901, and 6,009 carriers on February 1, 1902. By June 30, 1902, the service had 8,590 classified carriers.

## Political impact
At least one of these orders made the national wire. An Associated Press item from Washington on November 27, 1901, reported that "the President to-day signed an order bringing the rural free delivery service into the classified civil service," effective at once for "the 250 clerks, special agents and route inspectors" but not for the roughly 6,000 carriers until appointment rules were made (*San Francisco Call*, November 28, 1901). The item reported; it did not argue. A search of California papers for the rest of December found no notice of the December 11 enforcement orders; the civil-service news of those weeks was the Maclay dismissal and the circulars against "pernicious activity." The orders were no secret, though: the Commission printed its rule changes in the annual reports it sent to Congress.

Read together, the orders signaled that the merit system would be neither dismantled nor treated as absolute.

## Legacy
On their own these thirteen orders left little mark. They were routine adjustments, and all were overtaken when EO 209 replaced the 1896 rules with a consolidated set effective April 15, 1903. Their interest is as a starting point: the first entries in the numbered series of a president under whom the merit system more than doubled in size. The Commission counted 106,205 classified positions on June 30, 1901, 206,637 on June 30, 1908, and 234,940 on June 30, 1909, just under four months after he left office. That growth reflects the expansion of the government itself as well as Roosevelt's orders.

The pull between reform in the rules and pragmatism in the exceptions runs through Roosevelt's whole civil-service presidency. It set up a sharper fight a few weeks later, over the "gag order" of January 1902 ([The Civil-Service Orders of Early 1902 and the "Gag Order"](story:78)).

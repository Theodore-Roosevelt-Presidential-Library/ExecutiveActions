## At a glance
In the fall of 1908 Roosevelt used the Antiquities Act twice more, and the two monuments show the law's two sides. Tumacácori, in southern Arizona, protected something people made: the ruined church of a Spanish mission on ten acres a homesteader gave up for the purpose. Wheeler, in southern Colorado, protected something weather made: pale volcanic pinnacles "of unusual scientific interest as illustrating erratic erosion." One grew into a national historical park. The other was abolished in 1950.

## Background
Both monuments were made under section two of the Antiquities Act of June 8, 1906, and both were Forest Service monuments.

**Tumacácori (Proclamation 821, September 15, 1908).** The proclamation set aside the adobe-and-brick church of the old mission of San José de Tumacácori, in the Santa Cruz River valley south of Tucson, with the ten acres around it. The ground was not simply carved out of the public domain. It was handed over. The homesteader Carmen Mendez, a middle-aged Mexican immigrant farming in the valley, gave up the ten acres of his 160-acre claim on which the ruin stood, and the Interior Department accepted them. Mendez received a patent for his remaining 150 acres in December 1908. Because the site lay within two miles of national forest land, it was handed to the Forest Service to administer under a cooperative agreement between Interior and Agriculture.

The proclamation gets the building wrong twice. It calls the mission "erected probably in the latter part of the sixteenth century," which is off by about two hundred years. It calls it "largely of burned brick and cement mortar instead of adobe," when the walls are chiefly unfired adobe and fired brick was used sparingly. A proclamation is a legal act, not a work of scholarship, and in 1908 the government's grasp of these sites was thin.

**Wheeler (Proclamation 831, December 7, 1908).** This one protected a cluster of pale volcanic-ash pinnacles and hoodoos high in the La Garita Mountains of southern Colorado. It was Colorado's first national monument. Because the formations lay inside the Rio Grande and Cochetopa National Forests, the proclamation did something legally unusual and said so in its text. Monument and forests would both remain "effective on the land withdrawn," with the monument the "dominant reservation." One federal reservation was stacked on top of another, and the newer one given priority.

Neither proclamation states an acreage. Tumacácori's is fixed by its survey description, two adjoining five-acre pieces of section 30, and by the General Land Office plat bound with it ("Containing 10 acres"). Wheeler's boundary is drawn on a Forest Service diagram titled "Wheeler National Monument within Cochetopa and Rio Grande National Forests, Colorado, 1908," which gives no figure. The number of record, "three hundred acres," comes from the 1950 act that abolished the monument, and the diagram is consistent with it.

## Historical context
**A mission saved from ruin.** Tumacácori is the Antiquities Act used exactly as its authors intended, to save a decaying historic structure from pot-hunters, souvenir-takers and slow collapse. The mission belonged to the Pimería Alta frontier, which the Jesuit Eusebio Francisco Kino entered in 1687. Kino reached the O'odham settlement of Tumacácori in January 1691. That first mission stood on the east bank of the Santa Cruz and moved to the west bank after the Pima uprising of 1751. The church Roosevelt reserved came more than a century after Kino. Fray Narciso Gutiérrez began it sometime between 1799 and 1802, and Fray Ramón Liberós put it into use in December 1822, still surrounded by scaffolding and never finished.

No single person asked for the monument. Interest ran through the valley: neighbors in Tubac and Tumacácori such as William Lowe, Ramón Burruel, John F. Black and Carmen Mendez himself, and, from farther off, the Tucson Pioneer Historical Society, the territorial geologist W. P. Blake and the ethnologist Jesse Walter Fewkes. The Historical Society pressed the Forest Service to act. Forest Inspector Coert DuBois visited in the spring of 1907, found the mission "rapidly falling into ruins" through the work of "the vandalist [sic] and visitor," and recommended withdrawing forty acres. In June 1907 the General Land Office ruled that the Baca Float No. 3 land claim "constitutes no bar." That fall Forest Inspector Will C. Barnes inventoried the church and found the cemetery still in use, with a funeral under way the day he came. By February 1908 the Nogales forest supervisor had cut the proposal to about fifteen acres, since forty took in too much good farmland. On February 28 Secretary of Agriculture James Wilson wrote to the Secretary of the Interior, and Mendez's relinquishment closed the gap.

**A "wonderland" in the high country.** Wheeler belongs to the other Antiquities Act tradition, the one that stretched "objects of scientific interest" to cover striking scenery, as at Devils Tower (1906) and Pinnacles (1908). It was a Forest Service project from start to finish, and the best account comes from the man who pushed for it. Writing in *Colorado Magazine* in 1924, former Forest Supervisor Frank C. Spencer recalled: "It was during the summer of 1907 that the writer, while acting as United States Forest Supervisor, learned of the existence of some wonderfully carved rocks called the 'Sand Stones.'" A trip that year fell through because of "pressure of other duties." The next year, at the Wagon Wheel Gap hotel, he heard the rocks praised by "the proprietor of the hotel, Mr. Elwood Bergey," who "had seen them from a distance," and in 1908 Spencer's party reached them. "Not only was there an endless variety of curiously carved forms," he wrote, "but all were in nature's most gorgeous hues." He called the place "this wonderland."

Spencer wrote "a report … accompanied by photographs, strongly recommending the withdrawal of the land from private entry," and took the matter up with Gifford Pinchot in Washington. The name first suggested was "Freemont National Monument." The monument was named instead for Captain George M. Wheeler, who had led the Army's survey west of the 100th meridian in the 1870s. The Forest Service then drew the diagram printed with the proclamation.

## Political impact
Neither monument met organized opposition. By late 1908 a national-monument proclamation was a familiar, small event, and neither one cost anyone access to a working economy.

**Tumacácori.** The administrative history records "no significant opposition." In Tucson the story was told as good news for business, under the headline "Historic Tumacácori Mission Will Attract Many Tourists" (*Tucson Daily Citizen*, October 26, 1908). No land was taken from a claimant against his will: Mendez gave up his ten acres freely, and on December 14, 1908 he received a patent for the other 150. The Forest Service took up the site's care under a cooperative agreement between Interior and Agriculture. How other Arizona papers received the monument has not been checked.

Tumacácori, though, sat on top of a contested question. The mission lay within the area claimed under Baca Float No. 3, a disputed Mexican-era land grant. The proclamation came only months after the government had declared that claim no bar and treated the ground as public domain open to homesteading. That footing would not hold.

**Wheeler.** Colorado's papers greeted Wheeler warmly, and no criticism has been found. The Colorado Historic Newspapers Collection holds 35 items naming the "Wheeler national monument" between December 1, 1908 and January 31, 1909, nearly all one Washington notice reprinted across the state. Local word lagged the signing. On December 19, twelve days after it, the *Monte Vista Journal* reported it was "expected the president will soon issue a proclamation declaring Wheeler Monument a government park," a spot with "the finest scenery of any spot in the State—much grander than the Garden of the Gods at Colorado Springs." The same column found "F. C. Spencer" explaining "the status of the Forest service" on reservoirs to irrigation stockholders at Center.

The Washington notice then ran in the *Boulder Daily Camera* on December 21 as "New Colorado Park" and on the front page of the Creede *Candle* on December 26 under "Presidential Proclamation Creates Wheeler National Monument." It said the "fantastic forms resulting from the rapid erosion of rock and soil make the spot one of exceptional beauty," that it "will be much visited by tourists when it has been made accessible by road or trail," and that "at present it can only be reached on foot," with Wagon Wheel Gap the nearest railroad point. It also passed along the belief that John C. Frémont's "ill-fated expedition" had met disaster nearby, with "skeletons of mules, bits of harness and camp equipage" found in the area. A shorter version ran as "National Monument in Colorado" (*Lamar Register*, December 23), and an illustrated feature followed in January 1909 (*Elbert County Tribune*, January 14).

## Legacy
**Tumacácori endured and grew.** In *Lane v. Watts* (234 U.S. 525, decided June 22, 1914), the Supreme Court held that title to the Float had passed to the Baca heirs in 1864, and affirmed an order barring the Interior Department and the General Land Office from going ahead with entries on it. That struck down Mendez's homestead and the title his relinquishment rested on. The mission became the private property of the Bouldin family, claimants to the northern part of the Float, and the monument lived on as an oddity, "a privately owned, federally protected site," until the Bouldins deeded the ten acres back to the United States on December 8, 1917.

The Forest Service handed the monument to the new National Park Service in 1918, and Frank "Boss" Pinkley of Casa Grande began repairing the church. Small additions came in 1958 and 1978. In 1990 Congress abolished the monument and created Tumacácori National Historical Park, adding the ruins of Los Santos Ángeles de Guevavi ("the first mission in Arizona," in the act's words, about 8 acres) and the Kino visita and rancheria of Calabazas (about 22 acres). Another 310 acres, including the mission orchard and land along the Santa Cruz, followed in 2002. The park now tells the fuller story of the Spanish and O'odham frontier, including land-claim battles that, as the park's historians note, "neither of which served the interests of the original grantees."

**Wheeler went the other way.** The Forest Service ran it until the 1933 reorganization moved it to the Park Service. Congress never paid for a road or for development, and the Park Service never really put it into operation. It stayed a long horseback ride from Creede or Wagon Wheel Gap, drawing about a hundred visitors a year in the 1920s and only 43 recorded visits in 1943. The Act of August 3, 1950 abolished "the Wheeler National Monument, containing three hundred acres," and placed the land under "the national forest within which such properties are situated," the Rio Grande. The same day, by a separate act, Congress also abolished Colorado's Holy Cross National Monument (1,392 acres, proclaimed by Herbert Hoover in 1929), so Colorado lost two monuments on one day.

The Wheeler pinnacles are protected today as the Wheeler Geologic Area of the Rio Grande National Forest. The Forest Service enlarged the protected area in 1962, and the Colorado Wilderness Act of 1993 brought it into the La Garita Wilderness, while exempting a rough four-wheel-drive road of about fourteen miles.

Wheeler joins a short list of Roosevelt monuments later dissolved, alongside [Lewis and Clark Cavern National Monument, Montana](story:30), handed to Montana as a state park in 1937, a reminder that a proclamation could be undone as well as made.

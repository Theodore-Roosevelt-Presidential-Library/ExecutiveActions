## At a glance
Between May and August 1907 Theodore Roosevelt signed three proclamations that moved land once reserved to tribes into settlers' hands. Two set up lotteries: one for irrigated farm units in Montana's new Huntley Project, one for the western end of the Lower Brule Reservation in South Dakota. The third cut the price of leftover land at Devils Lake, North Dakota, from $4.50 an acre to $2.50.

## Background
All three put federal land into private hands, but they did three different jobs.

**Huntley Project, Montana (Proclamation 755, May 21).** This one opened irrigated farm units in a federal reclamation project on the south bank of the Yellowstone, about ten miles northeast of Billings. The land came from the Crow cession, an agreement of 1899 that Congress ratified "as herein amended" in the Act of April 27, 1904. That act made the ceded land "subject to withdrawal and disposition under the reclamation Act … so far as feasible irrigation projects may be found therein," and added that the reclamation charges "shall be in addition to the charge of four dollars per acre for the land," which went to the Crow. Because these tracts had been set aside for irrigation, they were held back when the rest of the Crow cession opened in 1906 (see [The Indian Reservation Openings of 1906](story:65)). Applicants filed sealed affidavits at Billings by June 25, 1907; a drawing on June 26 set the order of choice; and the units opened July 22. Each winner had to "file a water right application" along with the entry. The Reclamation Service planned 28,921 acres as 585 small farms, on the idea that forty irrigated acres could support a family.

**Lower Brule, South Dakota (Proclamation 771, August 12).** The Act of April 21, 1906 directed the sale of "the western portion of the Lower Brule Indian Reservation … comprising approximately fifty-six thousand five hundred and sixty acres": the west half of Townships 106–110 North in Range 77, the fractional townships in Range 78, and fractional Township 110 in Range 79. This was a cession of a defined block. Indians already allotted inside it could keep their allotments or trade them for new ones "on the diminished reservation." Sections 16 and 36 went to South Dakota for schools at $1.25 an acre. Settlers paid the appraised price, one-fifth down and the rest over five years, and the net proceeds went into the Treasury "to the credit of the Indians belonging and having tribal rights on the Lower Brule Reservation." The deal began with an agreement Inspector James McLaughlin made with the Lower Brule on February 21, 1906, amending one from 1901. In the House, Representative Charles H. Burke of South Dakota assured members the bill followed the February agreement and told them the committee's report was unanimous. The record shows three acreages: 54,240 in the agreement, 55,283.77 in the appraisal (priced from $2.50 down to $1.25 an acre, worth $114,818.60 in all), and the act's 56,560, the figure the newspapers used. Registration ran at Pierre from October 7 to 12, 1907, with the drawing on October 14 and entries from October 20 to December 20.

**Devils Lake, North Dakota (Proclamation 765, June 8).** This one opened nothing new and held no drawing. The Devils Lake opening of 1904 (see [The Sioux Land Openings of 1904](story:51)) had priced the ceded land at $4.50 an acre. Three years later the tracts still unsold were, in the proclamation's words, "small in acreage, or hilly and stony and cannot be disposed of at the price named." The 1904 act let the President lower the price once no more would sell, at whatever he judged "best for all interests concerned." Roosevelt dropped the floor to $2.50 an acre in cash, effective the day he signed, and allowed isolated tracts to be sold under section 2455 of the Revised Statutes. The money still went to the Sisseton, Wahpeton, and Cut-Head bands, for whom the United States acted as trustee in the sale.

## Historical context
These openings belong to the allotment era, when federal policy aimed to break tribal land into individual holdings and move the rest to settlers. Yet none of the three rests on the Dawes Act of 1887. Each rests on its own act of Congress, and in each one the United States acts as a seller for the tribe, not a buyer. The Lower Brule act says the United States "shall act as trustee for said Indians to dispose of said lands." The Crow act likewise swapped the 1899 agreement's fixed $1,150,000 for proceeds from sales at no less than $4 an acre. To the government of the day this looked like progress: tribes would earn income from land they did not farm, and settlers would make it productive. The cost fell on the tribes. They lost land that treaties had reserved to them and were paid only as settlers bought, at prices the government set and, as Devils Lake shows, could lower.

Huntley added the newer logic of the [Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21). The government built the canals; homesteaders entered the watered land and repaid the construction cost over time. Huntley settlers owed two charges: a $3 yearly construction installment for ten years, plus the $4 an acre owed to the Crow, with operating costs on top. On the northern plains in 1907, ceded reservation land, opened by lottery, met federal irrigation.

## Political impact
### Huntley: a rush, then a stall
Huntley was a genuine rush. The proclamation itself spoke of "the great demand for these lands because of their enhanced value by reason of the construction of irrigation works." The land office at Billings, according to Reclamation's later history, "received 5,491 applications from prospective homesteaders before the closing date of June 25th." The drawing on June 26 was national wire news. A Billings dispatch called it the latest of the government's famous land lotteries, held with Secretary of the Interior Garfield looking on, and described landseekers arriving from all over the country for some 700 farms of forty and eighty acres. A Kansas City *Star* piece reprinted in California cautioned readers that this was no free handout of farms: a unit of forty-seven and a half acres would cost a settler more than $1,550, paid in installments.

Accounts of the drawing itself differ. The wire stories said 1,500 names were drawn; the Reclamation Service's own annual report says that "in the presence of the Secretary of the Interior, a drawing was made from the registered applicants and 1,000 numbers were drawn," with fifty filings allowed each day from July 22 and open entry after August 23.

Then the winners stayed home. Two days into the filing period, a Billings dispatch counted only about twenty entries out of a possible 400 and predicted that fewer than one winner in five would follow through. Reclamation's history explains why: "many of the registrants were from Billings and applied only to see what number they would draw," "lottery fanatics" with "no intention of settling on the project." "Of the first 1,000 names drawn only 76 completed filing." By the close of the fiscal year in mid-1908, the Reclamation Service reported, "238 units had been entered at the close of the fiscal year, covering 10,675.60 acres," out of 585 units on 28,921.44 acres. In March 1908 Congress gave the July 1907 entrymen until May 15, 1908 to establish residence.

### Lower Brule: a quieter drawing
The Lower Brule drawing at Pierre was wire news too, but calmer. A Pierre dispatch in October reported only a handful of people on hand when registration opened, since everyone knew the order of registering had nothing to do with the order of the drawing; about 500 signed up that first day. A week later the running total stood at 3,460 with a day to go, and local notaries were angry at outside notaries who worked the trains, taking affidavits as soon as the cars crossed into the land district. The General Land Office later judged that the registration-and-drawing plan "proved entirely satisfactory to the people" and that "Practically all of the lands were entered by persons who registered for the opening." Coverage of both drawings was practical, about crowds, odds, and costs, with no protest recorded.

### Devils Lake: a price cut on paper
The Devils Lake price cut drew official, not public, attention. A General Land Office circular of June 28, 1907, signed by Acting Commissioner Fred Dennett, sent the proclamation to the register and receiver at Devils Lake and told them the unreserved and undisposed lands were open "at a price of not less than $2.50 per acre, cash, in addition to the usual fees." Who proposed the cut, and how settlers and the local press took it, is not known.

### The tribes
The people who bore the loss, the Lower Brule, the Crow, and the Sisseton, Wahpeton, and Cut-Head bands at Devils Lake, had little voice in the newspapers of 1907. In administrative terms all three proclamations carried out choices Congress had already made. Devils Lake was the exception that shows the President's own discretion: the 1904 act left the new price to be set "as he may deem best for all interests concerned." For Huntley, Reclamation's history credits Billings boosters with pressing for the project.

## Legacy
### Huntley Project
The openings did not settle the land as fast as the proclamations assumed. Reclamation's history says only 4,100 acres were irrigated in the 1908 season. The project went on through drainage trouble and the farm collapse after 1918. In 1921 the irrigators organized the Huntley Irrigation District and took over operation of the works. The Bureau of Reclamation says the project today "can furnish water to irrigate approximately 30,000 acres" in the Yellowstone Valley east of Billings.

### Lower Brule
Part of the 1906 cession was still unsold six years later. On September 24, 1913, Woodrow Wilson's Proclamation 1252 offered those lands for cash at public auction at Pierre, at no less than a dollar an acre, reciting that "a portion of said lands remained undisposed of." No court ruling on the present legal status of the 1906 cession has been found. A 1983 federal appeals decision describing the reservation's history said only that Congress had opened its western and southern portions in 1899 and 1906 and that the eastern boundary on the Missouri "was not altered."

### Devils Lake
The Interior Department later read the price cut narrowly. In *Clara F. Moran*, decided December 29, 1910, it refused a refund to a woman who had paid $4.50 an acre for land that had been in a cancelled Indian allotment on June 8, 1907, holding that "only such lands as were unreserved and undisposed of on that date were affected thereby." How many acres actually sold at the lower price is not known.

The larger question was the reservation itself. In *United States v. Grey Bear* (1987), the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals concluded that the 1904 act's "cede, surrender, grant, and convey" language, "standing alone, does not evince a clear congressional intent to disestablish the Devils Lake Reservation." The court noted that the act made no "unconditional commitment by Congress to pay the tribe for the ceded lands," and that a 1983 act of Congress referred to the "continued existence of the Devils Lake Reservation." (The decision was later vacated in part, on other grounds, on rehearing.)

### Reduced reservations
The longer shadow is the pattern of reduced reservations and mixed ownership these openings left behind. That pattern has fed a century of litigation over what land remains "Indian country," from *Rosebud Sioux Tribe v. Kneip* (1977) to *McGirt v. Oklahoma* (2020). Neither case concerned these three statutes, but both the Lower Brule act of 1906 and the Crow act of 1904 speak of what remained as "the diminished reservation."

## At a glance
Between May and August 1907 Theodore Roosevelt signed three proclamations that moved land once reserved to tribes into settlers' hands. Two set up lotteries: one for irrigated farm units in Montana's new Huntley Project, one for the western end of the Lower Brule Reservation in South Dakota. The third cut the price of leftover land at Devils Lake, North Dakota, from $4.50 an acre to $2.50.

## Background
All three put federal land into private hands, but they did three different jobs.

**Huntley Project, Montana (Proclamation 755, May 21).** This one opened irrigated farm units in a federal reclamation project on the south bank of the Yellowstone, about ten miles northeast of Billings. The land came from the Crow cession, an agreement of 1899 that Congress ratified "as herein amended" in the Act of April 27, 1904. That act made the ceded land "subject to withdrawal and disposition under the reclamation Act … so far as feasible irrigation projects may be found therein," and added that the reclamation charges "shall be in addition to the charge of four dollars per acre for the land," which went to the Crow. Because these tracts had been set aside for irrigation, they were held back when the rest of the Crow cession opened in 1906 (see [The Indian Reservation Openings of 1906](story:65)). Applicants filed sealed affidavits at Billings by June 25, 1907; a drawing on June 26 set the order of choice; and the units opened July 22. Each winner had to "file a water right application" along with the entry. The Reclamation Service planned 28,921 acres as 585 small farms, on the idea that forty irrigated acres could support a family.

**Lower Brule, South Dakota (Proclamation 771, August 12).** The Act of April 21, 1906 directed the sale of "the western portion of the Lower Brule Indian Reservation … comprising approximately fifty-six thousand five hundred and sixty acres": the west half of Townships 106–110 North in Range 77, the fractional townships in Range 78, and fractional Township 110 in Range 79. This was a cession of a defined block. Indians already allotted inside it could keep their allotments or trade them for new ones "on the diminished reservation." Sections 16 and 36 went to South Dakota for schools at $1.25 an acre. Settlers paid the appraised price, one-fifth down and the rest over five years, and the net proceeds went into the Treasury "to the credit of the Indians belonging and having tribal rights on the Lower Brule Reservation." The deal began with an agreement Inspector James McLaughlin made with the Lower Brule on February 21, 1906, amending one from 1901. In the House, Representative Charles H. Burke of South Dakota assured members the bill followed the February agreement and said, "It is a unanimous report." The record shows three acreages: 54,240 in the agreement, 55,283.77 in the appraisal (priced from $2.50 down to $1.25 an acre, worth $114,818.60 in all), and the act's 56,560, the figure the newspapers used. Registration ran at Pierre from October 7 to 12, 1907, with the drawing on October 14 and entries from October 20 to December 20.

**Devils Lake, North Dakota (Proclamation 765, June 8).** This one opened nothing new and held no drawing. The Devils Lake opening of 1904 (see [The Sioux Land Openings of 1904](story:51)) had priced the ceded land at $4.50 an acre. Three years later the tracts still unsold were, in the proclamation's words, "small in acreage, or hilly and stony and cannot be disposed of at the price named." The 1904 act let the President lower the price once no more would sell, at whatever he judged "best for all interests concerned." Roosevelt dropped the floor to $2.50 an acre in cash, effective the day he signed, and allowed isolated tracts to be sold under section 2455 of the Revised Statutes. The money still went to the Sisseton, Wahpeton, and Cut-Head bands, for whom the United States acted as trustee in the sale.

## Historical context
These openings belong to the allotment era, when federal policy aimed to break tribal land into individual holdings and move the rest to settlers. Yet none of the three rests on the Dawes Act of 1887. Each rests on its own act of Congress, and in each one the United States acts as a seller for the tribe, not a buyer. The Lower Brule act says the United States "shall act as trustee for said Indians to dispose of said lands." The Crow act likewise swapped the 1899 agreement's fixed $1,150,000 for proceeds from sales at no less than $4 an acre. To the government of the day this looked like progress: tribes would earn income from land they did not farm, and settlers would make it productive. The cost fell on the tribes. They lost land that treaties had reserved to them and were paid only as settlers bought, at prices the government set and, as Devils Lake shows, could lower.

Huntley added the newer logic of the [Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21). The government built the canals; homesteaders entered the watered land and repaid the construction cost over time. Huntley settlers owed two charges: a $3 yearly construction installment for ten years, plus the $4 an acre owed to the Crow, with operating costs on top. On the northern plains in 1907, ceded reservation land, opened by lottery, met federal irrigation.

## Political impact
Huntley was a genuine rush. The proclamation itself spoke of "the great demand for these lands because of their enhanced value by reason of the construction of irrigation works." A Billings dispatch of June 26 reported that "in the presence of Secretary Garfield today the government began another of its famous land lotteries," and that "from all parts of the country eager landseekers have come to file their affidavits." The land office had received 5,491 applications. A Kansas City *Star* piece warned readers this was "not, as many persons may suppose, a free distribution of farms," putting a forty-seven-and-a-half-acre unit at "something more than $1,550 in installments."

Then the winners stayed home. By July 24 a Billings dispatch counted "a total of twenty out of a possible 400" entries. Many registrants, Reclamation's later history explains, were Billings residents who "applied only to see what number they would draw" and had "no intention of settling on the project"; of the first 1,000 names drawn, "only 76 completed filing." By mid-1908, 238 of the 585 units, covering 10,675.60 acres, had been entered.

The Lower Brule drawing at Pierre was quieter. When registration opened, "only a handful were present," since it was "generally known that the order of the registration would have nothing to do with the order of the drawing." Some 3,460 had registered with a day to go, and local notaries were "in an angry buzz" at outsiders "working the trains" for affidavits. The General Land Office judged the plan "proved entirely satisfactory to the people" and reported that "Practically all of the lands were entered by persons who registered for the opening." Coverage of both drawings was practical, about crowds, odds, and costs, with no protest recorded.

The Devils Lake price cut drew official, not public, attention. The General Land Office sent the proclamation to the local land office on June 28, 1907, announcing the land open "at a price of not less than $2.50 per acre, cash, in addition to the usual fees." Who proposed the cut, and how settlers received it, is not known.

The people who bore the loss, the Lower Brule, the Crow, and the Sisseton, Wahpeton, and Cut-Head bands at Devils Lake, had little voice in the newspapers of 1907. In administrative terms all three proclamations carried out choices Congress had already made. Devils Lake was the exception that shows the President's own discretion: the price was his to set.

## Legacy
The openings did not settle the land as fast as the proclamations assumed. At Huntley, Congress gave the July 1907 entrymen until May 15, 1908 to establish residence, and only 4,100 acres were irrigated that season. The project went on through drainage trouble and the farm collapse after 1918; the irrigators formed the Huntley Irrigation District in 1921 and took over the works, which can still water about 30,000 acres of the Yellowstone Valley east of Billings. At Lower Brule, part of the 1906 cession was still unsold in 1913, when Woodrow Wilson offered it at public auction at Pierre for no less than a dollar an acre. At Devils Lake, the Interior Department later read the price cut narrowly, ruling in 1910 that it reached "only such lands as were unreserved and undisposed of on that date."

The longer shadow is the pattern of reduced reservations and mixed ownership these openings left behind, which has fed a century of litigation over what remains "Indian country." For Devils Lake, a federal appeals court ruled in *United States v. Grey Bear* (1987) that the 1904 act's "cede, surrender, grant, and convey" language, "standing alone, does not evince a clear congressional intent to disestablish the Devils Lake Reservation." No court ruling on the present status of the 1906 Lower Brule cession has been found.

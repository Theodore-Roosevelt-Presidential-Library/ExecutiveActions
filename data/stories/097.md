## At a glance
Most of the executive orders of late winter and spring 1908 were routine: appointments, Alaska telegraph sites, small changes to Canal Zone law. Three stand out. A February order started work on fortifications at Corregidor, the island guarding Manila Bay. An April order made the Dry Tortugas, west of Key West, a refuge for native birds. And at the end of April, Roosevelt gave federal workers around San Francisco Bay a day off to watch the battleship fleet steam through the Golden Gate.

## Background
**Corregidor.** Executive Order 763½, signed February 25, carries the catalog heading "Authorizing Construction of Fortifications on the Island of Corregidor, Manilla [*sic*] Bay, P.I. Upon Securing Any Lands Needed." By the catalog's summary, construction was "ordered started immediately upon obtaining the written consent of the owners of the land involved." It was an order to begin at once, conditioned on the owners' consent, on an island where the Army had been building since 1904.

**Tortugas Keys.** Executive Order 779 (April 6) reads in full:

> It is hereby ordered that all islands embraced within the group known as the Dry Tortugas, located in the Gulf of Mexico, near the western extremity of the Florida Keys, approximately in latitude twenty-four degrees, thirty-eight minutes north, longitude eighty-two degrees, fifty-two minutes west from Greenwich, and situated within the area segregated by a broken line upon the diagram hereto attached and made a part of this order, are hereby reserved and set aside for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding-ground for native birds; but the reservation made by this order is not intended to interfere with the use of these islands for necessary military purposes under the Executive Order of September 17, 1845, creating the Dry Tortugas Military Reservation, nor to, in any manner, vacate such order, except that such military use shall not extend to the occupation of the islet known as Bird Key. This reservation to be known as Tortugas Keys Reservation.
>
> Theodore Roosevelt. The White House, *April 6, 1908.*

It used the same one-paragraph device as the other bird reservations, but with a twist: the ground already belonged to the government for another purpose. The Dry Tortugas, a cluster of low coral keys about seventy miles west of Key West, had been a military reservation since President Polk's order of September 17, 1845. They were the site of Fort Jefferson, the great unfinished brick fort that had served as a Civil War prison. Roosevelt laid a bird reservation over the military one without disturbing it, except for one carve-out. The military could not occupy Bird Key, the great sooty-tern rookery the order was written to protect. The order names only the Department of Agriculture. In practice the Bureau of Biological Survey administered the reservation, sharing the cost of warden protection with the National Association of Audubon Societies.

**The rest of the run:**

- **Alaska telegraph.** The Army Signal Corps kept reserving ground for its line across the territory: 640 acres at Hogan (765), lands near Chena and Fairbanks that also revoked an earlier order, EO 328-B (769), and land at Sitka moved from the Navy to the War Department for a cable house and station (778). Executive Order 782 set aside nineteen acres on the left bank of the Chena River at Fairbanks for a *wireless* telegraph station.
- **Navy coaling stations.** Executive Order 771 created a coaling-station reservation on part of Hawkins Island in Prince William Sound. Executive Order 773 redrew the naval coaling depot at Lowell Point on Resurrection Bay, about 3,350 acres, revoking the February 21 order that had first set it aside.
- **The Canal Zone.** Executive Order 774 was a two-word fix: it replaced "collector of revenue" with "tax collector" in the Zone's jury-trial order of February. Executive Order 788 made it a misdemeanor to wilfully injure, deface, or remove a survey or boundary monument.
- **Appointments.** A long list of clerks, inspectors, and draftsmen were appointed or reinstated without examination, in the usual way.

## Historical context
**Defending Manila Bay.** The Corregidor order belonged to the long project of defending the territories won in 1898. On January 31, 1905, Roosevelt had appointed a National Coast Defense Board, headed by Secretary of War William Howard Taft. Its report, delivered February 1, 1906, ranked the harbors that should be fortified to modern standards and placed the entrance to Manila Bay among the priorities for the island territories. Whoever held rocky Corregidor, at the bay's mouth, held the bay. The island had been declared a military reservation in 1902 and battery construction had been underway since 1904. In 1908 the post got its name: Fort Mills, for Brigadier General Samuel M. Mills, Jr., Chief of Artillery from 1905 until he retired in 1906. Mills had died on September 8, 1907, at Galilee, New Jersey, soon after being stricken with apoplexy. The first of the seacoast batteries, Battery Wheeler, would proof-fire its guns in 1909.

**Wiring Alaska.** The Alaska reservations belonged to a different frontier: the effort, begun at the turn of the century, to tie the vast territory together with the Washington–Alaska Military Cable and Telegraph System, which the Signal Corps was still extending station by station.

**The terns of Bird Key.** The Tortugas reservation answered the crisis that had launched the whole refuge program: market gunners and eggers were stripping the coastal bird colonies. The sooty terns of Bird Key were the reason for the order, and their numbers tell a recovery story rather than a tale of untouched abundance. When federal warden protection began in 1903, the colony stood at roughly 5,000 adults, the remnant of decades of commercial egging. Once guarded, it bounced back fast: an estimated 18,858 adults in 1907 and about 40,000 by 1909. Tortugas Keys was one link in a Florida chain that ran from Pelican Island (March 14, 1903) through Passage Key (EO 357-B, October 10, 1905), Indian Key in Tampa Bay (EO 409, February 10, 1906; not the Indian Key of the upper Keys), and Mosquito Inlet just six weeks earlier ([The Executive Orders of Early 1908](story:96)).

## Political impact
None of these orders stirred national controversy. The Corregidor work fit a defense program that Congress had already funded and the public broadly supported. The Alaska and Canal Zone orders were routine.

The one order that touched the public did so in a spirit of celebration. Executive Order 789 (April 30) excused federal employees at San Francisco, Oakland, Berkeley, and Alameda on May 6 so they could join the crowds greeting the Atlantic battleship fleet, the "Great White Fleet," as it steamed through the Golden Gate. The Navy's itinerary puts the fleet's arrival at San Francisco on May 6, 1908, at the end of a 14,556-mile first leg from Hampton Roads. The ships stayed in the Bay two months and left on July 7. Representatives Knowland and Kahn had pressed the President to declare May 6 "a general holiday in all federal offices in San Francisco," and the *San Francisco Call* reported that he "expressed surprise that the matter had not already been settled." A few days later the paper reported the holiday extended to Oakland, Alameda, and Berkeley, and the San Francisco post office closed for the day. It was a small piece of a very large public spectacle, and a form Roosevelt used more than once: three weeks later, Executive Order 799 excused Civil and Spanish War veterans for a reinterment parade.

## Legacy
**Corregidor** became one of the most storied places in American military history. Its later nickname, the "Gibraltar of the East," belongs to the Second World War (and was applied at least as often to Singapore). Bataan surrendered on April 9, 1942, giving the Japanese artillery positions a short distance across the water. Corregidor held out under that bombardment until General Wainwright asked for terms late on May 6. A combined parachute and amphibious assault retook the island beginning February 16, 1945. Organized resistance ended February 26, and MacArthur returned for the flag-raising on March 2. Fort Mills and its batteries survive today as war memorials in the Philippines.

**The Tortugas Keys Reservation** was ended by name. Proclamation 2112 of January 4, 1935, issued by Franklin Roosevelt under the same section of the Antiquities Act Theodore Roosevelt had used, created Fort Jefferson National Monument. It expressly revoked "Executive Order No. 779, of April 6, 1908," along with the 1845 military reservation as it applied to the Dry Tortugas group. The monument was enlarged in 1983 and became Dry Tortugas National Park by act of Congress on October 26, 1992.

Bird Key itself is gone. The tern colony reached something like 80,000 to 100,000 breeding adults around 1917, but hurricanes in 1910 and 1919 stripped the island's vegetation. Without the bay cedar holding it together, the key wore steadily away. By 1934 the last warden found "very little of the island above water level," and by mid-1935 it was a sandbar. It was not, as a popular story has it, destroyed by the 1935 Labor Day hurricane; neither 1935 storm passed near the Dry Tortugas. The birds moved to nearby Bush Key, where up to 80,000 sooty terns and 4,500 brown noddies still nest, the only significant colony of either in the United States. The name survives only on the water, in Bird Key Harbor, a federally designated anchorage southwest of Garden Key.

**The Alaska telegraph reservations** reverted as wireless and, later, the telephone made the old military cable obsolete.

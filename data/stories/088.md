## At a glance
Between March 1 and June 16, 1906, the season of the great San Francisco earthquake, Roosevelt signed thirty-nine executive orders. Most were routine. But one sent thirteen collections of historical manuscripts, from a 1631 Spanish document to the Jefferson Davis papers, to the Library of Congress. Another moved the federal land office out of burned San Francisco to Oakland. Others closed Florida's surveying office, planted a tree nursery in Utah's mountains, and let the Civil Service Commission take applications from non-citizens when no citizens were available.

## Background
On May 23, 1906, Roosevelt ordered a set of "Historical Archives in the Department of State" moved to the Library of Congress. Executive Order 449 lists thirteen of them, including:

- "Spanish manuscripts of 1631, supposed to have been captured in the City of Mexico."
- "The Forton Prisoners manuscripts, 1777−1779"
- "The Whisky Insurrection in Pennsylvania, 1792−1796."
- "Correspondence of Albert Gallatin as Secretary of the Treasury."
- "Papers relating to the Burr Conspiracy."
- "Jefferson Davis Papers"

They were to go "to the possession and custody of the Library of Congress, to be there preserved and rendered accessible for historical and other legitimate uses." The order rested on the appropriations act of February 25, 1903, and continued the transfer Roosevelt had begun with the Revolutionary records that year ([The Executive Orders of Early 1903](story:81)).

**San Francisco's land office.** For years the federal land office had filled an entire floor of the Sub-Treasury building on Commercial Street. The fire that followed the earthquake destroyed its records: "thousands of plats, maps and records that reached back ... to the earliest Spanish grants," as the *San Francisco Call* put it. The Interior Department suspended "all business relative to the United States Land Office at San Francisco ... until further notice." Executive Order 443, dated April 28, 1906, sent the office temporarily to Oakland, where it was set up in the post office building. The General Land Office's report for 1906 noted the San Francisco office's "records completely destroyed by fire April 19, 1906," and the new Oakland office "Established by Executive order dated April 28, 1906." The order's own wording has not survived in any printing. (The phrase "the building in which it was housed having been destroyed," often given as its title, was written by catalogers in 1944.)

**Other notable orders in the run:**

- **Florida's surveyor-general (EO 442, April 27; EO 459, June 14).** The first order abolished the office of Surveyor General for Florida. The second moved the effective date "to make the effective date of such abolition June 30, 1907, instead of June 30, 1906."
- **A tree nursery (EO 455, June 6)** set aside land inside the Salt Lake Forest Reserve, Utah, for a nursery site.
- **Non-citizen applicants (EO 458, June 13)** let the Civil Service Commission accept applications from non-citizens where American citizens were lacking, chiefly for interpreter jobs. Citizens kept first claim: non-citizens found eligible "shall not be certified for appointment so long as persons are eligible who are American citizens."
- **The Bent Land District (EO 446, May 16)** revoked an order of the previous November that had abolished the district in Colorado.
- **A target range (EO 439, April 12)** reserved 307.54 acres in Mississippi for the Revenue Cutter Service.
- **Alaska** saw changes to the Fort Egbert (EO 426) and Fort St. Michael (EO 456) military reservations, and land reserved for an experimental farm station (EO 429).
- **Consular rules (EOs 422 and 423, March 1)** replaced paragraphs on export invoices and their verification.
- **Panama (EO 452, May 31)** allowed clerks earning under $75 a month on the Isthmus to be hired without examination.

The rest of the run is the usual list of appointments and reinstatements outside the competitive rules.

## Historical context
The archives order reflects a Progressive-era belief that the country's documentary heritage should be gathered, preserved and opened to researchers, not scattered across department vaults.

The San Francisco order shows how far the earthquake reached, even into the dry machinery of the federal land system. With the land office's plats and records burned, its business had to cross the bay to keep running.

Abolishing Florida's surveyor-general marked the slow closing of the land-survey frontier in the older states, where the original survey work was essentially done. The Bent District order, two weeks later, showed that such consolidations could run backward when local pressure pushed hard enough.

The Salt Lake nursery belonged to the practical side of conservation. The Forest Service, barely a year old, needed seedlings before it could replant anything. And the non-citizen order answered the staffing needs of an expanding federal service overseas. An empire needed interpreters faster than the civil-service lists could supply citizens who spoke the languages.

## Political impact
In April and May of 1906 the national press was consumed by San Francisco's ruin and recovery, and little of this paperwork made the papers. A search of regional newspapers in California, Colorado and Utah shows that three of these orders did register locally, and one stirred real complaint.

**The land-office move.** San Franciscans did not take it as mere housekeeping. Days after the order, the Santa Cruz *Surf* (April 30, 1906) carried the Interior Department's notice suspending "all business relative to the United States Land Office at San Francisco ... until further notice." By July the city's lawyers and land seekers were writing to its commercial bodies to ask why the office had gone to Oakland and whether it would stay. The *San Francisco Call* (July 18, 1906) called the removal "cause of annoyance to many people," since everyone from the counties who came to the city on land business now had "to take an additional trip to Oakland." The *San Jose Mercury-News* (July 19, 1906) reported that the commercial organizations planned "a protest to the Interior Department at Washington" and meant "to ask the President to order the return of the office to this city." The office's register told the paper that "the removal was made as the result of an executive order of President Roosevelt."

**The Bent District.** In Colorado, the revocation was a local victory. The *Lamar Register* (May 23, 1906) ran a Washington special under the head "Land Office Remains," reporting that the President had revoked the order of November 18, 1905, because entries at Lamar were running about 1,000 a year. It gave "most of the credit for saving the district" to "Congressman Brooks," Franklin E. Brooks of Colorado. The Springfield *Herald* (May 25) carried the Associated Press version.

**The Salt Lake nursery.** Utah papers covered it plainly. The *Salt Lake Herald* announced the planting station in May ("Uncle Sam to Plant Trees," May 6, 1906). The *Salt Lake Tribune* (July 17, 1906, "Nursery Site Fixed in Forest Reserve") placed it in section 17, township 2 south, range 3 west, on the north side of Big Cottonwood Canyon about four miles from Brighton, where trees would be grown "and afterwards transplanted upon the forest reserves."

**The archives.** The transfer was carried out within weeks. The Librarian of Congress's report for the year ending June 30, 1906, reprinted the order in full under "MSS. transferred from the State Department" and added: "The transfer has been made." No public reaction to it, for or against, has been found.

The rest, from the consular amendments to the Alaska reservations, passed without remark.

## Legacy
The archives transfer is the piece that still shows. The collections named in the order entered the Library of Congress's Manuscript Division, part of a long gathering-in of federal historical records that took institutional form with the National Archives in 1934. The 1903 law the order relied on was later codified and then repealed in 1951. Where each of the thirteen collections sits today has not been confirmed.

The San Francisco land office came home once the city rebuilt. Executive Order 1317 of March 14, 1911, signed by William Howard Taft, moved it from Oakland back to San Francisco, leaving the 1906 order as a small documentary trace of the disaster.

The Mississippi target range outlived the Revenue Cutter Service that asked for it. Public Land Order 1636 of May 14, 1958, revoked the reservation and made the ground part of the Horn Island National Wildlife Refuge. A gunnery range became bird habitat.

The non-citizen rule sits in the long, shifting history of citizenship requirements for federal jobs. It lasted forty-one years, until Executive Order 9830 swept it away in 1947. Florida's surveyor-general stayed abolished, one of many such offices wound down as the public-land survey was completed. The appointments, consular amendments and Alaska reservations close back into the ordinary record.

## At a glance
On November 16, 1907, Theodore Roosevelt reserved a quarter-section of canyon country in the Territory of New Mexico to protect the "Gila Hot Springs Cliff-Houses," stone-and-timber homes built into natural caves some 180 to 200 feet up a canyon wall. Of all his national monuments, this is the one the Antiquities Act was actually written for: a specific set of ancient ruins, shielded with only "as much land as may be necessary for the proper protection thereof." The monument was small, already looted, and for decades barely guarded — but it came to sit at the edge of the world's first designated wilderness.

## Background
Proclamation 781 set apart **160 acres** — the northeast quarter of section 27, township 12 south, range 14 west, as the diagram bound with the proclamation shows. The proclamation itself gives no acreage; the boundary is the map. Its reasoning fit in a single sentence: the cliff-houses were "of exceptional scientific and educational interest, being the best representative of the Cliff-Dwellers' remains of that region."

The proclamation places them "in the Mogollon Mountains," the period's loose way of putting it. More exactly, the dwellings stand in the canyon of the West Fork of the Gila River, at about 6,000 feet, in a valley half a mile across at its widest, with the Mogollon Range to the west, the Black Range to the east and the Pinos Altos Range to the south. The caves are natural, carved by running water and seeping groundwater out of the Gila Conglomerate — volcanic debris, gravel and sand cemented into rock. Seven caves sit close together, and five of them hold dwellings. The builders quarried that same conglomerate for their walls.

The land lay inside the Gila National Forest. The forest had begun as the Gila River Forest Reserve in 1899, was renamed Gila in 1905, and became a "national forest" when Congress renamed every reserve on March 4, 1907. The monument stayed under the Forest Service, and, as at Roosevelt's two volcanic monuments of that spring ([The Volcanic Monuments: Lassen Peak and Cinder Cone](story:23)), the proclamation made it "the dominant reservation" wherever it conflicted with ordinary forest use.

Roosevelt signed it the same day he proclaimed Oklahoma the forty-sixth state ([Oklahoma Statehood — Proclamation 780](story:17)). Statehood for a new state and a quarter-section of canyon on the same morning: conservation moved through a working presidency alongside much larger business.

## Historical context
The Antiquities Act of June 8, 1906 grew out of archaeologists' alarm at "pot-hunters" stripping ancestral ruins across the Southwest and selling the pottery and relics on the open market. Roosevelt would soon stretch the law to cover natural wonders like Devils Tower and the Grand Canyon. The Gila cliff-dwellings were the plain case it was built for.

The statute itself was largely the work of the archaeologist Edgar Lee Hewett. Ronald F. Lee's standard Park Service history says the law as enacted is Hewett's December 1905 draft with "not a single significant word" altered, and credits that draft with the phrase "objects of historic or scientific interest," the "smallest area compatible" formula in place of an acreage cap, and the term "national monument" itself. Hewett was not working alone, though. He was reconciling six years of competing bills, and his draft was endorsed by the Archaeological Institute of America and the American Anthropological Association before it went to Congress. Representative John F. Lacey of Iowa, who chaired the House Committee on the Public Lands, introduced Hewett's draft in the House in January 1906 and steered it there; the bill Roosevelt actually signed was Senator Thomas M. Patterson's S. 4698.

The dwellings were far older than the language of 1907 suggests. Tree-ring dates on the original roof timbers fall in the late 1270s and 1280s. The builders lived in the caves for one or two generations and had left by around 1300. Why they left, and where they went, is not known.

"Cliff-Dwellers" was the period's label. Archaeologists now attribute the site to the Mogollon, whom the Park Service also calls the Southern Ancestral Pueblo. The cliff dwellings belong to the Tularosa phase. Adolph Bandelier, who described the ruins in 1884, concluded that their builders "were in no manner different from the Pueblo Indians in general culture." The Antiquities Act protected the "American antiquities" of a seemingly vanished past while saying nothing of the descendants of the people who made them. Those descendants are very much present: the Park Service has found a shared group identity between the cliff-dwellers and the Hopi Tribe, the Pueblo of Acoma, the Pueblo of Laguna and the Zuni Tribe. The Chiricahua Apache consider the surrounding wilderness their homeland — a tie to the land itself, not descent from the builders.

## Political impact
**Where the idea came from.** As with the Lassen monuments six months earlier, the Gila proposal came up through the Forest Service rather than down from the White House. In December 1906, Gila Forest Supervisor R. C. McClure reported to the Chief Forester in Washington that the only structure he knew of worth preserving by the national government was the cliff dwellings "four miles above the Gila Hot Springs." In the administrative historian Peter Russell's summary, McClure noted that the site was very well preserved, although many artifacts had been "carried off since its discovery in the 1870s by hunters and prospectors"; that the area held no minerals, and so faced no commercial threat; and that a "half-mile stretch of fence--with a good gate--" should close off the mouth of Cliff Dweller Canyon, with protecting the ruins made the forest ranger's special duty. McClure also reported that the forest abounded with ruins and "hieroglyphics." The Forest Service agreed. In April 1907 the acting chief forester asked McClure for "an accurate description of the precise tract which should be withdrawn as a National Monument," and Roosevelt signed eleven months after McClure's first report. A supervisor asked for a fence across a canyon mouth and got a quarter-section, which is the plainest explanation for the monument's small size.

**How it was received.** No notice in the national press has been found. A search of every Chronicling America page using the words "national monument" between November 16 and the end of December 1907 turned up nothing about the Gila. But the proclamation did not go unnoticed at home. Two territorial papers carried it within two weeks, both approvingly. The *Albuquerque Morning Journal* of November 29, 1907 ran it as a headlined news story, "GOVERNMENT PROTECTS GILA CLIFF DWELLINGS," from a correspondent in Silver City: "The Cliff Dwellings on the upper Gila are to be protected by the government. The president has issued a proclamation in the interest of archaeology, reserving this section as a national monument." On December 6 the *Deming Graphic* added a front-page editorial paragraph headed "A National Monument": "President Roosevelt has wisely, and in the interest of archaeological science, issued a proclamation reserving the Cliff dwellings of the Upper Gila country as a national monument…" Whether the Silver City papers, closest of all to the site, said anything is not known, because their 1907 issues have not been digitized.

Neither paper used the proclamation's official name, "Gila Hot Springs Cliff-Houses," and that name turns up nowhere in the digitized newspapers of 1907–08. Locally, the place was already the Gila Cliff Dwellings.

No lawsuit or formal objection has been found. Whatever argument the Antiquities Act stirred up in these years was over its bold uses — the scale of the Grand Canyon, the meaning of "smallest area compatible." A quarter-section of cliff-houses that fit the law's original purpose exactly gave nobody much to fight about.

## Legacy
**An already-looted site.** The proclamation did not rescue an unknown ruin. The first recorded visit came in 1878, by Henry B. Ailman of Silver City and friends, and the Park Service's administrative history states that "within six years of the first recorded visit to the ruin in 1878, the site was thoroughly rifled." Bandelier reached the ruins on foot in January 1884, stayed about four days, recorded the thickness of the walls and the size of the roof beams, sketched a plan, and published in 1892. Walter Hough's *Antiquities of the Upper Gila and Salt River Valleys* came out in 1907, the same year as the proclamation. It rested partly on a 1905 expedition to the San Francisco and Blue rivers and drew on Bandelier for the cliff dwellings; Hough himself never visited them. What the proclamation did accomplish, as the administrative history credits it, was to take the site and its 160 acres out of reach of private ownership for good, and to prohibit damaging or removing its artifacts.

**Protection on paper, not on the ground.** With no funding, guarding the monument was an incidental job for a forest ranger seasonally based seventeen miles of river trail away. Administration passed from Agriculture to Interior under Executive Order 6166, signed June 10, 1933 and effective August 10, the date the Park Service uses for this monument. The agency's first employee to see the site was an assistant engineer who rode twenty miles on horseback in March 1935. In 1940 a regional director recommended managing the monument as an archaeological reserve and discouraging visitors. A nominal custodian was appointed in 1942, thirty-five years after the proclamation. A 1955 draft plan, written during the Park Service's MISSION 66 program, floated handing the monument to a state agency. The first full-time ranger arrived in early 1963, fifty-six years on. Looting in the region never stopped: the administrative history reports that the market in Mimbres pottery has despoiled 95 percent of Mimbres sites in the surrounding area.

**A larger monument.** On April 17, 1962, President John F. Kennedy's Proclamation 3467, countersigned by Secretary of State Dean Rusk, added "approximately three hundred and seventy-five acres" under two headings. One was more ground around the cliff dwellings. The other, "Detached Section—T. J. Ruins," brought in the TJ Ruin, a Classic Mimbres phase pueblo dating to roughly A.D. 900–1150, centuries older than the cliff dwellings. Since then the monument has been two separate units, not one block. Kennedy's proclamation also moved the monument out of Forest Service administration to the Interior Department, and kept for the Secretary of Agriculture a corridor 150 feet either side of the center of the West Fork as a route into the national forest. Another 107 acres was withdrawn that year as a shared administrative site and remains Forest Service land.

Today the monument covers 533 acres: 480 around the cliff dwellings and 53 at TJ Ruin. It is the only unit of the national park system containing Mogollon sites. In the spring of 1975, to save money, day-to-day management went back to the Forest Service under a cooperative agreement. The Park Service kept jurisdiction, paid the Forest Service for running the monument, and supplied interpretation, preservation and research expertise, and the two agencies ran the visitor center together.

**Next door to the first wilderness.** On the edge of all this, a different idea took shape. Aldo Leopold, then Assistant District Forester in charge of Operations in Albuquerque, surveyed the Gila backcountry with Supervisor Fred Winn in 1922 and proposed setting it aside. In June 1924, after Leopold had already moved to the Forest Products Laboratory in Madison, Wisconsin, District Forester Frank C. W. Pooler approved a recreation plan for the Gila National Forest that did so. It was an administrative approval, not a law or a regulation. The Forest Service calls the result "the world's first area of land designated as wilderness," and the agencies themselves draw the line from it to the Wilderness Act of 1964.

The wilderness has changed size more than once. It was roughly 750,000 acres in 1924, became a Primitive Area under Forest Service regulations in 1933, was cut back hard in the early 1950s, and stood at 438,360 acres when the Wilderness Act took it in. About 140,000 acres were added in 1980, the same year the neighboring Aldo Leopold Wilderness was designated on its own. Today it is roughly 558,000 acres; the Gila National Forest gives 559,688. The Park Service says the monument has been "essentially surrounded" by wilderness since 1924, but a corridor for the road in from the south was cut out of the wilderness in 1953. That left the cliff dwellings, in the administrative history's words, "no longer in the heart of the wilderness" but "on the administrative edge."

The wilderness came seventeen years after the monument; in 1907 Roosevelt reserved the ground to protect a ruin, not a wilderness. Even so, the small monument ended up at the meeting point of two milestones in American conservation, the Antiquities Act and the wilderness idea.

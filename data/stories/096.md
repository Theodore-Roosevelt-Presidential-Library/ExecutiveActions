## At a glance
The executive orders of early 1908 were mostly the nuts and bolts of running a government: land offices merged, telegraph sites reserved in Alaska, a legal code built for the Panama Canal Zone. Set among them is one more bird reservation. On February 24, 1908, Roosevelt set aside the mangrove islets and sand bars of Mosquito Inlet, on Florida's Atlantic coast, as a breeding ground for native birds.

## Background
Executive Order 763 is short. It reads in full:

> It is hereby ordered that all small mangrove and salt grass islets, shoals, sand bars and sand spits, situated in Mosquito Inlet, and in and near the mouths of the Halifax and Hillsboro rivers, in townships sixteen and seventeen south, range thirty-four east of the Tallahassee meridian, Florida, and located within the area segregated by a broken line, and shown upon the diagram hereto attached and made a part of this order, are hereby reserved and set aside for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds. This reservation to be known as the Mosquito Inlet Reservation.
>
> Theodore Roosevelt. The White House, *February 24, 1908.*

It used the same one-paragraph device as the Gulf and Pacific reservations before it ([The Bird Reservations of Late 1907](story:95)). The order reserves the ground "for the use of the Department of Agriculture" without naming the Bureau of Biological Survey, though the Survey was the arm of the Department that ran the reservations. It carries no penalty clause. The "Hillsboro" in the order was already an old name in 1908: the waterway had been renamed the Indian River North in 1901. It is not Tampa's Hillsborough River.

The rest of the run fell into a few familiar strands:

- **Land offices.** The General Land Office kept shrinking its field organization as districts emptied or overlapped. The Huron district in South Dakota was folded into Pierre (Executive Order 735), Yellowstone in Montana into Glasgow (755, extended by 759), North Platte in Nebraska into Alliance (756), and Woodward in Oklahoma into Alva (746). Two weeks later another order moved the combined district's office from Alva back to Woodward (757). Executive Order 736 did something narrower, moving certain lands from New Mexico's Colfax district to the Lincoln district and leaving Colfax standing.
- **Alaska.** The Army Signal Corps reserved ground at Minto for the telegraph line it was stringing across the territory (737).
- **The Canal Zone.** The Zone's government kept assembling its legal code. Executive Order 729 added vagrancy, loitering, peddling, and disorderly-conduct provisions to the penal code. Executive Order 730 set rules for freight and baggage left unclaimed with carriers, and Executive Order 731 fixed three judicial circuits, at Ancon, Emperador and Gorgona, and Cristobal. Executive Order 732 extended to the Zone Panama's Law No. 6 of March 11, 1904, a Chinese exclusion law. Executive Order 750 granted trial by an impartial jury, but only in prosecutions "wherein the penalty of death or imprisonment for life may be inflicted."
- **Indian reservations.** Executive Orders 743 and 744 restated the boundaries of the November 1907 additions to the Jicarilla and Navajo reservations to correct an overlap between them. These were boundary corrections, not new grants of land.
- **Other business.** One order reserved land in Alabama for the War Department's improvement of the Black Warrior, Warrior, and Tombigbee rivers (745). Another extended the Spanish Treaty Claims Commission's work to September 2, 1908 (749). Executive Order 753 (February 10) added a strip of about six acres, left unplatted when the Port Angeles town site was subdivided, to the Ediz Hook Lighthouse Reservation in Washington, enlarging a reservation dating from 1862. And the usual run of clerks and inspectors were appointed or reinstated without regard to the civil-service rules.

## Historical context
Two of these strands reflect the shape of the country in 1908. The land-district mergers were the bookkeeping of a closing frontier. As the rush of homestead filings peaked in one district and rose in another, the General Land Office merged or moved its offices to follow the work. It was a quiet echo of the same public-land story that ran through the forest reserves and the reclamation projects.

The Canal Zone orders belonged to the improvised business of governing a strip of land that had been another country's territory. Since taking the Zone in 1904 ([Governing the Panama Canal Zone: the Executive Orders of 1904](story:14)), Roosevelt had governed it by executive order through the Isthmian Canal Commission and the Secretary of War. A penal code, judicial circuits, and rules for jury trial were the steady construction of a working legal system where none had existed.

Mosquito Inlet answered the older crisis that had launched the whole refuge program. The millinery trade wanted plumes, and Florida's wading birds and shorebirds were being shot out of their colonies. That alarm had produced Pelican Island five years earlier, just down the same coast.

## Political impact
None of these orders drew recorded public reaction. The land-district and Canal Zone orders were pure administration. Like the bird orders before it, Mosquito Inlet was a small stroke made on the President's signature alone. It was unremarkable in itself, but part of a growing record of executive action that Roosevelt's opponents in Congress and the West increasingly resented as government by proclamation.

## Legacy
The Mosquito Inlet Reservation did not last as a refuge, though at first it grew. On April 2, 1909, a month into his term, President Taft signed Executive Order 1057, which pushed the reservation up into the Halifax River and added what Roosevelt's order had lacked: a ban on hunting, trapping, capturing, or disturbing the birds. The penalty language often quoted for Mosquito Inlet comes from Taft's order, not Roosevelt's.

On March 17, 1928, under Coolidge, Executive Order 4832 revoked both the 1908 and 1909 orders. The Bureau of Biological Survey explained why in its report for that year: the reservation, "near New Smyrna, on the east coast of Florida, became of little value to birds owing to the growth of the town and consequent question of jurisdiction." By then the inlet had a new name. The Florida Legislature renamed it Ponce de Leon Inlet by an act approved May 7, 1927. The act gives no reason, though the land boom had made "Mosquito" a hard name to sell. The inlet lies about ten miles south of Daytona Beach. A historical marker at the Ponce de Leon Inlet Lighthouse today reads "Mosquito Inlet Bird Reservation, 1909–1918" and credits Taft; the reservation actually ran from 1908 to 1928. Mosquito Inlet still counts among the fifty-one bird reservations Roosevelt created by executive order, the body of work that became the National Wildlife Refuge System.

The other strands faded into larger institutions. The Canal Zone legal framework these orders helped build governed the Zone until the Torrijos–Carter treaties, signed September 7, 1977, handed the canal to Panama at noon on December 31, 1999. Under Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1946, the General Land Office merged with the Grazing Service to form the Bureau of Land Management.

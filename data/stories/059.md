## At a glance
On October 14, 1905, Roosevelt closed every port in the United States and Puerto Rico to arms, ammunition and munitions of war bound for the Dominican Republic. On paper the ban had no exceptions. In practice the Dominican government could get any exception it asked for, so the embargo worked to disarm the revolutionists, not the sitting government. It was one small lever in Roosevelt's much larger, and much more contested, effort to take charge of Dominican finances.

## Background
The proclamation rested on a Joint Resolution of April 22, 1898 (30 Stat. 739), passed on the eve of the war with Spain, which let the President bar the export of "coal or other material used in war from any sea-port of the United States." Roosevelt used it to prohibit arms exports to the Dominican Republic "without limitation or exception," until he or Congress lifted the ban. Secretary of State Elihu Root countersigned.

The idea came from Washington, not Santo Domingo. On October 13 Root cabled the American minister, Thomas C. Dawson: "There is now some difficulty in lawfully preventing the exportation of arms and ammunition to Santo Domingo, which would be obviated by a proclamation by the President prohibiting such exportation under a resolution of Congress approved April 22, 1898 … Ascertain whether it would be agreeable to that government." Dawson saw the foreign minister and President Carlos F. Morales at once. They "appreciated the value of the proposed proclamation," and Dawson cabled back on the 14th that it "would be agreeable to the Dominican Government." Roosevelt signed that same day.

"Without exception" was less absolute than it sounded. Root's cable announcing the proclamation told Dawson to "Inform Dominican Government any exception desired by that government will be made by special order," and the Dominican government accepted only after asking for "definite assurances" that its own arms purchases would be let through. The result was an embargo on everyone in the Dominican Republic except its government.

The "difficulty" Root mentioned was already at sea. Since early September, U.S. Navy ships under Admiral Bradford had been stopping and searching vessels along the Dominican coast. On September 5 the U.S.S. *Yankee* seized a case of cartridges from the American steamer *Cherokee* off Monte Cristi. The cartridges had come in under a permit issued to Governor Arias at Monte Cristi, but the shipment held more than the permit allowed. The proclamation gave legal footing to an interdiction the Navy was already running.

## Historical context
In 1905 the Dominican Republic was bankrupt and torn by one revolution after another. The State Department put its public debt, "not including all claims," at $32,280,000 as of September 1904, about $22 million of it owed to European creditors, against yearly revenues that had averaged roughly $1.6 million. French, Belgian and Italian claimants, backed by their governments, had won arrangements that tied up much of the customs revenue. Under a 1903 agreement with Italy, the Italian minister collected his share straight from importers. The Venezuela blockade of 1902–1903 hinted at what might come next.

Roosevelt's answer was the doctrine that bears his name. In his annual message of December 6, 1904, he declared: "Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society, may in America, as elsewhere, ultimately require intervention by some civilized nation, and in the Western Hemisphere the adherence of the United States to the Monroe Doctrine may force the United States, however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power."

In Santo Domingo, that meant a customs receivership. A protocol signed February 7, 1905, would have had the United States collect Dominican customs and divide the receipts between the government and its creditors. Roosevelt sent it to the Senate on February 15, but Democratic opposition kept it from ever coming to a vote. Roosevelt then put a substitute into effect without the Senate: a *modus vivendi* proposed by the Morales government "under pressure foreign creditors and domestic peril," in Dawson's words, and approved by Roosevelt on March 28. The Dominican decree setting it up is dated March 31 and took effect April 1, 1905.

Under that arrangement a receiver named by the President of the United States, George R. Colton, collected all customs revenue. Forty-five percent went to the Dominican government for its administration. After collection costs, the rest went into a New York bank "for the benefit of all the creditors," frozen there until the Dominican Congress and the U.S. Senate acted on the pending treaty. All payments on the debt were suspended in the meantime.

The arms embargo fit inside that project. The receivership needed a government in Santo Domingo willing to keep it, and keeping American guns away from insurgents helped keep that government in power. Root said as much a few weeks later. When Dominican port officials objected to American naval officers boarding ships ahead of them, he asked the Dominican government to either make the searches legal or have them stopped, adding, "We have some apprehension that it would encourage would-be revolutionists" (telegram to Dawson, November 4, 1905). The Dominican government chose the searches. On November 16 it authorized U.S. naval officers to board American merchant vessels "as soon as anchored and, if necessary, before any other person or authority shall have intervened."

## Political impact
The embargo rested on a clear grant from Congress, and no objection to it has been found in the diplomatic record of the time. The proclamation was made public on October 17 and went out as a short Associated Press item from Washington. The *Stockton Mail* ran it that evening under "MUSTNT SEND ARMS TO SANTO DOMINGO / Roosevelt Will Help Keep Supplies From Revolutionists," reporting an "explanatory memorandum to the effect that this action has been taken after consultation with the Dominican government, and is intended to assist that government in the enforcement of regulations designed to prevent the perennial revolutionists of the island from getting warlike supplies." The same dispatch appeared in the *Fresno Bee*, the *San Francisco Call*, the *San Jose Mercury*, the *Stockton Independent*, the Riverside *Enterprise* and the *Los Gatos Mail-News*. All were news reports. A search of California papers turned up no editorial comment; papers elsewhere have not been searched.

The friction on record came from Santo Domingo. The Dominican finance minister "was at first inclined to resent" the *Yankee*'s refusal to hand the seized cartridges over to his government. Port authorities insisted on their right to board arriving ships first. And, as Dawson put it, the Dominican government "not unnaturally dreaded the odium of seeming to have requested the employment of force and the breach of its own regulations." Governor Arias's anger over his impounded cartridges, Dawson reported, had already set back efforts to organize a guard on the Haitian frontier. The embargo did nothing about arms coming overland from Haiti.

The policy around the embargo drew far more fire than the embargo itself. Anti-imperialists and much of the Senate saw the receivership as executive overreach, a way around the treaty power when the Senate would not consent, and Democrats attacked the Caribbean turn as imperialism by another name. That resistance is why Roosevelt ran the receivership by *modus vivendi* for more than two years instead of by ratified treaty.

## Legacy
The embargo's own beneficiary did not last the winter. In December 1905 President Morales broke with his cabinet. On the night of December 24 he slipped out of the capital and was reported fighting alongside revolutionists near Jaina, while the cabinet called on Vice President Ramón Cáceres to take charge. On January 15, 1906, Dawson acknowledged the Dominican Congress's acceptance of Morales's resignation and Cáceres's succession. Within ten weeks, the man the embargo was built to protect had become one of the insurgents it was meant to disarm.

The proclamation outlived him by years. It was still in force in March 1912, when the Taft administration issued an order permitting particular exports "under the terms of, and notwithstanding the proclamation of October 14, 1905," and pointed to the procedure as a likely model for Mexico. That same month Congress rewrote the 1898 resolution. The Joint Resolution of March 14, 1912 (37 Stat. 630) let the President bar arms exports to "any American country" where "conditions of domestic violence exist which are promoted by the use of arms or munitions of war procured from the United States," and attached penalties for the first time. The Dominican embargo had used the old war-material power for exactly that purpose more than six years earlier.

The Senate finally approved a Dominican–American convention putting the receivership on a treaty footing. Signed February 8, 1907, it won the Senate's consent on February 25, 1907, by a vote of 43 to 19 that split almost exactly on party lines (Republicans 41 to 1 in favor, Democrats 18 to 2 against), and was proclaimed July 25, 1907. The receivership lasted, in changing forms, for decades. The Roosevelt Corollary became one of the most consequential and most criticized statements of American policy toward Latin America, cited to justify a long run of interventions, including the U.S. military occupation of the Dominican Republic from 1916 to 1924.

Historians still debate whether the receivership truly headed off European action and steadied Dominican finances, or simply opened an era of North American oversight resented across the region. The 1905 arms embargo, requested by Washington, welcomed by a government that would itself be in revolt within ten weeks, and enforced by U.S. Navy boarding parties in Dominican waters, is a modest but telling piece of that record.

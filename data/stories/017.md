## At a glance
On November 16, 1907, Roosevelt signed Proclamation 780, admitting Oklahoma to the Union "on an equal footing with the original States." It joined Oklahoma Territory, settled through land runs, and Indian Territory, home of the Five Tribes, into a single state. Party politics in Washington shaped that outcome. More than a century later, the line between the two former territories still matters in court.

## Background
For most of the nineteenth century the land was split in two. Oklahoma Territory in the west was opened to white settlement through land runs that began in 1889. Indian Territory in the east belonged to the Five Tribes, the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek and Seminole, who had been removed there from the Southeast two generations earlier.

The Oklahoma Enabling Act, which Roosevelt signed on June 16, 1906, allowed the two territories to write one constitution and enter the Union together. A convention met at Guthrie and drafted the document. On September 17, 1907, voters ratified it and also approved a separate proposition making prohibition statewide. The Enabling Act had required prohibition for twenty-one years only in the former Indian Territory, the Osage Reservation and other reservation lands. Voters were the ones who applied it to the whole state, 130,361 to 112,258. Once the results were certified in Washington, Roosevelt signed the proclamation, and Guthrie became the first capital.

Statehood ended Indian Territory as a separate jurisdiction, but it did not abolish the Five Tribes' governments. The Curtis Act of 1898 had already done severe damage. It abolished the tribal courts, extended federal law over everyone in the territory, and placed tribal enrollment and land allotment under the Dawes Commission without the tribes' consent. But the Five Tribes Act of April 26, 1906 stated that "the tribal existence and present tribal governments" of the five nations "are hereby continued in full force and effect for all purposes authorized by law." For decades after 1907 those governments were suppressed, but they were never dissolved.

## Historical context
The road to a single Oklahoma was a hard political fight, and votes in Washington decided it as much as the wishes of people in the territories. At first, Indian Territory didn't want to join Oklahoma Territory. In August 1905, leaders of the Five Tribes held the Sequoyah Convention at the Hinton Theater in Muskogee and wrote a constitution for a separate Indian-Territory state named Sequoyah. Creek Chief Pleasant Porter chaired the convention, Charles N. Haskell was vice chair, and the Creek poet Alexander Posey served as secretary. On November 7, 1905, voters approved the Sequoyah constitution 56,279 to 9,073, and Congress refused to take it up.

A separate Sequoyah would almost certainly have sent Democrats to Congress, and the Republican majority had no interest in creating another reliably Democratic state with two Senate seats. Roosevelt backed joint statehood instead. In his Fifth Annual Message of December 5, 1905, he told Congress:

> "I recommend that Indian Territory and Oklahoma be admitted as one State and that New Mexico and Arizona be admitted as one State. There is no obligation upon us to treat territorial subdivisions, which are matters of convenience only, as binding us on the question of admission to Statehood."

He ended the paragraph impatiently: "There is no justification for further delay; and the advisability of making the four Territories into two States has been clearly established."

The Enabling Act also offered Arizona and New Mexico admission as a single state, but only if each territory approved it separately. On November 6, 1906, New Mexico voted yes, 26,195 to 14,735. Arizona voted no, 16,265 to 3,141. That ended the Southwest half of the plan, and the two came in as separate states in 1912.

The Oklahoma convention met at Guthrie from November 20, 1906 to March 15, 1907, with shorter sittings in April and July. Democrats held 99 of the 112 seats, and the convention reflected the Populist mood of the time. Under its president, William H. "Alfalfa Bill" Murray, it produced a long, detailed constitution full of railroad and corporate regulation.

Roosevelt didn't like it. He thought it was far too long and too full of ordinary legislation. On May 26, 1907, he wrote to Attorney General Charles J. Bonaparte:

> "Is it not worth while to consider whether you, or perhaps Taft, in a speech should not point out the worst features of the Constitution, or the features that are so bad that we cannot submit to them? If this is done, and the people deliberately refuse to amend the constitution so as to get rid of the objectionable features, we are entirely clear."

Bonaparte drafted the administration's objections, but he found nothing in the document that conflicted with the Enabling Act or the federal Constitution. In July the delegates made mostly small wording changes and removed one substantive clause, which would have suspended a corporation's charter if it appealed to the federal courts. Taft made the public case against the draft in Oklahoma City on August 24, 1907, calling it "a code of law; indeed, it is a code of by-law." William Jennings Bryan campaigned for ratification. On September 17, voters approved the constitution 180,353 to 75,051 and elected Haskell governor over Frank Frantz, 134,162 to 106,507.

The tribal nations of Indian Territory had the least say in any of this. The Curtis Act and the Dawes allotment rolls had already broken up their governments and their communally held land, and their bid for a state of their own was set aside because of party politics in Washington.

## Political impact
By November 1907 the real fights, over joint statehood and over the constitution, were finished, and the new state treated the signing as a celebration. On statehood day, on the steps of the Carnegie Library in Guthrie, a mock wedding joined "Mr. Oklahoma Territory," played by C. G. Jones of Oklahoma City, to "Miss Indian Territory," played by Anna Trainor Bennett of Muskogee. Haskell was inaugurated as the first governor, and the state quickly proved as Democratic as Republicans had feared.

National opinion on the constitution split. Reformers and Populists praised a charter that reined in railroads and corporations, while conservatives and much of the business press saw it as dangerously radical. The prohibition clause stayed controversial: the temperance movement welcomed it, and others objected to putting it in the state's founding document instead of ordinary law. The constitution required that the state make "no distinction in civil or political rights on account of race or color," as the Enabling Act demanded. Even so, the first state legislature passed a separate-coach law, approved December 18, 1907, a month after statehood.

A few weeks after signing, Roosevelt reported the new state to Congress in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907, with a pointed qualification:

> "Oklahoma has become a State, standing on a full equality with her elder sisters, and her future is assured by her great natural resources. The duty of the National Government to guard the personal and property rights of the Indians within her borders remains of course unchanged."

## Legacy
### The constitution
Oklahoma's 1907 constitution, the one Roosevelt thought unwieldy, is still in force, and no later document has replaced it. The Council of State Governments counted about 84,956 words as of January 1, 2023, making it the fourth-longest state constitution in the country, behind Alabama, Texas and Missouri. Of 376 amendments submitted, 200 had been adopted.

### Prohibition
Prohibition lasted far longer in Oklahoma than it did nationally. On April 7, 1959, voters approved State Question 386, 396,845 to 314,380. It repealed the Prohibition Ordinance and Article I, Section 7 of the constitution, added a new Article XXVII, and allowed privately owned package stores. Governor J. Howard Edmondson signed the Liquor Control Act on June 23, and at ten o'clock on the morning of September 1, 1959, the first legal package sales in the state's history began. Selling liquor by the drink stayed illegal for another quarter century, until State Question 563 passed on September 18, 1984, and even then each county had to approve it.

### The capital
The Enabling Act had fixed the capital at Guthrie until 1913, but it didn't stay there that long. In a special election on June 11, 1910, voters chose to move it right away, 96,448 to 64,522, and picked Oklahoma City over Guthrie and Shawnee. Governor Haskell moved the state seal to Oklahoma City and set up in the Lee-Huckins Hotel. In November 1910 the state supreme court voided that election because of faulty wording in the initiative, while also ruling that the Enabling Act's Guthrie clause didn't bind the state. The legislature then located the capital at Oklahoma City by an act of December 29, 1910.

In *Coyle v. Smith* (1911), the U.S. Supreme Court upheld the move. It ruled that "the power to locate its own seat of government, to change the same, and to appropriate its public money therefor, are essentially state powers beyond the control of Congress." A quarrel over a capital building became the leading statement of the principle that new states enter the Union on an equal footing with the original ones. A 1912 initiative to bring the capital back to Guthrie failed.

### The Five Tribes
For the tribal nations of the former Indian Territory, statehood completed the allotment-era breakup of their communally held land. Their governments lived on, suppressed but never dissolved. From 1907 to 1970 the principal chiefs were appointed by presidents and later by the Secretary of the Interior, sometimes for a single day to sign a document. The Oklahoma Indian Welfare Act of June 26, 1936 opened reorganization to Oklahoma tribes, and a law of October 22, 1970 restored the Five Tribes' right to elect their own principal officers.

The legal questions statehood left open were never fully closed. In *McGirt v. Oklahoma*, decided 5–4 on July 9, 2020, the Supreme Court held that Congress had never disestablished the Muscogee (Creek) reservation, so the land "remains 'Indian country'" under the federal Major Crimes Act. Justice Gorsuch wrote that "[o]nce a federal reservation is established, only Congress can diminish or disestablish it."

The decision concerns criminal jurisdiction. It didn't move any land or change any deed or title, and the Court said it "concerns the statutory definition of 'Indian country' as it applies in federal criminal law." Later rulings have narrowed it. Oklahoma's criminal appeals court held in 2021 that it doesn't apply retroactively on collateral review. *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta* (2022) held that the state shares jurisdiction with the federal government over crimes by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country, without overruling *McGirt*. Oklahoma courts have declined to extend it to civil and tax matters, as in *Stroble v. Oklahoma Tax Commission* (2025). They have applied its reservation holding to the Chickasaw, Cherokee, Choctaw, Seminole, Quapaw, Ottawa, Peoria, Miami and Wyandotte nations, and in 2024 they held the Osage reservation disestablished, a ruling now before the Tenth Circuit.

In dissent, Chief Justice Roberts described the area at stake as "the entire eastern half of the State — 19 million acres that are home to 1.8 million people, only 10%–15% of whom are Indians." The boundary Roosevelt's proclamation seemed to erase still had legal force more than a century later. The two territories joined in 1907 were never equal partners, and the proclamation settled less than it appeared to.

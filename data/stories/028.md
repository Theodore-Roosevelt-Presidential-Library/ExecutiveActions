## At a glance
For three days in May 1908, Theodore Roosevelt seated the governors of the states and territories in the East Room alongside the Cabinet, the Supreme Court and members of Congress, and asked them to think about the nation's forests, waters, soils and minerals as one connected problem. The conference turned "conservation" into a household word, and the governors' gatherings it started became today's National Governors Association. Roosevelt followed it with a National Conservation Commission that produced the first inventory of the country's natural resources. Congress, wary of commissions it had never authorized, cut off the money.

## Background
**On the river.** The idea for the conference came off the Mississippi. On March 14, 1907, Roosevelt created the Inland Waterways Commission, by letter of invitation rather than by executive order or act of Congress, to study the country's rivers as one system. Representative Theodore E. Burton of Ohio chaired it. W J McGee, an erosion expert in the Bureau of Soils, served as secretary and is generally credited as its intellectual organizer. The members came back convinced that rivers, forests, soils and minerals could not be managed piecemeal: strip the forest and the flood follows; silt the river and the harbor dies.

On October 3, 1907, meeting aboard the steamer *Col. A. Mackenzie* during a low-water inspection trip down the Mississippi, Burton and McGee wrote to Roosevelt on his own boat, the *Mississippi*, asking him to call a conference and specifying that "the conferees should comprise the governors of all our States and Territories." The next day, closing an address to the Deep Waterway Convention at Memphis on October 4, 1907, Roosevelt announced it:

> "As a preliminary step, the Inland Waterways Commission has asked me to call a conference on the conservation of natural resources, including, of course, the streams, to meet in Washington during the coming winter. I shall accordingly call such conference. It ought to be among the most important gatherings in our history, for none have had a more vital question to consider."

He sent the invitations to the governors on November 13, 1907, for a meeting at the White House on May 13–15, 1908.

**Who came.** The official *Proceedings* lists fifty-one governors and acting governors, covering all forty-six states and the territories and dependencies of Alaska, Arizona, Hawaii, New Mexico and Porto Rico. Twelve are marked absent, which leaves thirty-nine there in person: thirty-eight governors and Acting Governor X. O. Pindall of Arkansas. Texas sent its lieutenant governor and Maine an ex-governor, so every jurisdiction had someone in the room, and the District of Columbia was represented by the president of its Board of Commissioners. Seven of the nine Cabinet secretaries came (Taft was in Panama, and Metcalf was on the Pacific coast with the battleship fleet), along with the Vice-President and the nine justices of the Supreme Court. Every member of Congress was invited. Four special guests attended, and all four spoke: Andrew Carnegie, the railroad builder James J. Hill, the Democratic leader William Jennings Bryan, and the labor leader John Mitchell. A fifth, former President Grover Cleveland, was, in the words of the *Proceedings*, "confined to his home by illness which soon after proved fatal." He died six weeks later, on June 24, 1908.

**The commission.** On June 8, 1908, Roosevelt appointed the National Conservation Commission, again not by executive order but by a letter to each of its forty-eight members, with Thomas R. Shipp as secretary. Gifford Pinchot chaired the whole Commission. It worked in four sections: waters under Theodore E. Burton, forests under Reed Smoot, lands under Knute Nelson, and minerals under John Dalzell. Its charge was to count what the country had — timber, coal, iron, water power, arable soil — and how fast it was being used up. The same day, Executive Order 809 directed the departments to furnish the Commission the data it needed.

The Commission signed its report on December 7, 1908. Pinchot sent it to Roosevelt on January 11, 1909, and Roosevelt transmitted it to Congress by special message on January 22, 1909, calling it "the first inventory of its natural resources ever made by any nation." The Senate ordered it printed in three volumes in February 1909.

## Historical context
The conference gave a stage to an anxiety that had been building for a generation. The frontier had been declared closed in 1890. The timber was visibly going. The coal and iron everyone had called inexhaustible were, experts now said, finite and in sight of the end.

Roosevelt opened the conference in the East Room on May 13, 1908, with the address popularly titled "Conservation as a National Duty." He began:

> "This Conference on the conservation of natural resources is in effect a meeting of the representatives of all the people of the United States called to consider the weightiest problem now before the Nation; and the occasion for the meeting lies in the fact that the natural resources of our country are in danger of exhaustion if we permit the old wasteful methods of exploiting them longer to continue."

He laid out what had already been spent:

> "We began with an unapproached heritage of forests; more than half of the timber is gone. We began with coal fields more extensive than those of any other nation and with iron ores regarded as inexhaustible, and many experts now declare that the end of both iron and coal is in sight."

His conservation meant use, not lockup: "The wise use of all of our natural resources, which are our national resources as well, is the great material question of today." Minerals had to be spent carefully, because their exhaustion was certain; soil, forest and water had to be used in ways that improved rather than destroyed them. He tied all of it to "national efficiency" and to a parent's plain duty to hand the children an unspent inheritance:

> "So this Nation as a whole should earnestly desire and strive to leave to the next generation the national honor unstained and the national resources unexhausted."

It was a moral appeal built on an economic fear, which is exactly how conservation was argued in 1908.

Governors had met together before; thirteen Union governors gathered at Altoona, Pennsylvania, in September 1862. Roosevelt described what was new more carefully: "for the first time in our history the chief executive officers of the States separately, and of the States together forming the Nation, have met to consider it." The novelty was governors in a room with the President, the Cabinet and the Court, called together on one subject.

## Political impact
As spectacle, the conference was a triumph. The newspapers filled with "conservation," a word that had been Forest Service jargon, and it became a household term almost overnight.

On the final morning, May 15, Governor Newton C. Blanchard of Louisiana reported a declaration from the Committee on Resolutions. It opened:

> "We, the Governors of the States and Territories of the United States of America, in Conference assembled, do hereby declare the conviction that the great prosperity of our country rests upon the abundant resources of the land chosen by our forefathers for their homes and where they laid the foundation of this great Nation.
>
> We look upon these resources as a heritage to be made use of in establishing and promoting the comfort, prosperity, and happiness of the American People, but not to be wasted, deteriorated, or needlessly destroyed."

It closed: "Let us conserve the foundations of our prosperity." Governor Robert B. Glenn of North Carolina asked for time to consider it, so the vote came that afternoon. The question "was taken viva voce, and Governor Blanchard's motion was agreed to without dissenting voice."

The session was not free of argument. Glenn moved an amendment that would have obliged the President to call a conference "whenever requested to do so by one-third of the Governors," then withdrew it. Governor Joseph W. Folk of Missouri objected on principle, and his words are the strongest statement of the case against the whole exercise. The declaration, he said, was "conferring, not upon the individual who happens to occupy the Chair now, but upon that Office, a power and authority to call the Governors of the States together at any time that he may see fit. The distinguished personage before us can not always be President of the United States [laughter]." He added: "I do not believe in establishing any precedent, however unimportant it may seem at the time, which step by step may eventually destroy that independence."

The idea spread fast. By January 1909, Pinchot reported, thirty-six states and territories had formed conservation commissions of their own.

**Congress pushes back.** The enthusiasm in the East Room masked a hardening resistance in the Capitol. Congress had grown wary of Roosevelt's habit of governing through commissions it had never created and did not control: the Inland Waterways Commission, the Country Life Commission, and now the National Conservation Commission. Representative James A. Tawney of Minnesota, chairman of the House Appropriations Committee, led the response. Section 9 of the Sundry Civil Appropriations Act of March 4, 1909 barred paying any commission's expenses, or lending it government employees, "unless the creation of the same shall be or shall have been authorized by law." On February 25, 1909 the House voted 140 to 60 on Tawney's motion to suspend the rules and make the provision in order; members seeking a roll call fell short of the number needed to demand one. The provision became law with the rest of the bill. It starved the National Conservation Commission of the means to continue. To many members it was a matter of principle: setting standing commissions to work without a law or an appropriation was exactly the executive overreach they had been elected to check.

**One more conference, and a congress that never met.** In his last weeks in office Roosevelt tried to carry the momentum abroad. A North American Conservation Conference met at the White House from February 18 to 23, 1909, with delegates from the United States, Canada, Newfoundland and Mexico, and adopted a declaration of principles. On February 19 the State Department circulated an invitation to a world conservation congress at The Hague. Acting Secretary of State Alvey A. Adee signed the circular. It named no number of nations ("forty-five" is Roosevelt's later figure), and it claimed only that "upon informal inquiry a gratifying assurance of the sympathy of the Government of the Netherlands has been received." The Dutch chargé protested in April that his government had been left under a misapprehension.

## Legacy
The 1908 conference is usually marked as the moment American conservation stopped being a set of separate campaigns and became a national cause with a name.

**The governors.** The National Governors Association dates its founding to this conference, though the line is not unbroken. The 1908 meeting was called, hosted and run by the President on one subject; the governors did not organize themselves into anything. They did not meet in 1909. They met in Washington in January 1910, at Frankfort and Louisville, Kentucky, that November and December, and at Spring Lake, New Jersey, in September 1911. At Richmond on December 4, 1912, they adopted articles of organization and became a standing body, the Governors' Conference. It became the National Governors' Conference in 1965 and the National Governors Association in 1977.

**The world congress.** After Roosevelt left office, Secretary of State Knox sent a circular on April 21, 1909, recasting the February invitation as meant "merely to cause them to make preliminary inquiry" and calling a fixed date "obviously impossible at the time when the holding of the congress is problematical." It was a quiet downgrade rather than a cancellation, close to Roosevelt's own account that "after I left the White House the project lapsed." The congress never met.

**The commission and the quarrel.** Without money from Congress, the National Conservation Commission effectively ended after its inventory, and the conservation program lost its footing once Roosevelt left office and the Ballinger–Pinchot quarrel split the movement. On September 13, 1909, President Taft exonerated Interior Secretary Richard Ballinger and authorized him to dismiss Louis Glavis, who had raised charges about Alaskan coal claims. The quarrel broke into the open in *Collier's Weekly*, whose issue dated November 13, 1909 carried Glavis's charges. On January 7, 1910, Taft wrote to Pinchot that "by your own conduct you have destroyed your usefulness as a helpful subordinate of the Government," and directed Secretary of Agriculture James Wilson to remove him as Forester; Wilson's removal order bears the same date. A joint congressional committee took testimony from January to May 1910, with Louis Brandeis, retained by *Collier's*, appearing as counsel for Glavis. Seven Republican members signed a majority report clearing Ballinger, four Democrats signed a minority report, and Representative Edward H. Madison of Kansas filed his own. The reports went to Congress on December 7, 1910. Ballinger resigned anyway; Taft accepted his resignation on March 7, 1911, and Walter L. Fisher took the oath as his successor on March 13.

What survived was less an institution than a habit of mind: the idea, made vivid in the East Room, that the country's forests, waters, soils and minerals are a single inheritance held in trust, to be used, in Roosevelt's framing, but not used up. The Commission's inventory was the first serious attempt to count what the nation had. That the counting stopped when the money did is as much a part of the story as the speech that began it.

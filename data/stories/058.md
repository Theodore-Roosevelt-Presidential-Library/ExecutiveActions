## At a glance
On March 29, 1905, Roosevelt proclaimed a great naval, marine, and military celebration on Hampton Roads for 1907, the three-hundredth anniversary of Jamestown. In it he invited "all the nations of the earth" to send their warships. The fair that followed lost money and closed deep in debt. But its grounds on Sewell's Point became Naval Station Norfolk.

## Background
Congress passed the enabling act on March 3, 1905. The act itself set the celebration "on and near the waters of Hampton Roads," starting May 13 and ending no later than November 1, 1907. It *authorized* the President to proclaim the celebration and invite foreign nations, but did not require him to. The proclamation repeated the act's dates word for word. What it added was the invitation: the President, "in the name of the Government and of the people of the United States," asked every nation to send naval vessels and, as far as practicable, military units.

The act, not the proclamation, did the spending and organizing. It appropriated $250,000:

- $50,000 for the expenses of a federal commission
- $125,000 to entertain foreign military and naval guests
- $50,000 for a permanent monument on Jamestown Island, on a site to be donated to the United States
- $15,000 for permanent moorings in Hampton Roads
- $10,000 to exhibit "one or more of the old monitors" where the *Monitor* and *Merrimac* had fought

The act also let the President form a Jamestown Ter-Centennial Commission of the Secretaries of the Treasury, War, and the Navy. None of the money went to the private company building the fair. The proclamation's text breaks off one section of the act with an ellipsis. The part it skips, authority to invite the state militias "but at their own expense," was used a year later (see [Ceremonial and Relief Proclamations of 1906](story:66)).

One small wording change is worth noticing. The act's title speaks of the first English settlement "on the Western Hemisphere," while the proclamation says "on the American Continent." Both date it to "the thirteenth day of May, sixteen hundred and seven." That was the day the three English ships came up to Jamestown Island. The colonists went ashore the next day.

## Historical context
Americans of the era marked founding events with great expositions: Philadelphia in 1876, Chicago in 1893, and the Louisiana Purchase Exposition at St. Louis in 1904 (see [Louisiana Purchase Exposition](story:36)). Williamsburg businessmen proposed a Jamestown tercentennial as early as 1900. Business leaders in Newport News and Norfolk turned the idea into a national exposition. The Virginia General Assembly chartered the Jamestown Exposition Company on March 10, 1902, with Fitzhugh Lee, the former governor and Confederate cavalry general, as president. In 1903 it voted the company $200,000, on condition that the company first sell stock.

So the push came from the Hampton Roads business community, its company, and Virginia's delegation in Congress. The President backed them. In his annual message to Congress of December 6, 1904, Roosevelt wrote: "In 1907 there will be held at Hampton Roads the tricentennial celebration of the settlement at Jamestown, Virginia, with which the history of what has now become the United States really begins. I commend this to your favorable consideration. It is an event of prime historic significance, in which all the people of the United States should feel, and should show, great and general interest."

The naval theme was Virginia's idea, not Roosevelt's. The company chose Sewell's Point in 1902 for local reasons. Jamestown Island was then lonely ground without water, sewers, or shelter for crowds, and the Hampton Roads cities wanted the visitors for themselves. Sewell's Point was undeveloped farmland in Norfolk County, about equally distant from the sponsoring cities, and it faced the deep anchorage of Hampton Roads. Representative Harry L. Maynard of Virginia had proposed "an international naval and marine exposition" in 1904. When a larger bill stalled in 1905, Representative James A. Tawney of Minnesota told the House that the final bill was "drafted yesterday at the request of the Speaker and others who were interested" as a compromise, adding that "this is not an exposition but a celebration." The Navy Department advised only on cost. The House passed the bill 192 to 92 on March 2, 1905, and the Senate passed it the same day without amendment. Still, the theme suited a President who had been Assistant Secretary of the Navy and who argued steadily for a bigger battleship fleet. A review of the world's warships on Hampton Roads would put the growing American Navy beside them.

## Political impact
The proclamation asked nothing of anyone and drew no recorded objection. It used a power Congress had granted four weeks earlier. Virginia greeted it as a win. It made the front pages of the Norfolk *Virginian-Pilot*, the Newport News *Daily Press*, and the Richmond *Times-Dispatch* on March 30, 1905, and ran in town and country papers across the state over the following week. The *Times-Dispatch*'s Norfolk report said the President's prompt action had put the fair's management in good shape, and quoted C. Brooks Johnston, chairman of the company's board of governors, praising the promptness and wording of the proclamation, which let the managers call at once on foreign governments for exhibits. On April 4 a Norfolk paper wrote that the proclamation made certain the exposition would feature the greatest naval display in the world's history, and predicted that the chance to see the warships of every navy together would draw more visitors than anything at any earlier American fair.

The real fight was over money, and it happened before and after the proclamation. A 1904 attempt to win federal backing, through bills from Senator John W. Daniel and Representative Maynard, had died in committee. The 1905 act gave the promoters the national proclamation they wanted but little cash for the fair. In 1906 Congress paid for the government's own exhibits, buildings, and piers at the fair, $200,000, $350,000, and $400,000, and nothing for the company. Money for the company came only with an urgent deficiency act of February 9, 1907: $1,000,000 "for the purpose of aiding in the payment of the cost of the construction, completion, and opening" of the exposition, paid in installments through April. It was a debt, not a gift. The company owed it back to the government, secured by a first lien on gate and concession receipts, 40 percent of which were to go to the Treasury twice a month starting May 31.

Fitzhugh Lee died in 1905. Former Congressman Harry St. George Tucker succeeded him as company president, and the company struggled from the start to pay for its grounds and finish them on time. The Ter-Centennial Commission the act allowed was formed by executive order on January 5, 1906, with Secretaries Leslie M. Shaw, William H. Taft, and Charles J. Bonaparte (see [The Executive Orders of Early 1906](story:87)).

## Legacy
The exposition opened on April 26, 1907, with Roosevelt there to open it and to review the American and foreign warships from the presidential yacht *Mayflower*. He came back on June 10 for Georgia Day. The grounds were not finished on opening day, and the crowds fell far short of the six million the promoters had hoped for.

The fair closed on November 30, 1907, the closing date set by Congress. The Newport News *Daily Press* reported that Tucker turned off the lights "at midnight" after the formal exercises. Director General Alvah H. Martin put "the total admissions since the opening" at 2,800,000. Tucker listed the company's debts as a first mortgage, a second mortgage, and a "balance due the government" of $900,000, so only about a tenth of the 1907 loan had been repaid. Less than a month after closing, the company declared bankruptcy.

The fair's lasting mark was on the map, not the ledger. The sixteen Atlantic Fleet battleships later known as the "Great White Fleet" sailed from Hampton Roads on December 16, 1907, with Roosevelt watching. They returned there on February 22, 1909, and he reviewed them as they came into the roadstead.

After the United States entered the First World War, an urgent deficiency act of June 15, 1917, authorized the President to take "the tract of land known as the Jamestown Exposition site" for a naval operating base. It set aside up to $1,200,000 to pay the owners and $1,600,000 toward equipping the base. Woodrow Wilson took possession by proclamation on June 28, 1917. The grounds became Naval Station Norfolk, today one of the largest naval bases in the world. Seventeen exposition buildings still stand on the base, most of them former state pavilions now used as officers' quarters along "Admiral's Row." They were listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.

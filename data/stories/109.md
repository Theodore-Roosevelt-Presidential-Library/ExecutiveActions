## At a glance
Three small proclamations from February 1909 close out Roosevelt's numbered proclamations. One set apart February 12, the hundredth anniversary of Abraham Lincoln's birth, as a special legal holiday. One lowered the duty on Spanish sparkling wine in exchange for Spanish concessions. One called the Senate into session on inauguration day to act on William Howard Taft's nominations.

## Background
All three were countersigned by Robert Bacon, who became Secretary of State on January 27, 1909, the same day Elihu Root left the office. Root had been elected to the Senate from New York, but his term did not begin until March 4. Bacon's thirty-seven days as Secretary filled exactly that gap.

Each proclamation did one narrow thing.

**The Lincoln holiday (Proclamation 842, February 11).** The proclamation did not create the holiday on its own. Congress had already done that in a joint resolution approved the same day (Public Resolution No. 42, 60th Congress), making February 12, 1909 a special legal holiday "in the District of Columbia and the Territories of the United States." The resolution authorized the President to proclaim it, and Roosevelt did so with one day to spare:

> *Now, Therefore, I, Theodore Roosevelt,* President of the United States of America, in virtue of the authority conferred upon me by the said Joint Resolution, do hereby set apart the twelfth day of February, nineteen hundred and nine, as a special legal holiday.

The resolution said nothing about the states. Congress makes laws directly for the District and the territories, but it has no general power to declare legal holidays inside the states, which set their own.

**Spanish sparkling wine (Proclamation 850, February 20).** Section 3 of the Dingley Tariff Act of July 24, 1897 let the President trade modest cuts from the tariff's high rates for equivalent concessions from other countries. The United States and Spain had a commercial agreement, signed at San Sebastián on August 1, 1906. An exchange of notes on February 20, 1909 extended it to sparkling wine. Roosevelt suspended the regular duties on Spanish sparkling wines and put the lower section 3 rates in their place: "six dollars per dozen" for quart bottles, three dollars for pints, and a dollar and a half for half-pints or smaller. The official text spells the city "San Sebastion," a misspelling that appears in the *Statutes at Large* itself.

**Calling the Senate (Proclamation 856, February 27).** This was the routine summons that Article II, section 3 of the Constitution allows. Roosevelt called the Senate to meet at noon on March 4, 1909, inauguration day, "to receive such communications as may be made by the Executive." That meant the new president's nominations.

## Historical context
**A national birthday.** The Lincoln centennial was a real national event. A hundred years after his birth, with the Civil War two generations past, Lincoln belonged to a reunited country, and the day was marked with speeches, dinners and ceremonies from coast to coast.

Roosevelt gave the day personal weight. On February 12, 1909, he traveled to Hodgenville, Kentucky, and laid the cornerstone of the memorial building going up over the log-cabin birthplace site. A funding shortfall meant that only the foundation and the cornerstone were in place that day. The building's plaque still reads "Cornerstone Laid By President Roosevelt February 12th, 1909 / Dedicated By President Taft November 9th, 1911."

Roosevelt spoke there too. The Government Printing Office published the speech, and it opens:

> "We have met here to celebrate the hundredth anniversary of the birth of one of the two greatest Americans; of one of the two or three greatest men of the nineteenth century; of one of the greatest men in the world's history."

A line often credited to Roosevelt at Hodgenville, comparing the log cabin to the stable at Bethlehem, does not appear in the printed address, and no Roosevelt source for it has been found.

**Tariff bargaining.** The Spanish wine order followed a well-worn path. Section 3 of the Dingley Act had already produced a string of small reciprocity deals, each swapping a narrow duty cut on a signature export for better treatment of American goods ([The Residual 1908 Proclamations](story:107)). Spain was not singled out: France had received the same sparkling-wine concession in 1908, and Italy got one about a week after Spain.

**The calendar's rhythm.** A Congress expired at noon on March 4, the new president was sworn in the same day, and the Senate had to be sitting to act on his appointments. Presidents issued this kind of call as a matter of course. Roosevelt had signed one before each of his own terms: Proclamation 498 of March 2, 1903, and Proclamation 538 of February 23, 1905. This time he was summoning the Senate for the benefit of his chosen successor.

## Political impact
None of the three stirred real controversy, and each did exactly the job it was written to do.

**The Lincoln holiday.** The proclamation was part of a celebration the whole country embraced. Roosevelt's trip to Kentucky drew the press to the birthplace, not to the document. The Associated Press dispatch printed in the *Los Angeles Herald* the next day reported that "The laying of the corner stone took place after appropriate forensic ceremonies." The memorial building rose over the following years, and President Taft dedicated it on November 9, 1911.

**The Spanish wine order.** This was a technical tariff notice that interested importers, customs officers and the domestic wine trade, and almost no one else. The Treasury put it to work right away: a circular of February 26 (T.D. 29583) told customs officers to apply the new rates. Although the proclamation is dated February 20, newspapers did not pick it up until February 23–25, mostly from the same Associated Press paragraph: the *Alexandria Gazette* on February 23, the *Salt Lake Herald* ("CONCESSION TO SPAIN") on February 24, and the *New-York Tribune* ("LOWER DUTY ON SPANISH WINES"), the New York *Sun*, the *San Francisco Call* and the *Washington Herald* on February 25. Five country weeklies reprinted the same paragraph into early March. None ran an editorial or quoted an objector, and several used it as column filler.

The one interest it touched did object. San Francisco's trade paper, the *Pacific Wine and Spirit Review*, covered the order twice. On February 28, 1909 it reported from Washington that sparkling wines from Spain were to get the benefit of the Dingley Act's minimum rates. On March 31 it reprinted the proclamation in full, with the Treasury circular, alongside an *Oakland Tribune* editorial. The announcement, the editorial said, had come "[w]hen the California wine industry is passing through a period of extraordinary depression," and the concession "will scarcely be welcomed [b]y California wine growers. If any one is entitled to be favored [i]t is the grower and manufacturer of the domestic product, not [t]he producer of a foreign wine which comes in competition with [t]he home article." It was a minor irritant to California growers in a bad year. It drew no recorded fight in Washington.

Sparkling-wine duties were still a live issue, though. By April 13, the New York *Sun* reported, the Senate Finance Committee was raising them in the Payne tariff bill, from eight dollars to nine dollars and sixty cents a dozen quarts. Within seven weeks of Roosevelt's proclamation, Congress was moving the duty the other way, and section 3 reciprocity as a whole was under attack in the Payne–Aldrich fight.

The new tariff, approved August 5, 1909, replaced the Dingley rates the next day. It did not cancel the reciprocity agreements outright, though. Section 4 directed the President to give notice of termination within ten days and provided that "the terms of said commercial agreements shall remain in force" until each notice ran out. Secretary of State Philander Knox wrote the Spanish Minister on August 7, 1909, ending the agreements of August 1, 1906 and February 20, 1909 "to take effect one year from the present date, namely, August 7, 1910." Roosevelt's lower rates on Spanish sparkling wine lasted about eighteen months.

**The Senate session.** The Senate met as called at noon on March 4, 1909, sat three days, and adjourned on March 6. Its real work came on March 5, the day after the inauguration. In an executive session the *Congressional Record* puts at fifty-five minutes, the Senate received and confirmed all nine of Taft's Cabinet nominations: Knox at State, MacVeagh at the Treasury, Dickinson at War, Wickersham as Attorney General, Hitchcock as Postmaster General, Meyer at the Navy, Ballinger at the Interior, Wilson at Agriculture and Nagel at Commerce and Labor. Huntington Wilson's nomination as Assistant Secretary of State came in the same day.

## Legacy
**Lincoln's Birthday.** February 12 never became a federal holiday. Many states observe it, but it has never appeared in the federal holiday law. In popular practice it was absorbed into the third Monday in February created by the Uniform Monday Holiday Act, approved June 28, 1968 and effective January 1, 1971. That law moved Washington's Birthday from February 22 to the third Monday; it renamed nothing. "Presidents' Day" appears nowhere in the statute, and the holiday is still officially Washington's Birthday.

**The birthplace.** The cornerstone Roosevelt laid became the granite Memorial Building that still shelters the birthplace cabin at Abraham Lincoln Birthplace National Historical Park. The site has carried several titles: national park in 1916, national historical park in 1939, national historic site in 1959, and national historical park again under the Omnibus Public Land Management Act of March 30, 2009. The National Park Service presents the cabin as a symbolic one, standing in for the original, which the Service says was likely lost; its logs date mostly to the 1840s.

**The wine deal.** The Spanish agreement ended in August 1910. Spain later announced in November 1922 that it was ending the 1906 agreement, and that date is often the first one a search turns up. The editors of *Foreign Relations of the United States* note that the agreement had in fact been dead since 1910.

**The Senate call.** The special session was routine, and its only distinction was its place at the hinge between two administrations. With these three proclamations, the series of Roosevelt's numbered proclamations is complete.

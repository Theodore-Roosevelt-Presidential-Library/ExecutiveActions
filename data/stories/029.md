## At a glance
On February 7, 1908, Theodore Roosevelt set aside Jewel Cave, in the Black Hills of southwestern South Dakota, as a national monument "of scientific interest." It was the first monument proclaimed for a cave as its sole named object. Two prospecting brothers had found it in 1900, failed to make it pay as an attraction, and asked the government for a game preserve; the Forest Service found no game but recommended a monument instead. Barely a mile or two of passage was known in 1908. More than 220 miles have been mapped since, most of it outside the line drawn that year.

## Background
Proclamation 799 reserved a small block of the Black Hills National Forest to protect "the natural formation, known as the Jewel Cave," and its calcite crystal linings, the "jewels" that gave the place its name. Like Devils Tower and the volcanic monuments, it rested on the Antiquities Act's "scientific interest" clause, not the "historic" clause used for the cliff dwellings of the Southwest.

The monument stayed inside the national forest and under Forest Service administration. The proclamation let both reservations stand on the same ground, allowing forest uses to continue but making "the National Monument hereby established … the dominant reservation," as the Gila and Pinnacles proclamations had done ([Gila Cliff-Dwellings National Monument, New Mexico](story:24); [Pinnacles National Monument, California](story:26)).

**Not the first cave, but the first cave alone.** The Park Service calls Jewel Cave "the first cave to ever be named a National Monument." That is not quite right. Three weeks earlier, on January 16, 1908, the Pinnacles proclamation had reserved rock formations "with a series of caves underlying them," and the Southwest's cliff dwellings sit in rock alcoves. What Jewel Cave can claim is being the first monument whose named object was the cave and nothing else. Others soon followed: Lewis and Clark Cavern in Montana on May 11, 1908, then Oregon Caves and Shoshone Cavern in 1909.

**How big.** The proclamation names no acreage. The boundary is carried entirely by the attached diagram, drawn by the Forest Service and labeled with the Department of Agriculture's name, a sign on the face of the document that Agriculture originated it. The diagram shows a rectangle straddling a township line, with Jasper Cave, Hill Spring, Jewel Cave, Prairie Dog Spring and Hell Canyon marked. Interior's 1911 report estimated the area at 1,280 acres, the same figure the *Custer Weekly Chronicle* had printed two weeks before the proclamation. The Park Service's acreage of record today is 1,273.51 acres.

## Historical context
**Wind from the rock.** In 1900 two local brothers, Frank and Albert Michaud, noticed air moving from a small hole in the limestone on the east side of Hell Canyon. Interior's 1911 report says they went in "in the hope of discovering some valuable mineral and the source of the wind." They enlarged the opening with hand tools and dynamite and, with their partner Charles Bush, found a passage lined with calcite crystals that sparkled in the lantern light. Bush was a co-owner, not just a family friend; Frank Michaud bought out his share in 1905 for $300.

On October 31, 1900, they filed the "Jewel Tunnel" mining claim at Custer. The claim was a means, not the goal. As the Park Service puts it, "although calcite crystals have little commercial value, the mining claim allowed the Michauds and Bush a relatively fast and easy way to control access to this natural wonder." They spent several years trying to draw paying visitors up the rough canyon.

**A game preserve with no game.** The tourist venture never paid, and because the cave lay on public land inside the Black Hills Forest Reserve — created by President Cleveland in February 1897 and enlarged by President McKinley in September 1898 — its future was a federal question. In 1906 the Michauds, with Bertha Cain, who had bought part of the claim, petitioned for a "Jewel Cave Game Reserve," hoping a federal preserve would bring visitors. Forest Service men came out and surveyed it, and turned it down: there was not enough game to preserve. But the cave itself was of scientific interest, they said, and they recommended a national monument instead.

Word reached Custer before the proclamation did. On January 25, 1908, the *Custer Weekly Chronicle* told readers the cave would be set apart as the "Jewel Cave National Monument" and that "the 'Game Preserve' which was advocated some time ago will not be established." It was an easy call. The ground was already federal, no rancher or logger was displaced, and reserving it cost almost nothing while keeping a natural curiosity out of private hands.

## Political impact
Like the other small monuments of 1907 and 1908, Jewel Cave drew no recorded controversy. It was remote, it displaced no established business, and it closed off no grazing or timber a Western delegation would fight for. No lawsuit and no adverse comment has been found.

That does not mean nobody noticed. Eleven South Dakota newspapers carried the news in 1908, most of them far from the Hills. The *Custer Weekly Chronicle* ran it three times, and the *Hot Springs Weekly Star* of February 21 gave it a headline: "ANOTHER GREAT CAVE. Jewel Cave, in Custer County, Made a National Monument." Papers in Madison, Pierre, Miller, Webster and Elk Point, hundreds of miles east, picked it up too. Beyond the state it traveled as wire filler from February into August 1908, through more than twenty out-of-state papers: Bismarck, Dickinson and Jamestown in North Dakota; Wyoming; Nebraska; Iowa; Wisconsin; and on east to Rhode Island, Pennsylvania, Maryland, the Carolinas and Savannah, and west to Oregon. For six months the country read about a great cave in the Black Hills that now belonged to the government, and nobody anywhere objected.

## Legacy
**Protection before exploration.** For its first half-century Jewel Cave was a lightly visited monument with a known length of a mile or two. Mining claims held up development into the 1910s; the Michaud claim, protected by the proclamation's clause for "prior valid adverse claims," was not bought out until 1928, when the Jewel Cave Corporation paid $750 for it. Administration passed from the Forest Service to the Park Service under Executive Order 6166, signed June 10, 1933 and effective August 10, 1933.

**The Conns.** The modern story begins in 1959, when three rock climbers, Herb and Jan Conn and Dwight Deal, began a systematic exploration. Deal returned in 1961 to write a master's thesis on the cave's geology at the University of Wyoming. Over roughly two decades the Conns surveyed some sixty-five miles of passage and laid out the routes that became the park's Scenic and Discovery tours. Herb Conn also worked out how to read the cave's breathing: he published "Barometric Wind in Wind and Jewel Caves, South Dakota" in the *Bulletin of the National Speleological Society* in April 1966.

**A new boundary.** In 1965 Congress redrew the boundary to cover the part of the cave then known. Public Law 89-250, approved October 9, 1965, worked by reference to a Park Service drawing and returned excluded lands to the Black Hills National Forest. It states no acreage.

**Still growing.** As of September 7, 2024, surveyors had mapped 220.33 miles (354.59 kilometers) of passage, making Jewel Cave the fifth-longest known cave in the world, behind Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, the Yucatán systems Ox Bel Ha and Sac Actun, and China's Shuanghedong. Barometric airflow suggests the mapped cave is a small fraction of the whole, and more than 55 percent of the known passage already lies outside the monument, under the national forest.

The story is a quiet one. Two brothers enlarged a hole with dynamite, filed a mining claim so they could charge admission, could not make it pay, asked the government for a game preserve, and were told there was no game, but that there was something else there worth keeping. What they had found turned out to be one of the great underground labyrinths of the planet, most of it still unmapped and lying outside the line drawn on the Forest Service's 1908 diagram.

## At a glance
Early in 1907 Congress moved to take away the president's power to create forest reserves in six Western states. Before signing that bill, Roosevelt and his Forester, Gifford Pinchot, rushed out thirty-three proclamations on March 1 and 2, adding about 15.6 million acres to the forest reserves. Only then, at 9:43 a.m. on March 4, did Roosevelt sign the law. Historians later nicknamed them the "midnight reserves."

## Background
On March 1 and 2, 1907, Theodore Roosevelt signed thirty-three forest-reserve proclamations, Proclamations 707 through 739: twelve on the first day and twenty-one on the second. The run begins with the Toiyabe reserve in Nevada and ends with Tillamook in Oregon. Most of them fell in the six states a new law was about to put off-limits: Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado and Wyoming. A few reached beyond them. Toiyabe is in Nevada, and Las Animas straddled Colorado and New Mexico.

By the titles on the documents, thirteen proclamations established or set apart a reserve and twenty enlarged or changed the boundaries of an existing one. The familiar "21 new reserves and 11 enlarged" does not match the documents, and even the Forest Service's own publications count the episode three different ways. The safest number is thirty-three proclamations.

The Forest Service's *Highlights in the History of Forest Conservation* adds up the land: "he signed 33 proclamations by which new reserves were created and areas added to already established reserves so that a total of 15,645,631 acres was added to the forest reserve system." The "sixteen million acres" heard so often is Roosevelt's own round number, from his 1913 *Autobiography*.

Legally, these reserves were the same as any other forest reserve. What set them apart was the timing, and the sheer volume packed into forty-eight hours.

A few from the stack:
- **Toiyabe, Nevada** (Proc. 707), the first of the run, outside the six restricted states.
- **Bear Lodge, Wyoming** (Proc. 716), a brand-new reserve in Wyoming's northeastern corner. See [Bear Lodge Forest Reserve, Wyoming](story:10).
- **Las Animas, Colorado and New Mexico** (Proc. 718), spanning two states.
- **Tillamook, Oregon** (Proc. 739), the last proclamation signed.

## Historical context
The president's power to set aside forests came from Section 24 of the Forest Reserve Act of 1891, the "Creative Act" signed by Benjamin Harrison. It let a president reserve forested public land by proclamation. Roosevelt used it more than anyone before him, and his reasons belonged to his era. He rarely talked about forests as wilderness. He talked about timber supply, monopoly and the nation's future. Pinchot's doctrine was "wise use": forests managed scientifically so the next generation would inherit more timber, not less. Behind it ran a Progressive fear that railroads, timber barons and "land grabbers" would corner the public domain unless the government held it in trust for everyone.

By 1907 the growing reserves had made enemies. Cattlemen, sheepmen, loggers, mining interests and homesteaders in the West chafed at the fees and rules of federal management, and their allies in Congress moved to shut the door. On February 23, Senator Charles W. Fulton of Oregon, a one-term Republican, offered an amendment from the Senate floor to the must-pass Agriculture Appropriations Act. The language was not entirely his own. Senator Weldon B. Heyburn of Idaho said on the spot that it was "almost in the exact language of a bill which I introduced, and which has been pending before the Committee on Public Lands since the first or second day of this session of Congress." Its key sentence read:

> *Provided further,* That hereafter no forest reserve shall be created, nor shall any additions be made to one heretofore created within the limits of the States of Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado, or Wyoming, except by Act of Congress.

The amendment did not ban new reserves outright. It handed the power to Congress.

The Senate adopted the amendment and passed the bill on February 25, the same day the House disagreed to the Senate's changes. Two conferences followed. The House agreed to the final report on March 2, the Senate on March 3, and the bill went to Roosevelt that day. Noon on March 4 marked the end of the 59th Congress, but it was not a deadline for his signature. The real pressure was that leaving the bill unsigned would have killed the entire Agriculture appropriation.

Pinchot's own account is the only first-hand description of the scramble:

> "At once I saw T.R. and got his enthusiastic consent to our plan. Thereupon we set every available man at work drawing proclamations for National Forests in those six states. We knew precisely what we wanted. Our field force had already gathered practically all the facts. Speedily it supplied the rest. Our office force worked straight through, some of them for thirty-six and even forty-eight hours on end, to finish the job. As usual our people were superb."

"As the Proclamations were completed," Pinchot wrote, "I took them over to the White House. T.R. signed them and sent them to the State Department for safekeeping… Then, and not until then, did T.R. sign the Agricultural Bill." Pinchot's title at the time was "Forester." "Chief Forester" came later.

The popular retelling adds clerks working around the clock, blue pencils on maps and a midnight deadline. Pinchot mentions none of that, and he never mentions maps at all.

Roosevelt gave his reason before the fact. A White House memorandum dated March 2, printed in the *Morning Oregonian* on March 5, 1907, said: "Failure on my part to sign these proclamations would mean that immense tracts of valuable timber would fall into the hands of the lumber syndicates because Congress has no opportunity to act, whereas the creation of the reserves means that this timber will be kept in the interest of the homemaker." He made the larger argument later that year, in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907. There he warned "that the country is unquestionably on the verge of a timber famine which will be felt in every household in the land," and wrote that "the conservation of our natural resources and their proper use constitute the fundamental problem which underlies almost every other problem of our National life."

## Political impact
Western opponents were furious, and they understood exactly what had happened. In a few days the president had put more land out of their reach than the ban would ever let back in. The *Morning Oregonian* ran the story on March 5, 1907, under the headline "SNATCHES FOREST FROM DESTROYER / President Reserves Land by Wholesale. / BEFORE POWER IS RECALLED." Its Washington bureau added that, "Being hastily made in order to circumvent the restrictions of the Fulton amendment, the reserves are loosely created," and predicted that much of the land would later be reopened to settlement. The paper's own tally, "Thirty-two Reserves of 17,000,000 Acres," was yet another version of the count.

Roosevelt was unrepentant, even delighted. He told the story at length in his *Autobiography* (1913), chapter XI:

> "But for four years the Forest Service had been gathering field notes as to what forests ought to be set aside in these States, and so was prepared to act. It was equally undesirable to veto the whole agricultural bill, and to sign it with this amendment effective. Accordingly, a plan to create the necessary National Forest in these States before the Agricultural Bill could be passed and signed was laid before me by Mr. Pinchot. I approved it. The necessary papers were immediately prepared. I signed the last proclamation a couple of days before, by my signature, the bill became law; and, when the friends of the special interests in the Senate got their amendment through and woke up, they discovered that sixteen million acres of timberland had been saved for the people by putting them in the National Forests before the land grabbers could get at them. The opponents of the Forest Service turned handsprings in their wrath; and dire were their threats against the Executive; but the threats could not be carried out, and were really only a tribute to the efficiency of our action."

His own telling, "a couple of days before," matches the documents. It was not a scramble against a same-day deadline. He calls them "the six Northwestern States," though Colorado is hardly northwestern.

The same act that took the power away also renamed what he had made. Tucked into the Forest Service's funding line was the phrase "forest reserves, which shall be known hereafter as national forests." From March 4, 1907, every reserve in the stack was a national forest.

The ban was narrower than it is often made to sound. It read "hereafter," so it bound Roosevelt only from 9:43 a.m. on March 4, and only in six states. Everywhere else the 1891 power was untouched, and he kept using it. At least a dozen more forest proclamations followed later in 1907, among them Chugach (Proc. 770) and Tongass (Proc. 772) in Alaska (see [The Forest Reserves of 1907](story:67)). By Roosevelt's own account the national forests grew by "more than forty-three million acres" that year, so the midnight reserves were roughly a third of one year's work. What ended on March 4, 1907, was one specific power in six specific states.

Critics saw a president defying the plain intent of Congress in the short window before a law took effect. Defenders saw a president using lawful authority, while he still had it, to save public land from interests that wanted it opened. The episode hardened both the conservation movement and its opponents. It also foreshadowed the split that would later set Roosevelt and Pinchot against President Taft in the Ballinger–Pinchot affair of 1909–1910.

## Legacy
The lands reserved in those two days are still part of the national forest system, scattered across the northern Rockies, the Cascades and the Colorado high country. The name "national forests," slipped into the 1907 funding line, is the one Americans still use.

The nickname came much later. "Midnight" does not appear in Pinchot's account or in Roosevelt's, and searches of Western newspapers from 1907 to 1912 have not turned it up. The earliest use found so far is Harold K. Steen's "the so-called midnight reserves," in *The U.S. Forest Service: A History* (1976). The same page gives the episode its best-known verdict: "Maybe so, but the setting aside of the so-called midnight reserves was really the last flamboyant act of the conservation movement." Those are Steen's words. "Turned handsprings in their wrath" is Roosevelt's, and the two are often run together.

The question the midnight reserves raised has never gone away: how far, and how fast, a president may go to protect public land against the wishes of Congress and local industry. The same argument runs through the fights over Antiquities Act monuments.

### Roosevelt's wider record, by the numbers
The midnight reserves are usually followed by a string of career totals. Several need care:
- **National forests.** About 150 national forests existed when Roosevelt left office, but many of the roughly two hundred establishing proclamations and orders he signed were consolidations, splits and renamings, chiefly in the July 1908 reorganization (see [The Great Reorganization of the National Forests, June 1908](story:99)). So "150 existed," not "150 created."
- **Acreage.** For 1909 the Forest Service gives 172.2 million acres of federally owned land, or 194,505,325 acres counting everything inside the boundaries. By Pinchot's figures the area inside boundaries rose from 62,354,965 acres in 1903 to 194,505,325 in 1909, more than threefold. The National Park Service puts the starting point at "41 forest reserves containing more than 46 million acres" by 1901. The popular "about 150 million acres" matches no Forest Service figure.
- **Birds and game.** The Fish and Wildlife Service says Roosevelt created 55 bird and game reservations by 1909: 51 bird reservations and four game preserves. The four are Wichita in Oklahoma (1905), Grand Canyon in Arizona (1906), the National Bison Range in Montana (1908) and Fire Island in Alaska (Executive Order 1038, February 27, 1909), a "Reservation" for the Alaska moose. Fire Island's reservation was revoked in 1921 and restored in 1925.
- **Monuments and parks.** Eighteen national monuments, from Devils Tower (September 24, 1906) to Mount Olympus (March 2, 1909). The National Park Service counts five national parks: Crater Lake, Wind Cave, Sullys Hill, Mesa Verde and Platt. Other counts run from four to six, because Platt renamed the existing Sulphur Springs Reservation and Sullys Hill became a game preserve in 1931.
- **Reclamation.** The Bureau of Reclamation speaks of "about 25 projects" authorized in 1903–1906 and "about 30" in 1902–1907, so "roughly two dozen" is the safe phrase.

## At a glance
The Constitution lets a president call Congress, or the Senate alone, into session "on extraordinary Occasions." Roosevelt did it twice in 1903. In March he summoned the Senate to act on the canal treaty with Colombia and a trade treaty with Cuba. In October he called both houses to meet in November, because the Cuban treaty could not take effect until all of Congress approved it.

## Background
Article II, Section 3 of the Constitution authorizes the President, "on extraordinary Occasions," to convene one or both houses. A Senate-only session can do only Senate business: confirming nominations and giving advice and consent to treaties, which it takes up in closed "executive session."

Proclamation 498, signed March 2, 1903, called the Senate of the new 58th Congress to meet at noon on March 5, the day after the old Congress expired. Calls like this were customary in inauguration years, but no president was being inaugurated in 1903; Roosevelt was halfway through the term he had inherited from McKinley. This call had a stated purpose. His message to the Senate of March 5, 1903 opens: "I have called the Senate in extraordinary session to consider the treaties concerning which it proved impossible to take action during the session of Congress just ended." The two treaties were the canal treaty with Colombia, signed January 22, 1903 (the Hay–Herrán treaty), and the commercial reciprocity convention with Cuba.

Proclamation 506, signed October 20, 1903, called *both* houses for a legislative purpose. It recites that a Senate resolution of March 19, 1903 required "the approval by Congress" before the Cuban convention, signed at Havana on December 11, 1902, could take effect. It therefore summoned the full 58th Congress to meet November 9, "to the end that they may consider and determine whether the approval of the Congress shall be given to the said Convention." Without the call, the new Congress would not have met until its regular first Monday in December.

## Historical context
The March call grew out of a failed winter. Both treaties had stalled in the short session that ran out on March 4. In his March 5 message Roosevelt called them treaties of "great and far-reaching importance" and said that "the urgent need for their adoption require[s] me to impose upon you the inconvenience of meeting at this time."

The Senate sat in executive session from March 5 to March 19. On March 17 it consented to the Colombian canal treaty without a single change, by a vote the *Morning Oregonian* reported as 73 to 5. The Cuban convention took longer. After days of amendment fights, the Senate consented on March 19, 50 to 16. Beet-sugar senators had won amendments guarding against further cuts in sugar duties; the remaining opposition came chiefly from the Southern cane-sugar states. Louisiana's Murphy J. Foster spoke for about two and a half hours against the treaty, warning of ruin for his state's growers. One amendment provided that the convention "shall not take effect until the same shall have been approved by the Congress."

That condition was not unusual. Tariff treaties touch revenue, and the House had long insisted on a voice in them. The Hawaiian reciprocity convention of 1875, for one, would not take effect "until a law to carry it into operation shall have been passed by the Congress of the United States of America." The 1903 amendment followed that precedent, and it is the reason Roosevelt had to convene the whole Congress in November.

Cuban reciprocity was one of the long fights of Roosevelt's first term. He argued it as a matter of honor as much as trade. His message to the special session, dated November 10, 1903, called the necessary legislation "demanded not only by our interest, but by our honor," and warned: "A failure to enact such legislation would come perilously near a repudiation of the pledged faith of the Nation." He tied Cuba's special position to the Platt Amendment and to the naval stations Cuba had conceded. Against him stood beet-sugar growers in the West, cane planters in Louisiana, protectionist Republicans wary of any crack in the tariff wall, and many Democrats, who wanted general tariff reduction rather than a single bargain. Tennessee's Edward Carmack, opening the Senate debate in November, argued that only general tariff revision would bring the relief needed.

## Political impact
Neither call slipped by unnoticed. The *Morning Oregonian* of March 3, 1903 ran the Senate call on page one under "EXTRA SESSION CALLED," printing the proclamation in full. On October 21 it headlined "EXTRA SESSION / President Calls Congress to Meet November 9," again with the full text. Both were wire stories.

The November session was a real political fight, and Roosevelt did not get everything he asked for within it. Only the House finished its work. On November 11 the Republican majority carried a special rule fixing the time of the vote, 183 to 160, with six Republicans from Michigan and Colorado joining the Democrats against it. Democrats under John Sharp Williams of Mississippi pressed amendments to the last and lost every one. On November 19 the House passed the bill by a rising vote of 335 to 21. Because it was a count by division, not a roll call, no member's vote was recorded by name; as the *Los Angeles Herald* put it, "There Was No Record Vote."

The Senate made the bill its unfinished business on November 25, then talked it past the end of the special session, which merged into the regular session at noon on December 7. The Senate passed the bill on December 16, 57 to 18. The yeas were 48 Republicans and 9 Democrats; the nays were 17 Democrats and one Republican, Thomas Bard of beet-growing California. Roosevelt signed it December 17, 1903. Because the special session had not finished the job, it became chapter 1, Public No. 1, of the regular second session.

## Legacy
Proclamation 498 opened the door to more than calendar business. The session it called gave American consent to the Hay–Herrán canal treaty. Colombia's Senate rejected that treaty on August 12, 1903, and the Panama crisis of that November followed.

Proclamation 506 belongs to the history of the Cuban Reciprocity Treaty. Roosevelt proclaimed the convention on December 17, 1903, and it took effect on December 27, 1903. The treaty bound the Cuban and American economies, above all their sugar trades, closely together for the next three decades. For other Cuba proclamations of these years, see [Suspending Duties on Cuban Vessels](story:38) and [Extending Copyright Protection to Citizens of Cuba](story:43).

The power behind both proclamations, to convene Congress on extraordinary occasions, remains in the Constitution. Special sessions of this kind grew rare in the twentieth century as Congress came to sit for most of the year.

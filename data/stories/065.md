## At a glance
In 1906 Roosevelt signed seven proclamations touching Indian reservation land. Three opened large "surplus" tracts to settlers: on the Crow Reservation in Montana, at Wind River in Wyoming, and in Oklahoma's "Big Pasture." A fourth opened land at Walker River in Nevada. The other three were smaller: a sale of gilsonite land in Utah, a seven-week move of a land office, and the return of 8.80 acres to the public domain to fix one homesteader's paperwork. The big openings drew thousands to the lotteries, and then almost no one filed.

## Background
These seven proclamations sit together because they all deal with reservation land in a single year, not because they do the same thing. Where a proclamation is a true opening, it rests on an earlier act of Congress that ratified an agreement with the tribe (or, in the harder cases, did without one) to allot land to tribal members and declare the rest "surplus." The proclamation was the final switch.

**Crow, Montana (Proclamation 625, May 24).** The unallotted ceded lands of the Crow Reservation opened to homestead, townsite and mineral entry on and after July 16, 1906. The cession was the agreement of August 14, 1899, which sat unratified for five years until the Act of April 27, 1904 confirmed it. The ceded strip ran 1,137,500 acres, of which 969,656.58 were unallotted and actually opened. Registration ran June 14–28 at Miles City and Billings, Montana, and Sheridan, Wyoming, with the drawing at Billings on July 2, 3 and 5. Land cost $4.00 an acre: a dollar down and the rest in four yearly payments. It did not sell. Of 16,923 who registered and 6,500 who drew the right to enter, 263 had filed by the end of August. After a full year there were only 398 entries covering 56,320.75 acres, under six percent of the land opened. In 1912 President Taft cut the floor price to $1.50 and ordered an auction.

**Wind River, Wyoming (Proclamation 631, June 2).** This did the same for Shoshone and Arapaho land ceded under the McLaughlin Agreement of April 21, 1904 and ratified by the Act of March 3, 1905. The tract ran 1,472,844.15 acres. It is usually described as lying north of the Wind River, but the proclamation actually defines the *retained* reservation as a polygon bounded by the Big Wind River, the Big Popo-Agie, the North Fork of the Big Popo-Agie and the reservation's south and west lines, so the cession also took in the southeast corner. Land cost $1.50 an acre for the first two years, then $1.25 and lower still in later steps. It failed as completely as the Crow opening: 10,559 registered, 7,240 drew rights, and after a year 635 entries covering 94,383.31 acres had been filed.

**Shoshoni, Wyoming (Proclamation 640, July 20).** A companion order six weeks later simply directed that entries on the Shoshone Reservation be made at the land office in Shoshoni, and only from August 15 through October 3, 1906. After that, filings went back to Lander. Pure administrative plumbing.

**Uncompahgre, Utah (Proclamation 632, June 6).** This one opened no reservation. The Uncompahgre lands had opened back on April 1, 1898, under an 1897 act that held back land bearing gilsonite, asphaltum, elaterite "or other like substances." Under an act of March 3, 1903, Proclamation 632 offered the even-numbered sections of those withheld lands for sale on sealed bids at the Vernal land office, in tracts of forty acres or less, with bids to be opened at one o'clock on September 15, 1906. The odd-numbered sections and all the minerals were "specifically reserved for future action of Congress." In short, it was a gilsonite sale, and it states no acreage.

**Uinta, Utah (Proclamation 648, September 1).** Smaller still. A homesteader named David Eskelson had been "erroneously allowed" on September 26, 1905 to include two lots of Section 10, Township 2 South, Range 1 East of the Uintah Special Meridian in his homestead entry. He had built valuable improvements on them, and the lots, "containing 8.80 acres," were cut off from the rest of the tribes' grazing reserve by the Raven Mining Company's claims and so were "of no value as a part of said reserve." Roosevelt restored them to the public domain. That is the whole document. It did not open the Uintah grazing reserve, which the tribes still held in common. (Later catalogs title it "Grazing Site Lands," which suggests far more than it did.)

**Kiowa, Comanche and Apache, Oklahoma (Proclamation 653, September 19).** This covered 480,000 acres of grazing land plus a 25,000-acre wood reserve in Oklahoma Territory: the "Big Pasture," in what are now Comanche, Cotton and Tillman counties, named for the 1880s leases to Texas cattlemen like Waggoner and Burnett. McKinley's proclamation of July 4, 1901 had held it back from that year's great opening, and the Act of June 5, 1906 ordered it sold. Notice what this proclamation does *not* do: it sets no date. The land would open "at such time and in such manner as the said Secretary of the Interior may fix and prescribe." Interior chose sealed bids at the Lawton land office, with a floor of $800 a quarter section, or $5.00 an acre. Over six days in December 1906, 7,621 bids came in, totaling $2,286,300. They were opened and awarded beginning March 15, 1907.

The proclamation carries a curiosity. It cites the Kiowa-Comanche-Apache grazing provision as part of "An Act to ratify and confirm an agreement with the Indians of the Fort Hall Indian Reservation, in Idaho." That is not a slip. The Act of June 6, 1900 really was a Fort Hall ratification act, and Congress attached the Kiowa-Comanche-Apache agreement (the 1892 Jerome Agreement) to it as section 6. One law, two agreements.

**Walker River, Nevada (Proclamation 659, September 26).** This opened the Walker River Reservation lands that had not been allotted in twenty-acre parcels or set apart as common grazing or timber land or as agency, school, cemetery and church reserves. It rested on the Indian appropriation act of May 27, 1902, and the cession agreement was signed at Schurz on July 20, 1906 by 108 tribal members. The land opened at noon, Pacific standard time, Monday, October 29, 1906. The reservation is Northern Paiute, home to the Aga'idökadö and Pakwidökadö ("Pah Ute" in the proclamation's spelling), and it runs along the lower Walker River north of Walker Lake. How many acres were opened is not known.

## Historical context
These openings were the local machinery of the allotment era. The General Allotment (Dawes) Act of 1887 made it federal policy to break up communally held reservations and give each Indian family a farm. Its architects sincerely believed the program would turn tribal members into self-supporting farmers, and it would also free enormous acreage for a land-hungry West. The Dawes Act did not itself open the surplus. It let the Secretary of the Interior negotiate with a tribe "for the purchase and release" of unallotted land, but "such purchase shall not be complete until ratified by Congress." Every cession needed its own ratifying act, which is why each of these proclamations cites a different statute and none cites the Dawes Act. The 1887 law also exempted the Five Tribes, the Osage and several other nations outright.

By 1906 the routine was settled: a commission negotiated a cession (or Congress simply legislated one), Congress ratified it, and a presidential proclamation set the date. The pressure behind each opening was intensely local and economic. Around Wind River, the promise of a federal irrigation project drew settlers. Riverton was born of that opening: platted by the Wyoming Central Irrigation Company, its lots drawn by lottery, and opened August 15, 1906, the same day as the land. Shoshoni was already there, laid out in 1904 at the railhead by a Chicago & North Western subsidiary and grown to a tent city of some two thousand by the summer of 1905. The Crow opening was meant to fill the Billings country, and the Big Pasture sale answered Oklahoma cattlemen and would-be homesteaders.

What let these transfers happen without much national argument was an assumption almost universal among white policymakers of the day: reservation land not under the plow was land going to waste. The tribes bore the cost. They lost the larger share of what remained of their land. At Wind River that came to about two-thirds of what was left of a reservation of nearly 2.3 million acres, often for prices and irrigation promises that proved illusory. The reservations that survived became checkerboards of Indian allotments, fee patents and settler homesteads.

## Political impact
As national news these proclamations drew almost no notice. Locally, they were the biggest thing to happen in years. An opening meant a land rush, with registration booths, a lottery drawing and a boom town overnight, and the Western press covered it as opportunity, not dispossession.

Expectations ran far ahead of results. Both big 1906 lotteries drew thousands of registrants and then almost no takers. After a year settlers had filed on under six percent of the Crow land and about six percent of the Wind River land, and both openings were later repriced. Whatever these proclamations cost the tribes, they did not buy the settlement boom the promoters advertised.

In Congress the openings were by now a settled, bipartisan policy. Where there was debate, it was over price, the size of the irrigation appropriation, and which land office would take entries, not whether to open the land at all. Native opposition was real: tribes resisted cessions, contested acreage and payment, and in some cases, as with the Ute openings ([The Uintah Indian Reservation Openings of 1905](story:56)), never consented at all. But it had little hold on the politics of the moment. The President's signature was the last formality on decisions already made.

## Legacy
The reservations these proclamations touched were left diminished and checkerboarded, and the central legal question — did a surplus-land act merely open a reservation, or actually shrink it? — has produced a long line of Supreme Court cases turning on the exact words of statutes like these.

The Wind River cession was at the heart of a twenty-first-century boundary fight over the very act behind Proclamation 631. In December 2013 the EPA approved the Eastern Shoshone and Northern Arapaho tribes' application to be treated as a state under the Clean Air Act, on the view that the 1905 act had not shrunk the reservation. That would have put the city of Riverton inside Indian country. The Tenth Circuit disagreed. *Wyoming v. EPA* (2017) opens: "This case requires us to determine whether Congress diminished the boundaries of the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming in 1905. We find that it did." The Supreme Court declined review in June 2018, and the EPA revised its approval in 2019. Riverton is not in Indian country.

The Ute openings produced *Hagen v. Utah* (1994), which held the Uintah Reservation diminished under the Indian appropriation act of May 27, 1902. Roosevelt appears in the opinion himself: the Court quoted his July 14, 1905 proclamation opening the Uintah lands as an "unambiguous, contemporaneous, statement, by the Nation's Chief Executive," and noted that the largest town in the opened country is Roosevelt, Utah, "named for the President who opened the Reservation for settlement."

*McGirt v. Oklahoma* (2020) held that only Congress, in plain terms, can disestablish a reservation. *McGirt* concerned the Muscogee (Creek) Reservation and addressed none of these proclamations, and it has since been narrowed by *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta* (2022). It did not help the Kiowa, Comanche and Apache: in *Martinez v. State* (2021), Oklahoma's Court of Criminal Appeals held that Congress had disestablished their reservation in the Act of June 6, 1900, pointing to its "cede, convey, transfer, relinquish, and surrender, forever and absolutely" language and its two-million-dollar payment.

Unsold Wind River land was ordered restored to the tribes by the Act of July 27, 1939, carried out in 1940 and 1944, and those and later restorations in 1953 helped carry the reservation back past 2.2 million acres. At Walker River, an act of June 22, 1936 let the Secretary of the Interior restore public-domain land to the reservation, and part of the 1906 lands came back. Riverton remains, and so does Shoshoni, which was there first. So do the tribes, on reservations that were narrowed but not erased.

## At a glance
Mixed in with the June 1908 forest orders were eight small administrative ones. Most let a named person be hired or brought back into government service without the usual civil-service rules. One, Executive Order 818, cleared the way for the Treasury to staff up quickly under the emergency-currency law Congress had passed after the Panic of 1907. Another let yachts of the Royal Vancouver Yacht Club visit American waters without the usual entry, clearance or charges.

## Background
The eight orders are the non-forest remainder of a crowded two weeks. The forest orders of June 18 and June 25 fall in the middle of the number range ([The Great Reorganization of the National Forests, June 1908](story:99)). The orders carried no captions of their own, and their "titles" are later compilers' headings.

**The currency law.** Executive Order 818 (June 15) is short enough to quote whole:

> The requirements under Civil Service Rule VIII regarding temporary appointments, as amended by executive order of January 27, 1908, are hereby waived as to temporary appointments in the Treasury Department under the act of Congress approved May 30, 1908, "To amend the national banking laws." Temporary appointments under the act above referred to may be made by the Secretary of the Treasury without regard to civil-service rules for such time as may be necessary to carry into effect the provisions of the act mentioned.
>
> THEODORE ROOSEVELT.
> THE WHITE HOUSE, *June 15, 1908.*

The order names the statute by its real title, "To amend the national banking laws." The catalog heading's "Banking Act of 1908" is a later label that period sources did not use; they called it the Aldrich–Vreeland bill, the emergency currency bill or simply the currency bill. The waiver targets one rule, Civil Service Rule VIII on temporary appointments, as Roosevelt had amended it earlier that year in Executive Order 741.

**A two-word change.** Executive Order 832 (June 25) amended the list of positions excepted from examination. For the New York customs district, "One paymaster; stitch counters" became "Two paymasters; stitch counters." That was the whole order: the district could have a second paymaster outside the examined service. It amended Executive Order 209 of March 20, 1903, and was itself revoked in 1911.

**One person at a time.**
- **Executive Order 819 (June 15)** let Andrew Christensen, "a former employee of the classified service," be appointed a special agent of the General Land Office. Christensen had already passed the examination; what the order waived was "the limitations of the reinstatement and transfer rules," the time limits that otherwise kept a former employee from coming back.
- **Executive Order 819½ (June 18)** allowed the appointment of John McCarthy, a retired U.S. Army commissary sergeant, as laborer in charge of warehouse for the Subsistence Department at New York, "by reason of the unusual experience and training required by the position and possessed by Mr. McCarthy."
- **Executive Order 828 (June 19)** let Frank E. Elder be reinstated in the Government Printing Office "without regard to the fact that he has been separated from that office for more than a year."
- **Executive Order 830 (June 24)** authorized the appointment of Basil Miles as superintendent of the division of foreign mails in the Post Office Department without examination. Miles (1877–1928) had been third secretary of embassy at St. Petersburg and Berlin; he ran the foreign mail service until 1913 and then returned to the State Department.

**Language officers in East Asia.** Executive Order 829 (June 20) concerned the posts of assistant Japanese and assistant Chinese secretary at the embassy in Tokyo (spelled "Tokio" at the time) and the legation at Peking. These were the working language officers of the American missions in East Asia. The catalog title says the posts were "to be filled in rotation," but the order's text has not been found, and the State Department's own records describe an examination-and-promotion ladder, not a rotation. The phrase may be the compiler's, not the order's.

**Yachts from Vancouver.** Executive Order 831 (June 25) authorized yachts of the Royal Vancouver Yacht Club to visit United States waters without entry, clearance or charges. Congress had given the President power to extend that courtesy, where other nations returned it, in an act of May 28, 1908. Roosevelt used the new authority twenty-eight days later.

## Historical context
The law behind Executive Order 818 is the one history books call the Aldrich–Vreeland Act, approved May 30, 1908. It was Congress's direct answer to the Panic of 1907, the bank runs and credit freeze of the previous autumn. Groups of ten or more national banks could form "national currency associations." When the Secretary of the Treasury found that "business conditions in the locality demand additional circulation," they could issue notes backed by securities and commercial paper, up to a national limit of five hundred million dollars. A stiff tax, five percent a year in the first month and rising a point a month to ten, made sure the money would be used only in an emergency and retired as soon as it passed. The act also created the National Monetary Commission, nine Senators and nine Representatives, to report "what changes are necessary or desirable in the monetary system of the United States or in the laws relating to banking and currency."

The signing was a late-night affair. May 30, 1908 was a Saturday and Decoration Day. The Senate had sat all night, and the conference report passed 43 to 22 that afternoon after a filibuster collapsed around half past four. Roosevelt came to the Capitol at nine that evening and signed the bill in the President's Room shortly before ten, not at the White House, handing the pen to Representative Wilson of Illinois. Congress adjourned at 11:50 that night. Executive Order 818, sixteen days later, supplied the staff needed to put the law to work.

The other orders show the everyday machinery of a federal government that had grown large under a merit system while still letting the President make exceptions for named appointments. The Tokyo and Peking order belongs to the slow professionalization of the diplomatic and consular services (see [The Executive Orders of Early 1905](story:85) and [The Executive Orders of Late 1905](story:86)). Under an executive order of June 27, 1906, student interpreters were chosen by examination and attached to the missions at Tokyo, Peking or Constantinople. They studied the language for at least two years under the mission's language secretary. Passing the second examination, according to the *Register of the Department of State*, "shall indicate the student as qualified for the post of Assistant Japanese or Assistant Chinese Secretary." The first men into those posts followed right after Executive Order 829: Charles Jonathan Arnell as assistant Japanese secretary at Tokyo on June 25, 1908, and Willys Ruggles Peck as assistant Chinese secretary at Peking on November 9, 1908.

## Political impact
Almost none of these orders drew public notice. Apart from the currency law standing behind Executive Order 818, they were routine actions of the kind issued by the dozen. The one exception found is the yacht order. On July 4, 1908, the *San Juan Islander* reported: "An order has been issued by President Roosevelt, in conformity with an act passed on the 28th of May, by which yachts of the Royal Vancouver Yacht Club are permitted to visit Puget Sound ports without entry or clearance or payment of any charges." Within two weeks a Royal Vancouver skipper won the deciding race of the Alexandra Cup series from the Seattle defender *Spirit*. The timing fits, but the paper tied the order to the general reciprocity law, not to any regatta, and no record has been found linking the two.

## Legacy
### The emergency currency
The order with the longest afterlife only touched staffing. The Aldrich–Vreeland emergency currency was issued just once. In the words of the Comptroller of the Currency's report for 1915, "Between the crises of 1907 and 1914 there was no unusual demand for currency, hence there were no issues of national-bank currency on other security than United States bonds." When the demand came, at the outbreak of the First World War, the act was days from expiring, and Congress had already extended it to June 30, 1915 in section 27 of the Federal Reserve Act. The first notes went out August 4, 1914 and the last in February 1915: $382,502,645 issued to 1,363 banks in forty-one states and the District of Columbia. At the peak, on October 24, 1914, $363,632,080 was outstanding, and all of it was retired without loss by December 1915.

### The National Monetary Commission
The commission created by the same law spent three and a half years commissioning studies of the banking systems of Britain, France, Germany, Canada and a dozen other countries and holding hearings in thirteen American cities. It reported on January 8, 1912 with a draft law for a "National Reserve Association," the Aldrich Plan, and was wound up by statute the following March. The Federal Reserve Act followed on December 23, 1913. Executive Order 818 itself was housekeeping for the 1908 law and had no life of its own.

### The rest
The single-person orders (819, 819½, 828 and 830) left no mark beyond the people they named. The New York paymaster order was revoked by Executive Order 1371 of June 12, 1911. The yacht-club courtesy rested on a law that lasted much longer than the order: the reciprocity provision of the May 28, 1908 act was later codified and remained the basis for excusing foreign yacht-club vessels from entry and clearance.

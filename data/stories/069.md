## At a glance
In 1907 Roosevelt signed two short proclamations that carried American commercial policy abroad without a vote in Congress. Proclamation 762 (June 1) lowered duties on German brandies, wines, champagne, crude tartar, and paintings and statuary in return for concessions from Germany under a temporary trade agreement. Proclamation 774 (September 20) found that Austria protected American authors and opened American copyright to "the subjects of Austria." The German deal made headlines and drew organized protest; the Austrian finding passed almost unnoticed.

## Background
Both proclamations used authority Congress had already handed the President.

**Germany.** Proclamation 762 announced a reciprocal commercial arrangement with the German Empire under the third section of the Dingley Tariff Act of July 24, 1897. That section let the President lower duties on a short list of goods in return for concessions abroad that he judged "reciprocal and equivalent." It rested on a temporary Commercial Agreement, signed for the United States by Secretary of State Elihu Root at Washington on April 22, 1907, and for Germany by Ambassador Baron Speck von Sternburg at Levico on May 2. The agreement replaced the stopgap of Proclamation 615 ([The Reciprocity Proclamations of 1906](story:64)), which ran only until June 30, 1907.

The new arrangement was not a final settlement. The agreement calls itself temporary, made "until a comprehensive commercial treaty can be agreed upon." It ran from July 1, 1907 to June 30, 1908, and after that only until six months after either side gave notice to end it.

The American side paid in two currencies:
- **Lower duties.** The proclamation set the section-3 rates on argols (crude tartar), brandies, still wines and vermuth, and paintings and statuary, as in 1906, and added champagne and all other sparkling wines, which no 1906 reciprocity proclamation had covered. The new rates took effect July 1, 1907.
- **Changes in customs procedure.** Root's note of April 22 to the German ambassador, made "a part of the consideration" of the agreement, promised that "market value" would be read as the export price where goods were sold only for export; that consuls would stop demanding certain sworn statements and personal appearances; that reappraisement hearings would be open to the importer; and that certificates of value from German chambers of commerce would be accepted as evidence. Roosevelt amended the Consular Regulations to match on June 1, the same day as the proclamation.

In return, Germany granted American products the rates on a list attached to the agreement. The German official gazette printed that list, selected numbers of the German general tariff of 1902 running from rye and wheat to musical boxes, and called them the "conventional" rates. The list did not cover everything, and the State Department said so. A wire summary of its statement reported that "about forty articles of American production" would get the minimum rates, that "regret is expressed that it has not been possible, owing to the attitude of the German government, to secure the minimum tariff for all American productions," and that "95.7 per cent" of American exports to Germany would keep most-favored-nation treatment. Germany extended its conventional rates to most, but not all, American goods.

**Austria.** Proclamation 774 found, on "satisfactory official assurances," that Austrian law gave American citizens copyright "on substantially the same basis" as its own citizens. On that finding it declared the first condition of section 13 of the International Copyright ("Chace") Act of March 3, 1891 "now fulfilled in respect to the subjects of Austria." It was the same finding Roosevelt had made for Cuba ([Extending Copyright Protection to Citizens of Cuba](story:43)) and Norway ([The Reciprocity Proclamations of 1905](story:61)). The proclamation names Austria alone. It does not mention Hungary, which kept its own copyright law within the Dual Monarchy.

Austria had prepared the ground. Its law of February 26, 1907 let the government extend its 1895 copyright law to foreigners on proof of reciprocity. The American proclamation came first, on Austrian assurances. Eleven weeks later, on December 9, 1907, the Austrian Minister of Justice issued an ordinance on copyright protection in relation to the United States. It recited section 13 of the 1891 act and Roosevelt's proclamation, and declared that the 1895 law now applied to works of American citizens not published in Austria, so long as they were protected in the United States.

## Historical context
Section 3 of the Dingley Act was the reciprocity tool the executive could use alone. It allowed narrow tariff bargaining, not broad free trade. The larger measures of the period took Congress: the Cuban reciprocity treaty needed the Senate and an act of Congress in December 1903, and the broader treaties allowed by section 4 of the same act, the Kasson treaties, were never ratified by the Senate. Section 3 was the part of the system that actually worked without Congress, and it was always small.

With Germany the stakes were real. Germany's tariff law of March 1, 1906 reserved its lower conventional rates for treaty partners, and the United States was not one. The 1906 bridge had kept American goods off Germany's higher general rates for sixteen months. The press saw danger: the San Diego *Union and Daily Bee* of June 2, 1907 headed its wire story on the proclamation "Commercial Pact with Germany Is Made — Tariff War With Kaiser Is Averted." The 1907 agreement was the next step, still temporary, and paid for as much in customs procedure as in duty rates. Those procedural concessions reached beyond Germany: the State Department's 1907 correspondence has a separate chapter on applying them to Austria-Hungary, France, Great Britain, Italy, and Switzerland.

International copyright moved gradually too. The first section-13 proclamation, of July 1, 1891, took in Belgium, France, Great Britain and the British possessions, and Switzerland together. After that, proclamations came mostly one country at a time as each government met the Chace Act's reciprocity test. Austria was the sixteenth country brought in this way, and the third under Roosevelt, after Cuba (1903) and Norway (1905). The United States stood outside the multilateral Berne Convention, so findings like this one were how an Austrian author gained protection in American courts.

Protection under the Chace Act had a price. For books, photographs, chromos, and lithographs, the 1891 act required printing from type set or plates made within the United States. That "manufacturing clause" was what American printers and typographers had won for supporting the act, and it limited what a foreign author actually gained.

## Political impact
The Austrian finding drew no notice that can be found. The German agreement was wire news, and it drew organized opposition from protectionist manufacturers. California papers alone carried dozens of stories on "the German tariff agreement" between April and July 1907.

On July 9 the Santa Barbara *Morning Press* printed a New York wire story headed "Americans Plan War Upon German Tariff Pact." Citing the *Journal of Commerce*, it reported that "domestic manufacturers, largely under the leadership of the American Protective Tariff association," whose secretary was William M. Wakeman, "former appraiser of the port," were "to conduct a systematic campaign against the German tariff agreement." It added that "several manufacturers' associations throughout the country have had meetings and have drafted resolutions of protest," and that the main effort would go into "bringing pressure to bear on congressional representatives." Other headlines ran the same way, "Manufacturers Will Fight German Tariff" (San Jose *Mercury-News*, July 10) and "Tariff League to Fight" (Colusa *Daily Sun*, July 1), while on the other side came "New York Merchants Approve German Treaty" (*Morning Press*, July 21).

**Champagne.** One quarrel came from abroad. On July 7, 1907 the French chargé d'affaires protested that the agreement listed "champagne" among "the products of the soil and industry of Germany." The word, he wrote, "can not but be considered regrettable" and would favor "frauds as to indications of origin," and he invoked the misbranding sections of the Pure Food and Drugs Act of 1906. There was money in the complaint too: German sparkling wines paid the reduced rate from July 1, 1907, while French champagne did not receive it until the Franco-American agreement of January 28, 1908 (Proclamation 797, [The Residual 1908 Proclamations](story:107)).

Root answered on September 24. The agreement's wording, he wrote, "necessarily follows the language" of section 3 of the Dingley Act, which reads "Champagne and all other sparkling wines," and "it may not be correct to say that champagne, eo nomine, is a German product." He passed on the view of the Secretary of Agriculture, who enforced the pure-food law: "the term 'champagne' should be restricted to the wine which is produced in the champagne district in France," and any other use of it on a label was misbranding. It was an early fight over a place name on a bottle, riding along on a tariff schedule.

## Legacy
The section-3 reciprocity system ended with the Payne-Aldrich Tariff of August 5, 1909. That act set up a maximum-and-minimum tariff and directed the President to give notice, within ten days, to every country holding a section-3 agreement; on termination, their goods would pay the new law's rates. The German agreement of 1907 was ended this way. Broad reciprocal-trade authority did not return until the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934.

The Austrian finding outlasted the Chace Act. The Copyright Act of March 4, 1909 replaced the 1891 law, and a proclamation by President Taft of April 9, 1910 re-extended American copyright to Austria and the other countries already covered, effective July 1, 1909. Hungary came under American copyright by a separate convention, proclaimed in 1912. On the Austrian side, the Justice Minister's ordinance of December 9, 1907 outlived the monarchy that issued it: Austria's Copyright Act of 1936 kept it in force, and it was not repealed until the end of 2018.

Country-by-country arrangements remained the American method for eighty years. The United States joined the Berne Convention, effective March 1, 1989, under the Berne Convention Implementation Act of 1988.

## At a glance
In 1907 Roosevelt signed two short proclamations that carried American commercial policy abroad without a vote in Congress. Proclamation 762 (June 1) lowered duties on German brandies, wines, champagne, crude tartar, and paintings and statuary in return for concessions from Germany under a temporary trade agreement. Proclamation 774 (September 20) found that Austria protected American authors and opened American copyright to "the subjects of Austria." The German deal made headlines and drew organized protest; the Austrian finding passed almost unnoticed.

## Background
Both proclamations used authority Congress had already handed the President.

**Germany.** Proclamation 762 announced a reciprocal commercial arrangement with the German Empire under the third section of the Dingley Tariff Act of July 24, 1897. That section let the President lower duties on a short list of goods in return for concessions abroad that he judged "reciprocal and equivalent." It rested on a temporary Commercial Agreement, signed for the United States by Secretary of State Elihu Root at Washington on April 22, 1907, and for Germany by Ambassador Baron Speck von Sternburg at Levico on May 2. The agreement replaced the stopgap of Proclamation 615 ([The Reciprocity Proclamations of 1906](story:64)), which ran only until June 30, 1907.

The new arrangement was not a final settlement. The agreement calls itself temporary, made "until a comprehensive commercial treaty can be agreed upon." It ran from July 1, 1907 to June 30, 1908, and after that only until six months after either side gave notice to end it.

The American side paid in two currencies:
- **Lower duties.** The proclamation set the section-3 rates on argols (crude tartar), brandies, still wines and vermuth, and paintings and statuary, as in 1906, and added champagne and all other sparkling wines, which no 1906 reciprocity proclamation had covered. The new rates took effect July 1, 1907.
- **Changes in customs procedure.** Root's note of April 22 to the German ambassador, made "a part of the consideration" of the agreement, promised that "market value" would be read as the export price where goods were sold only for export; that consuls would stop demanding certain sworn statements and personal appearances; that reappraisement hearings would be open to the importer; and that certificates of value from German chambers of commerce would be accepted as evidence. Roosevelt amended the Consular Regulations to match on June 1, the same day as the proclamation.

In return, Germany granted American products the rates on a list attached to the agreement. The German official gazette printed that list, selected numbers of the German general tariff of 1902 running from rye and wheat to musical boxes, and called them the "conventional" rates. The list did not cover everything, and the State Department said so. A wire summary of its statement reported that about forty American products would get the minimum rates, that the Department regretted that the German government's position had kept it from winning the minimum tariff for every American product, and that 95.7 percent of American exports to Germany would keep most-favored-nation treatment. Germany extended its conventional rates to most, but not all, American goods.

**Austria.** Proclamation 774 found, on "satisfactory official assurances," that Austrian law gave American citizens copyright "on substantially the same basis" as its own citizens. On that finding it declared the first condition of section 13 of the International Copyright ("Chace") Act of March 3, 1891 "now fulfilled in respect to the subjects of Austria." It was the same finding Roosevelt had made for Cuba ([Extending Copyright Protection to Citizens of Cuba](story:43)) and Norway ([The Reciprocity Proclamations of 1905](story:61)). The proclamation names Austria alone. It does not mention Hungary, which kept its own copyright law within the Dual Monarchy.

Austria had prepared the ground. Its law of February 26, 1907 let the government extend its 1895 copyright law to foreigners on proof of reciprocity. The American proclamation came first, on Austrian assurances. Eleven weeks later, on December 9, 1907, the Austrian Minister of Justice issued an ordinance on copyright protection in relation to the United States. It recited section 13 of the 1891 act and Roosevelt's proclamation, and declared that the 1895 law now applied to works of American citizens not published in Austria, so long as they were protected in the United States.

## Historical context
Section 3 of the Dingley Act was the reciprocity tool the executive could use alone. It allowed narrow tariff bargaining, not broad free trade. The larger measures of the period took Congress: the Cuban reciprocity treaty needed the Senate and an act of Congress in December 1903, and the broader treaties allowed by section 4 of the same act, the Kasson treaties, were never ratified by the Senate. Section 3 was the part of the system that actually worked without Congress, and it was always small.

With Germany the stakes were real. Germany's tariff law of March 1, 1906 reserved its lower conventional rates for treaty partners, and the United States was not one. The 1906 bridge had kept American goods off Germany's higher general rates for sixteen months. The press saw danger: the San Diego *Union and Daily Bee* of June 2, 1907 headed its wire story on the proclamation "Commercial Pact with Germany Is Made — Tariff War With Kaiser Is Averted." The 1907 agreement was the next step, still temporary, and paid for as much in customs procedure as in duty rates. Those procedural concessions reached beyond Germany: the State Department's 1907 correspondence has a separate chapter on applying them to Austria-Hungary, France, Great Britain, Italy, and Switzerland.

International copyright moved gradually too. The first section-13 proclamation, of July 1, 1891, took in Belgium, France, Great Britain and the British possessions, and Switzerland together. After that, proclamations came mostly one country at a time as each government met the Chace Act's reciprocity test. Austria was the sixteenth country brought in this way, and the third under Roosevelt, after Cuba (1903) and Norway (1905). The United States stood outside the multilateral Berne Convention, so findings like this one were how an Austrian author gained protection in American courts.

Protection under the Chace Act had a price. For books, photographs, chromos, and lithographs, the 1891 act required printing from type set or plates made within the United States. That "manufacturing clause" was what American printers and typographers had won for supporting the act, and it limited what a foreign author actually gained.

## Political impact
### Germany: wire news and organized protest
The German agreement was national news, and it drew organized opposition at home. California papers alone ran dozens of stories on "the German tariff agreement" between April and July 1907. The first word was the State Department's own summary, printed in the San Diego *Union and Daily Bee* on June 2, which admitted that Germany had not given its lowest rates to every American product.

By early July the protectionist side was mobilizing. A New York wire story printed in the Santa Barbara *Morning Press* on July 9, drawing on the *Journal of Commerce*, reported that domestic manufacturers, led largely by the American Protective Tariff association, planned a systematic campaign against the agreement. The association's secretary was William M. Wakeman, a former appraiser of the port. Manufacturers' associations around the country, the story said, had met and drafted resolutions of protest, and the main effort would be pressure on members of Congress. Other headlines that month ran the same way, with manufacturers and a tariff league promising a fight (San Jose *Mercury-News*, July 10; Colusa *Daily Sun*, July 1), while on the other side New York merchants were reported to approve the agreement (*Morning Press*, July 21). The wire story does not say which terms the manufacturers objected to. That a former customs appraiser led the campaign hints at the changes in customs procedure, but that has not been confirmed.

### Germany: the champagne quarrel
One objection came from abroad. On July 7, 1907 the French chargé d'affaires, writing from the embassy's summer quarters at Newport, protested that the agreement listed "champagne" among "the products of the soil and industry of Germany." The word, he wrote, "can not but be considered regrettable" and would favor "frauds as to indications of origin," and he invoked the misbranding sections of the Pure Food and Drugs Act of June 30, 1906. There was money in the complaint too. German sparkling wines paid the reduced rate from July 1, 1907, while French champagne did not get it until the Franco-American agreement of January 28, 1908 (Proclamation 797, [The Residual 1908 Proclamations](story:107)).

Root answered on September 24, four days after the Austrian proclamation. The agreement's wording, he wrote, "necessarily follows the language" of section 3 of the Dingley Act, which reads "Champagne and all other sparkling wines," and "it may not be correct to say that champagne, eo nomine, is a German product." He also passed on the view of the Secretary of Agriculture, who enforced the pure-food law: "the term 'champagne' should be restricted to the wine which is produced in the champagne district in France," and any other use of the word on a label was misbranding. Root argued from the tariff's wording; the restriction itself was Agriculture's. It was an early fight over a place name on a bottle, riding along on a tariff schedule.

### Germany: concessions that spread
The customs-procedure promises Root made to Germany did not stay with Germany. The State Department's correspondence for 1907 has a separate chapter on applying them to other countries, with sections for Austria-Hungary, France, Great Britain, Italy, and Switzerland.

### Austria: quiet at home, answered in Vienna
The Austrian copyright finding drew no American press notice that could be found, though only California papers were searched. The answer came from Vienna. On December 9, 1907, eleven weeks after the proclamation, the Austrian Minister of Justice issued an ordinance on copyright protection in relation to the United States. It cited section 13 of the 1891 act and Roosevelt's proclamation, and declared that Austria's 1895 copyright law now covered works of American citizens not published in Austria, so long as they were protected in the United States. The American finding came first, on Austrian assurances; Austria's own protection for American authors followed and rested on it.

## Legacy
### The end of section-3 reciprocity
The section-3 reciprocity system ended with the Payne-Aldrich Tariff of August 5, 1909. Section 2 of that act set up a maximum-and-minimum tariff. Section 4 directed the President to give notice, within ten days of the act's passage, to every country holding an agreement under section 3 of the 1897 act; once those agreements ended, imports from those countries would pay only the new law's rates. The German agreement of 1907 was one of the agreements ended this way. Broad reciprocal-trade authority did not return until the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934.

### Austrian copyright
The Austrian finding outlasted the Chace Act. The Copyright Act of March 4, 1909 replaced the 1891 law, and President Taft's proclamation of April 9, 1910 re-extended American copyright to Austria and the other countries already covered, effective July 1, 1909. Hungary, left out of the 1907 finding, came under American copyright by a separate convention, proclaimed in 1912.

On the Austrian side, the Justice Minister's ordinance of December 9, 1907 outlived the monarchy that issued it. Austria's Copyright Act of 1936 kept it in force, and it was not repealed until the end of 2018.

### Country by country
Country-by-country arrangements like these remained the American method for eighty years. The United States joined the Berne Convention, effective March 1, 1989, under the Berne Convention Implementation Act of 1988.

## At a glance
In January 1904 Roosevelt granted the old Fort Marcy army post in downtown Santa Fe to the city, which had agreed to hand it to the Board of Education to support the public schools. He did it over the objections of his own Interior Department, which suspected "a job." Nine weeks later he signed a second proclamation, labeled a correction, that changed a single word: "town" became "city."

## Background
These two proclamations disposed of the downtown army post at Santa Fe, not the old hilltop fort that shares its name. Fort Marcy dates from August 1846, when Brigadier General Stephen Watts Kearny's troops raised an earthwork on a hill overlooking the town during the Mexican–American War. That earthwork was never garrisoned; the soldiers lived in the old Spanish presidio north of the Plaza. An Executive Order of August 28, 1868, set apart a separate military reservation there, and a new post went up along Grant, Lincoln and Washington Avenues: officers' quarters facing the streets, with a hospital, barracks, parade ground, stables and storehouses behind. An Interior Department memorandum of about April 1903 put the reservation at "between 17 and 18 acres, including about 20 buildings."

The post's last years were stop-and-start. The reservation was relinquished on October 7, 1891, re-established on November 12, 1891, and relinquished again on June 15, 1895, for disposal under the 1884 act on abandoned military reservations. The Army had pulled out its last troops on October 10, 1894, about nine years before Roosevelt signed. In the meantime the Interior Department let prominent people live in the empty houses. N. B. Laughlin, a former justice of the territorial supreme court, told Senator Matthew Quay in April 1903 that "there are nineteen families living in the houses and have been for the past ten years without paying any rent."

The legal tool was a single proviso tucked into the sundry civil appropriations act of March 3, 1893. It let the President grant any abandoned military reservation of twenty acres or less to the town around it, for public use. Roosevelt used it to withdraw the Fort Marcy tract from sale and grant it to Santa Fe. The proclamations recite an agreement of August 14, 1902, in which the city promised to turn the land over to its Board of Education "to aid and assist in the support of the public schools," and the Board promised to keep and use "the same and the proceeds thereof" for the schools. That word "proceeds" mattered: the Board was free to sell.

**Why two proclamations?** Proclamation 519, signed March 10, 1904, repeats the four recitals and the grant of Proclamation 514 almost word for word. The *Statutes at Large* label it "Correction." Its one real change is that the grant runs to "the incorporated city of Santa Fe" rather than "the incorporated town." Every recital in both documents already called Santa Fe a city, and Santa Fe had elected mayors since 1891, so "town" did not match the grantee's own corporate name. The first was countersigned by Francis B. Loomis as Acting Secretary of State, the second by Secretary of State John Hay.

## Historical context
This was no tidy formality. The Interior Department's own file shows a fight that ran from 1896 to 1903, laid out in a memorandum Secretary Ethan Allen Hitchcock sent the President:

- **January 30, 1896:** on the General Land Office's recommendation, Interior sent President Cleveland a draft proclamation granting the reservation to Santa Fe. On **March 23** it asked for the draft back, because the land might be needed for an Indian school.
- **December 27, 1898:** the Land Office Commissioner recommended cutting the reservation into town lots and selling them "at public outcry to the highest bidders." On **February 8, 1899,** after the Indian Office said no school was needed, he was told to have the land subdivided.
- **1899 and 1903:** petitions poured in to grant the land to the city instead, "and one in the shape of a Memorial by the Territorial Legislature," the stated purpose being "to secure the property in aid of public schools."
- **March 16, 1903:** Land Office Commissioner W. A. Richards reported against the transfer, "as in his opinion the act does not warrant a grant of this kind," and the Department agreed. On **March 25** the mayor of Santa Fe asked for reconsideration, saying the city meant to use the land for schools and also "for location of a city hall, jail, etc." On **April 16** the Land Office again recommended a sale, with the money going to the Treasury.

The objections ran on two levels. In principle, the memorandum argued that Congress had already given New Mexico an unusually large grant of land for schools, and that nothing in the 1893 act let the government make a city carry out the purpose of a grant. It added that "because of this defect in the act, evidently, ... no action as warranted thereby has ever been taken thereunder." On the ground, Santa Fe was split. Laughlin wrote for "the tax payers here," who were "exceedingly anxious that this property pass into the hands of private owners who will keep it in repair, beautify the town and add to our taxable valuation."

Hitchcock sent Roosevelt that letter with his own on April 24, 1903, eleven days before the President's train reached Santa Fe on May 5: "When you reach Santa Fe, you will doubtless be importuned to issue a proclamation granting to the City of Santa Fe, for public purposes, Fort Marcy Military Reservation ... This, I trust, you will not consent to do, as there is more involved in this matter than appears on the surface of the application, in other words, a job." He closed by objecting to "transfer of this property to a local Board, under whose control I think this property should not be placed."

**Who asked for it.** The petitioners were the Territorial Legislative Assembly, the city government and, by June 1903, a delegation that reached the President in person. In a personal letter to Commissioner Richards on June 24, 1903, Roosevelt wrote: "Captain Fritz Muller, formerly of my regiment and an absolutely square man, together with the Mayor of Santa Fe and the Delegate from New Mexico, have been to me about the school land" matter in Santa Fe. He went on: "Their statement on its face seems a strong one. May I ask your particular attention to the matter and that after conferring with Delegate Rodey you will bring the subject before me?" The statement he enclosed was signed by Delegate Bernard S. Rodey, Mayor I. Sparks and Fred Muller, receiver of the Santa Fe land office and a former Rough Rider captain. Six months later the grant was made. Whether Richards or Hitchcock came around in the meantime, or whether Roosevelt overruled them, is not known.

## Political impact
The grant went against a recorded objection from the Secretary of the Interior and the General Land Office, and against a local taxpayers' faction that wanted the land auctioned to private owners who would "add to our taxable valuation." The winners are clear from the result: the city and its schools. The petitioners had reached the President himself, through a delegation that included a former captain of his own regiment, and six months after Roosevelt asked the Land Office to give their case "particular attention," the grant was made. Whether the Interior Department changed its advice in the meantime, or whether Roosevelt overruled it, is not known.

The authority, too, was anything but settled. The Land Office had ruled that the 1893 proviso did not support a grant meant for schools, and the April 1903 memorandum said no action had ever been taken under it. Proclamation 514 appears to have been the first time a President used it: no earlier proclamation in the *Statutes at Large* quotes its operative words, "withhold from sale and grant for public use." Congress had meanwhile handed other abandoned posts to towns by statute, among them one to Oklahoma City for its schools in 1894 and Fort McKinney to Buffalo, Wyoming, but those were acts of Congress, not uses of the President's power.

How much notice the grants drew in the newspapers has not yet been established; the *Santa Fe New Mexican* for January and March 1904 has not been read. The one oddity on the face of the record is the pair itself: two grants of the same land sixty-five days apart, the second labeled a correction, changing "town" to "city."

## Legacy
### The downtown post
The Board of Education did what the agreement allowed: it sold the property. The land was delivered to the city in 1904 and passed to the Board "for sale to private owners." The Otero-Bergere family, living in one of the officers' quarters with Interior's permission since 1901, bought that house from the Board on December 22, 1905, for $2,700. One officers' quarters still stands on Lincoln Avenue. It was bought in 1916 as Edgar Lee Hewett's residence, remodeled in Pueblo Revival style, and now houses the Museum of New Mexico Foundation. The other five officers' quarters and the post buildings "were eventually razed," according to the National Park Service. The New Mexico Museum of Art went up across Lincoln Avenue from the Palace of the Governors in 1916–17.

### The hill
The hilltop earthwork of 1846 was never part of the grant. By 1901 it was private land, held under a quitclaim deed by L. Bradford Prince and W. T. Thornton. It came to the city by a separate route after Prince's heirs offered it; one secondary account dates the city's acquisition to 1961. It is now the Old Fort Marcy park overlook. The Fort Marcy Ballfield on Bishops Lodge Road, built by the WPA in 1936 and part of the city's Fort Marcy Recreation Complex, is described as standing on the former military reservation, but whether its ground lies inside the 1904 grant has not been confirmed.

### What the grant became
The proclamations' larger legacy is the one their recitals promised. For a time the old post's buildings and land helped support Santa Fe's public schools, and once sold, the property became part of the downtown museum and residential district around the Plaza. The Fort Marcy grant also appears to be the first use of a presidential power Congress had written into an appropriations bill eleven years before.

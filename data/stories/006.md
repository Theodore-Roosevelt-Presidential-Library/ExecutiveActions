## At a glance
On April 16, 1908, Theodore Roosevelt used the Antiquities Act to protect three enormous natural bridges in Utah's remote canyon country. It was Utah's first national monument, and it was tiny: a forty-acre square around each bridge, 120 acres in all. Signed three months after the sweeping Grand Canyon proclamation, it shows the same law read at its narrowest.

## Background
Proclamation 804 protected three spans of Cedar Mesa Sandstone in the southeastern corner of Utah. It called them "extraordinary examples of stream erosion" with "heights more lofty and spans far greater than any heretofore known to exist," and named them as they were then known, "by common report named by Horace J. Long": Augusta Bridge, Caroline Bridge, and the Little Bridge.

The proclamation places them "on the White Canyon and tributaries," and that wording is exact. Only Augusta stands in White Canyon proper. Caroline rises near where White and Armstrong canyons meet, and the Little Bridge sits in Armstrong Canyon, carved by a stream from Tuwa Canyon and now left high above the active channel, which is why it looks like a relic beside the other two. All three lie in San Juan County, the largest county in Utah.

What the proclamation reserved was strikingly small: "forty acres in square form" around the center of each bridge, and nothing in between.

## Historical context
The bridges reached a national audience in August 1904, when W. W. Dyar's illustrated article "The Colossal Bridges of Utah: A Recent Discovery of Natural Wonders" ran in *The Century Illustrated Monthly Magazine*. *The National Geographic Magazine* printed a condensed version the next month. Both presented the spans as wonders of science, exactly the kind of "scientific interest" the Antiquities Act named. The groundwork for the proclamation itself came later, from a 1907 University of Utah survey led by Byron Cummings, whose maps went to Edgar Lee Hewett at the General Land Office.

The names came from that early publicity rather than from the land. Horace J. Long's party reached the bridges in March 1903, setting out from Dandy Crossing. Long named the largest for his wife, Augusta. He named a second the Caroline because his guide, James Scorup, made it a condition of guiding him; the honor was for Scorup's mother. The third Long simply called "the Little Bridge" in his field notes. Prospector Cass Hite is said to have seen the bridges in 1883 and named them for the President, the Senator, and the Congressman, but that story rests on Hite's own memory a quarter-century later, and Dyar's 1904 account does not mention him.

Natural Bridges is best read beside the Grand Canyon. The Antiquities Act limited a monument to "the smallest area compatible with the proper care and management" of what it protected. On January 11, 1908, in [Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3), Roosevelt read that clause to cover more than 800,000 acres. Three months and five days later, here, he read it literally: three bridges, three forty-acre squares. Side by side, the two proclamations show the full range of what a president could claim the law allowed, and Natural Bridges is the quiet proof that the broad reading was a choice, not a necessity.

## Political impact
Nobody objected to protecting the bridges. Plenty of people objected to how little land came with them, and the Utah coverage was immediate and statewide.

The news broke warmly. On April 17, 1908, the *Salt Lake Herald* ran "PRESERVED FROM VANDALS / Utah's Three Natural Bridges Will Be Placed in Charge of Custodian by President" on page one, and the *Salt Lake Tribune* ran "MOVE TO PRESERVE OUR NATURAL BRIDGES." Within a day the tone changed. The *Tribune*'s editorial of April 18 said: "It seems to us, however, that the area thus set apart — forty acres — is too small. It might as well be a full section." It was the first of at least four editorials in twelve days. Byron Cummings, whose survey had laid the groundwork, told the *Deseret Evening News* the same day that the reservation "is not sufficient for the purpose intended." On April 19 the *Tribune* wrote that the President "was even more mistaken in his sparingness than we thought," and proposed a full township, six miles square, or 23,040 acres. On April 23 the Salt Lake Real Estate Association formally petitioned for exactly that.

Two details stand out. The papers almost all reported the reservation as "forty acres," not 120, so the critics were campaigning against a monument one-third its real size. And the *Tribune* explained why no one fought the reservation itself: there was "no reason at all why he should not, because there are no conflicting interests down there, no vested rights, no settlements on that ground — nothing whatever in the way" (April 29, 1908). Nobody wanted the ground.

Roosevelt responded quickly. By May 7 the *Tribune* reported that he had asked Gifford Pinchot how much more land was needed, with Senator Reed Smoot working the question in Washington. On May 13 the *Tribune* went further and reported a new proclamation adding a tract "nine by fourteen miles." No such proclamation was ever issued. The monument stayed at three forty-acre squares through the rest of Roosevelt's presidency, and the story stands as an example of the press running ahead of the government.

The campaign for more land got its answer the next year. President William Howard Taft's Proclamation 881 of September 25, 1909 threw out the three squares and drew a single tract of "about two thousand four hundred and twenty acres," plus two 160-acre tracts at cave springs. That made roughly 2,740 acres, more than twenty times the original.

## Legacy
The boundary has moved three times since 1908. After Taft's enlargement, Woodrow Wilson's Proclamation 1323 of February 11, 1916 re-described the same ground against newly established survey corners without enlarging it. John F. Kennedy's Proclamation 3486 of August 14, 1962 added about 5,236 acres, taking in more cliff dwellings, a visitor center, and an entrance road, and dropped about 320 acres at Snow Flat Spring Cave and Cigarette Spring Cave. Today the monument covers about 7,636 acres. It was Utah's first national monument and the first unit of the national park system anywhere in the state.

### New names for the bridges
The bridges carry different names now, and those names arrived with a survey, not a proclamation. William Boone Douglass, Examiner of Surveys for the General Land Office, resurveyed the area in 1908 and thought the family names "entirely inappropriate." Neil Judd, who saw it happen, put it plainly: "Since none was known, Indian names had to be invented." Douglass turned to a colleague at the National Museum who studied the Hopi, and the U.S. Geographic Board accepted the result:

- **Augusta** became **Sipapu**.
- **Caroline** became **Kachina**.
- **The Little Bridge**, nicknamed "Edwin" in 1905 for Col. Edwin F. Holmes of the Salt Lake Commercial Club, became **Owachomo**.

The names are Hopi words, supplied by an Anglo ethnologist at a surveyor's request, for bridges that stand well outside Hopi country. Even the official record got tangled. The Geographic Board's *Fourth Report* (1916) paired two of the three names with the wrong bridges and repeated the mistake in its *Fifth Report*, so the federal name record was wrong for years until the Board re-decided all three names in 1937.

### Dark skies
One more distinction came that no one in 1908 was thinking about. On March 6, 2007, the International Dark-Sky Association (now DarkSky International) certified Natural Bridges as the world's first International Dark Sky Park. (Flagstaff, Arizona, had been named the first International Dark Sky City in 2001, a different category.)

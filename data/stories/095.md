## At a glance
In the autumn of 1907 the federal bird refuge crossed the continent. On October 14 Roosevelt signed an order setting aside Three Arch Rocks, off the Oregon coast, as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds and animals: the first federal bird reservation on the Pacific Coast. Nine days later he added three more off Washington's Olympic coast, and in December one more island in Louisiana. Around them ran the usual stream of government housekeeping.

## Background
Every one of these bird orders used the device Roosevelt had first tried at Pelican Island in 1903. Take small, uninhabited rocks or islets the government already owned, and in a single paragraph reserve them "for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." The Pacific orders added "and animals," because the rocks off Oregon and Washington sheltered hauled-out sea lions as well as nesting seabirds. All of the reservations went to the Bureau of Biological Survey, the Agriculture Department bureau that ran the growing chain of refuges.

Three Arch Rocks is a cluster of three large and six small rocks about half a mile off Oceanside, Oregon. Executive Order 699 (October 14, 1907) reads in full:

> It is hereby ordered that the group of small unsurveyed islands known as the "Three Arch Rocks," located in the Pacific Ocean from one-half mile to one mile off the coast of Oregon, approximately in latitude 45° 28′ north, 124° west from Greenwich, as shown upon the United States Coast Survey Chart No. 6100, and located within the area segregated by a broken line and shown upon the diagram hereto attached and made a part of this order, is hereby reserved and set aside for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds and animals. This reservation to be known as Three Arch Rocks Reservation.
>
> THEODORE ROOSEVELT.
> The White House, *October 14, 1907.*

On October 23 Roosevelt signed three near-twins for the Washington coast: Flattery Rocks (Executive Order 703), Copalis Rock (Executive Order 704), and Quillayute Needles (Executive Order 705). Together they swept in hundreds of rocks, reefs, and islets scattered along the Olympic coast from Cape Flattery south toward Copalis. They are worded almost exactly like the Oregon order, down to the reservation's purpose. The Oregon order treats its rocks as one group that "is hereby reserved"; the Copalis Rock order treats its islands as individuals that "are hereby reserved," and cites coast survey chart No. 6400 instead of No. 6100. On December 7, Executive Order 718 added East Timbalier Island in Louisiana. It reads "native birds" only, without the Pacific orders' "and animals."

The rest of the run was ordinary government business:

- **Executive Order 708** (November 2) released post-cemetery land at old Fort Sumner, New Mexico, that had been reserved by an order of May 22, 1871.
- **Executive Orders 709 and 711** (November 9 and 11) added land to the Navajo Reservation in Arizona and New Mexico and to the Jicarilla Reservation in New Mexico. Both were later amended by Executive Order 743 of January 28, 1908.
- **Executive Order 721** (December 14) told department officers who held archives to admit credentialed agents of the Carnegie Institution's Department of Historical Research. The access was hedged: it reached only as far as "public interests and departmental orders permit," and only at times "not inconvenient in respect to public business."
- **Executive Order 723** (December 17) directed the Civil Service Commission to help Members of Congress test the qualifications of applicants for designation to the military and naval academies, at no added expense to the Commission.
- Other orders amended the consular-fee schedule, reinstated clerks without regard to the civil-service rules, and set aside telegraph-line lands in Alaska.

## Historical context
The Pacific reservations answered the same kind of crisis that had produced the Gulf and Atlantic refuges, with a local cast of characters. Off Oregon and Washington the main threat was not the millinery plume trade. It came from eggers who raided the colonies, sport shooters who fired into the wheeling flocks for fun, and hunters who killed sea lions by the dozen for hides and oil.

The decisive push at Three Arch Rocks came from two young Portland naturalists, William L. Finley and Herman T. Bohlman. They spent several weeks in the summer of 1901 photographing the slaughter from shore. On June 20, 1903, they returned and camped among the colonies on the westernmost rock, photographing nesting murres and puffins at close range. Accounts of how long that camp lasted disagree. Roosevelt had met Finley in Portland in May 1903, during his Great Loop tour, and invited him to the White House to make the case. Finley went and spread the photographs on a table. By Brinkley's account he got an enthusiastic "Bully, bully" and a promise of a sanctuary. When that first presentation took place is uncertain. Finley was back at the White House in 1906 for the Oregon Audubon Society, asking again about Three Arch Rocks.

The President was a lifelong bird-watcher and needed no convincing. Once again he used the tool that required neither an appropriation nor an act of Congress: an executive order reserving public land the government already held. What stood out in the autumn of 1907 was how far the program now reached. By October at least eight bird reservations already existed, including Pelican Island, Breton, Passage Key, Siskiwit, Huron, Indian Key, Tern Islands, and Shell Keys. Stump Lake in North Dakota, set aside by Executive Order 296-A on March 9, 1905 ([The Executive Orders of Early 1905](story:85)), already lay well west of the Mississippi. Three Arch Rocks was the first on the Pacific Coast, and within nine days the same idea had reached the Washington coast too.

## Political impact
None of these orders drew much notice at the time. Each covered a few acres of rock that no rancher or homesteader wanted, so no local economy was disturbed and no lawsuit followed.

The orders were exactly the kind of quiet stroke Roosevelt could make on his own signature, and that was what made the practice behind them controversial in principle. To his critics in Congress and the West, the steady pile-up of executive reservations of forest, mineral and bird land looked like the President "locking up" the public domain by fiat, without the consent of the people's representatives. The bird orders were too small to fight over, but they were part of the larger quarrel over executive power that would sharpen in Roosevelt's last two years in office.

## Legacy
These small refuges long outlived the administration that made them.

### Three Arch Rocks
Three Arch Rocks, fifteen acres across nine rocks by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's count, is still the first federal bird reservation on the Pacific Coast. Proclamation 2416 of July 25, 1940, renamed it a National Wildlife Refuge, along with the Washington reservations. Congress designated it wilderness by Public Law 91-504, approved October 23, 1970, the same act that created the Oregon Islands and Washington Islands Wildernesses; that act describes "about seventeen acres" of wilderness at Three Arch Rocks. The Fish and Wildlife Service calls it the largest and most diverse seabird colony in Oregon. The often-quoted figure of more than 200,000 birds refers to common murres, and the Service gives it as a historical figure rather than a current count. The rocks also hold the only Steller sea lion pupping site on the north Oregon coast. The eastern population of Steller sea lion, the one found in Oregon, was removed from the endangered list as recovered on November 4, 2013. Only the western population is still listed as endangered.

### The Washington coast
Proclamation 2416 made the three Washington reservations the Flattery Rocks, Quillayute Needles and Copalis National Wildlife Refuges. Together they take in about eight hundred offshore rocks, reefs and islands from Cape Flattery south to just below Copalis Head, apart from those belonging to Native American reservations. In 1966 Public Land Order 4095 removed James Island from Quillayute Needles as part of the Quileute Indian Reservation. The 1970 act made the three refuges the Washington Islands Wilderness. Today they are managed within the Washington Maritime National Wildlife Refuge Complex. They provide nesting habitat for more than seventy percent of Washington's seabirds and support some of the largest seabird colonies in the continental United States.

### East Timbalier Island
East Timbalier Island, low and shifting like the Gulf reservations before it, fared worst. Proclamation 2416, whose Louisiana list named Breton, Lacassine, Sabine, Shell Keys and Tern Islands, never renamed it a National Wildlife Refuge. On April 10, 1969, Public Land Order 4597, signed by Assistant Secretary of the Interior Harrison Loesch under delegated authority, revoked the reservation outright. That was an Interior Department action, not a president's. Oil infrastructure overran the island. A restoration project reached the design stage but was closed in July 2020, after a breach caused rapid degradation and revised sediment quantities drove up the cost. Louisiana's Coastal Protection and Restoration Authority moved the money to West Belle Headland, Timbalier Island and Trinity-East Island. By early summer 2021 the island had, in the words of a U.S. Geological Survey report, "finally erod[ed] away." It is not the end of the story. The authority's *Fiscal Year 2027 Annual Plan* (March 2026) lists East Timbalier Island Restoration among its active projects, in engineering and design, and says that "Wine Island, East Timbalier Island, Grand Isle, and the Fourchon shoreline are all in the restoration process."

### Fifty-one reservations
These five orders are among the fifty-one bird reservations Roosevelt created by executive order, the practical foundation of today's National Wildlife Refuge System. The Fish and Wildlife Service's published figure of fifty-five adds the four national game preserves (see [The Executive Orders of Late Summer and Autumn 1907](story:94)).

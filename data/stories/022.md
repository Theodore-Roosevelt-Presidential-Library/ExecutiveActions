## At a glance
On October 4, 1904, Roosevelt signed an executive order reserving Breton Island, the Old Harbor Islands and the Freemason Islands, off the mouth of the Mississippi, "as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." It was the country's second federal bird reservation, after [Pelican Island](story:1). A second order followed in November 1905. Roosevelt visited the islands himself in 1915, and Breton is believed to be the only refuge he ever saw in person.

## Background
About a year and a half after the Pelican Island order, Roosevelt used the same tool on the Louisiana Gulf coast. He took a cluster of unclaimed federal islands and, with his signature alone, set them aside for the birds that nested there. The whole order is just two sentences:

> It is hereby ordered that Breton island, as shown by the General Land Office map of the State of Louisiana of date 1896, in Township 18 South, Range 20 East, St. Helena Meridian, when same shall be surveyed; and Old Harbor and Freemason islands, in Townships 14 and 15 South, Ranges 21 and 22 East, same Meridian, when surveyed, be, and they are hereby reserved and set apart for the use of the Department of Agriculture, as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds. This reservation to be known as "Breton Island Reservation."

The reserved ground was a chain of shifting sand-and-shell barrier islands. They were bare and storm-swept, useless to a farmer or a logger, and for that very reason ideal for the colonial seabirds and shorebirds that packed onto them to breed. Breton Island lies south of the Chandeleur chain. Old Harbor and Freemason lie roughly eighteen to twenty-four miles to the north, and Freemason sits inside Chandeleur Sound, about eight miles west of the chain. None of the land had been surveyed yet. The order described it by reference to an 1896 General Land Office map and reserved it "when same shall be surveyed."

The reservation was in force within months. In January 1905, the Biological Survey issued a circular warning against trespass on Breton Island Reservation. Its own T. S. Palmer recorded in 1905 that the keys "were set aside as a Federal reserve on October 4, 1904," and he photographed terns breeding on one of the Old Harbor keys that July.

Then, on November 11, 1905, Roosevelt signed a second Breton order, later numbered Executive Order 369-A. The index of numbered orders describes it as reserving "Freemason Islands, Old Harbor Islands, and Breton Island as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds," the same three groups as before. Why it was issued is not known, and its text has not been found in print.

Breton matters less for its acreage than for what it repeated. Done once at Pelican Island, the step might have been a curiosity. Done a second time, and then again and again, it became a system. Between Pelican Island in March 1903 and Breton in October 1904 there were no other bird reservations; after Breton, they came quickly. Stump Lake followed in March 1905, then Passage Key, Siskiwit and Huron that October. By the end of his presidency, Roosevelt had signed 51 executive orders establishing bird reservations in 17 states and three territories.

## Historical context
The threat was the same one behind Pelican Island: the plume trade, now on the Gulf. Fashion still called for feathers and whole birds on women's hats. Along the Louisiana coast, egg collectors and plume hunters raided the seabird colonies in nesting season, taking eggs by the barrel and shooting adults for their breeding plumes. Terns, gulls, skimmers and other birds that nest in colonies were being hit hardest where they were most vulnerable.

The push to protect them came from the bird-protection movement: the young Audubon societies and their allies. Their case combined two arguments. One was economic, that wild birds were worth real money to farmers and the country. The other was moral, a growing disgust at slaughtering whole nesting colonies for hats. The Lacey Act of 1900 had put federal weight behind state bird laws. Pelican Island had shown that a president could simply reserve worthless-looking federal land as shelter for birds. Breton applied that lesson to a second flyway. Roosevelt himself credited the Audubon movement with starting the drive for bird refuges. No single advocate can be tied to the Breton order specifically.

Reserving the land was one thing. Keeping armed egg collectors and plume hunters off remote islands was another. That job fell to very few people working lonely, dangerous water. At Breton, it fell to one man, Captain William Sprinkle, master of the Audubon Society boat *Royal Tern*. Writing about his 1915 visit in *A Book-Lover's Holidays in the Open* (1916), Roosevelt described Sprinkle as "born and bred on this Gulf coast, who knows the sea-fowl, and the islands where they breed and dwell, as he knows the winds and the lovely, smiling, treacherous Gulf waters." Sprinkle "was at once put in charge" when the reservation was created. He and his boat were "the police force for over five hundred square miles of sand-bars, shallow waters, and intricate channels." Funding came from both sides: "The Biological Survey does its best with its limited means; the Audubon Society adds something extra; but this very efficient and disinterested laborer is worth a good deal more than the hire he receives." The risk was real. In July 1905, Audubon warden Guy Bradley was shot and killed by plume hunters in Florida.

## Political impact
Like [Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1), the Breton order needed no act of Congress and got none. No one in Congress fought the reservation of a few uninhabited sandbars, and no organized opposition appeared.

The Agriculture Department put the order to work quickly. On January 19, 1905, the Biological Survey issued Circular 45, a one-page "Warning against trespass on Breton Island Reservation." Captain William Sprinkle, in Roosevelt's later words, "was at once put in charge," patrolling in the Audubon boat *Royal Tern* with pay from both the Biological Survey and the Audubon Society. By July 1905 the Survey's T. S. Palmer was photographing terns breeding on Southwest Harbor Key, one of the Old Harbor Islands, and he wrote in the department's 1905 *Yearbook* that the keys had been "set aside as a Federal reserve on October 4, 1904."

On November 11, 1905, Roosevelt signed a second order for the same three island groups, later numbered Executive Order 369-A. Its purpose, and its text, are not known.

How the press received the reservation is still an open question. A search of the digitized California papers for 1904 and 1905 turned up almost nothing about it, but the New Orleans papers, the most likely to have covered it, have not been checked.

What can be said is that the order rested on a bold claim: that a president could set public land aside purely for wildlife, with no act of Congress. It went unchallenged because each piece looked so small. By signing one reservation after another on the same unremarked authority, Roosevelt built a federal wildlife system out of executive orders that no one bothered to fight one at a time. The significance was cumulative, and at the time it was mostly invisible.

## Legacy

### From reservation to refuge
On October 4, 1938, Franklin D. Roosevelt's Executive Order 7983 revoked "The Executive orders of October 4, 1904, and November 11, 1905, reserving certain of these islands as the Breton Island Reservation." It did more than rename the unit. It reserved Breton Island, the Freemason Islands, the Old Harbor Islands, Errol Island and the southern part of the Chandeleur Islands, which had never been in Theodore Roosevelt's reservation and came in only when part of their 1847 lighthouse reservation was revoked to make room. The order provided that "This reservation shall be known as the Breton Bird Refuge." That name did not last either; Proclamation 2416 of July 25, 1940 changed it again. Today it is Breton National Wildlife Refuge, which the Fish and Wildlife Service calls "the second oldest refuge of over 540 currently in the National Wildlife Refuge System."

The two Roosevelt orders later turned up in a federal-state lawsuit: Louisiana entered both, the October 1904 order and the November 1905 order, as exhibits in *United States v. Louisiana* (No. 9 Original).

### Roosevelt's 1915 visit
Breton is the only refuge Theodore Roosevelt is known to have visited. He wrote up the trip as "Bird Reserves at the Mouth of the Mississippi," a chapter of *A Book-Lover's Holidays in the Open* (1916), first printed as "The Bird Refuges of Louisiana" in *Scribner's Magazine* for March 1916. "ON June 7, 1915, I was the guest of my friend John M. Parker, of New Orleans, at his house at Pass Christian, Mississippi," he began. The party set out at four the next morning and returned on the morning of the 12th, a five-day voyage. Parker was the leader of the Louisiana Progressives; in 1916 he ran for governor and was the Progressive Party's vice-presidential nominee on a ticket with Roosevelt, and he became governor in 1920. Also aboard were Herbert K. Job, the ornithologist and photographer sent by the National Audubon Society at Frank M. Chapman's suggestion; Frank M. Miller, founder of the Louisiana State Audubon Society; M. L. Alexander of the state Conservation Commission; and Captain Sprinkle.

Roosevelt counted the results himself. "Of the five chief birds, the royal terns, Caspian terns, Cabot's terns, laughing gulls, and skimmers, there were that season about one thousand nests. This season, ten years later, there are about thirty-five thousand nests. The brown pelicans and Louisiana herons also show a marked increase. The least tern, which had been completely exterminated or driven away, has returned and is breeding in fair numbers."

Much of what he saw was not federal ground. The Chandeleurs, Grand Isle, Bird Island, Last Island, Battledore Island and Barataria Bay were Audubon and state sanctuaries, and the difference showed: "The westernmost island we visited was outside the national reservation, and that very morning it had been visited and plundered by a party of eggers." In the same account he wrote that "This particular reservation was set apart by presidential proclamation in 1905." Both details are slips: the reservation was created by executive order, in October 1904.

Job's film of the trip survives as *Roosevelt, Friend of the Birds* in the Library of Congress's Theodore Roosevelt Association Collection, free to use and reuse. The Roosevelt Memorial Association's 1924 title cards claimed he had visited Pelican Island, which he never did; the Library of Congress has corrected that, placing the film at "the Breton Island Reservation and Audubon bird sanctuaries off the coast of Louisiana." A hurricane later in 1915 destroyed the Breton Island fishing village and school the party had sailed past, and they were never rebuilt.

### Islands on the move
The refuge's later history centers on a threat no one in 1904 was thinking about: these are barrier islands, and they are washing away. Hurricanes and steady erosion have torn at the Breton and Chandeleur chain again and again. The Chandeleur lighthouse stood for 111 years before Hurricane Katrina brought it down in 2005. The North Breton Island Restoration, begun December 9, 2020, used about 5.9 million cubic yards of dredged sand to rebuild more than 400 acres of barrier island. The roughly $72 million project was paid for from the Deepwater Horizon natural-resource-damage settlement, together with an earlier oil-spill settlement. Louisiana's Coastal Protection and Restoration Authority runs a separate Chandeleur Islands restoration.

The Fish and Wildlife Service puts the refuge at 1,100 acres in St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes, though the land above water shifts with every storm and restoration. Public Law 93-632 of January 3, 1975 designated 5,000 acres as wilderness, covering all federally owned refuge land except North Breton Island, which was left out because of an oil facility. The smallest wilderness in the national system is another Roosevelt bird reservation: Pelican Island Wilderness in Florida, at 5.5 acres.

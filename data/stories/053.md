## At a glance
On May 2, 1904, Roosevelt reserved a scattering of public land along the Snake River in south-central Idaho "for occupation as townsites." The request came from inside the new reclamation bureaucracy, nine days after the government approved the Minidoka irrigation project. Planners expected irrigated farms to draw settlers, and settlers to need towns on the coming railroad. Two of the reserved blocks now lie under the towns of Rupert and Heyburn, and a third is the site of Acequia.

## Background
The proclamation reserved land in the Hailey Land District, the General Land Office district headquartered at Hailey in the Wood River country. The land had already been held back once. On November 17, 1902, Secretary of the Interior Ethan A. Hitchcock withdrew the irrigable land of the prospective Minidoka Project from entry except under the homestead law. On April 26, 1904, Interior lifted that withdrawal for these tracts and closed them to all entry of any kind, so the President could reserve them.

The legal tool was an old one: sections 2380 and 2381 of the Revised Statutes, the general town-site law, which let the President reserve public land at natural or prospective centers of population and provided for selling town lots. This was a town-site reservation, not a reclamation withdrawal and not a reserve for dams or canals.

The proclamation names the chain of requests itself. The Director of the U.S. Geological Survey (the Reclamation Service was then a branch of the Survey) wrote on April 15, 1904, that the lands were "suitable for townsite purposes along the line of a proposed railroad." The Secretary of the Interior formally asked for the reservation on April 30, and Roosevelt signed on May 2. Francis B. Loomis countersigned as Acting Secretary of State. Settlers and town companies did not ask for it.

The text states no total acreage. Its described tracts add up to 1,280 acres, plus four numbered lots of fractional, river-cut land along the Snake whose areas it does not give. One detail stands out in the text: it cites the Reclamation Act as "31 Stat., 388." The correct citation is 32 Stat. 388. The mistake is in the document as issued, and the *Statutes at Large* prints it that way, flagging it in the margin.

## Historical context
The timing explains the proclamation. Secretary Hitchcock approved the Minidoka Project on April 23, 1904, and allotted it $2.6 million, making it one of the first irrigation projects under the Reclamation Act Roosevelt had signed in 1902 ([The Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21)). The Survey's town-site letter came eight days *before* that approval, so town planning was moving along with the decision to build.

The "proposed railroad" fits the Minidoka & Southwestern, which the Oregon Short Line organized in January 1904 to build south and west from Minidoka toward the Twin Falls country. Construction began that November. A settler writing from Minidoka on July 10, 1904, in a letter printed in the *Iron County Record* of Cedar City, Utah, connected the two. He wrote that the Short Line had surveyed a branch from Minidoka to a point twenty miles down the river, through the heart of the land the irrigation project would water, and that the government had laid out three town sites on the tract along the line of that proposed branch.

The planning logic was simple. Irrigated land would draw settlers, settlers would need towns on the rail line, and the government wanted the best town sites held before anyone else could file on them. In the proclamation's words, the sites would "become centers of population and necessary to the proper development of the project."

## Political impact
The reservation stirred no fight anyone has found. The Salt Lake and Ogden dailies, which followed the Short Line and the Minidoka country closely, did not report the proclamation itself. The town sites reached print instead as news of opportunity. The settler W. H. Pace, writing from Minidoka in July 1904 in a letter printed in the *Iron County Record* of Cedar City, Utah, told readers that the government's town lots would not be sold until water reached the land, and then would go to the highest bidders. He reported that homesteaders were making a 123-mile trip to the land office at Hailey to file, and that in sixty days Minidoka had grown from a depot and a water tank into a place with lumberyards, a hotel, a restaurant and saloons. No objection to the reservation turned up in the Utah papers searched; how Idaho's own papers treated it is not known.

The action came at the Reclamation Service's request, under long-settled town-site law, on land the government already held back. It mattered most to the Hailey land office, the Reclamation Service, the railroad, and anyone hoping to file on the riverbank tracts. Work on the ground followed within two years. The Oregon Short Line began building its branch out of Minidoka in November 1904. The Rupert and Heyburn sites were surveyed from August to October 1905, and the General Land Office reported in 1906 that the plats of two town sites on the Minidoka project had been approved. Rupert was platted November 21, 1905, and incorporated April 12, 1906.

## Legacy
The town sites changed legal hands in 1906. On June 15 of that year, Proclamation 633 cited this proclamation by name and excluded 480 acres from it so they could be withdrawn and sold under a new law, the Act of April 16, 1906. That act let the Secretary of the Interior withdraw up to 160 acres at a time for town sites on reclamation projects, survey them into lots and auction the lots, with the money going into the reclamation fund. The land never reopened to settlement; it moved from one town-site system to another. The 480 acres made three 160-acre blocks, the same number as the three town sites the 1904 letter described, though the letter does not name or locate them.

### Rupert, Heyburn and Acequia
All three blocks became towns. Downtown Rupert lies in section 29 of Township 9 South, Range 24 East, and Heyburn in section 15 of Township 10 South, Range 23 East. The third block, in section 1 of Township 9 South, is Acequia. The Reclamation Service's *Sixth Annual Report* (1906–1907) named all three as the town sites established on the Minidoka project, on the north side of the Snake on the Minidoka and Southwestern Railway, and said the land for them had been withdrawn by the proclamation of May 2, 1904.

Lots were first offered on August 20, 1906 at Heyburn and August 27, 1906 at Rupert, under the 1906 act rather than the old town-site law. Acequia was to be surveyed in the fall of 1907. The Bureau of Reclamation lists four government towns on the project: Rupert, Heyburn, Minidoka and Acequia. Burley, the other familiar town nearby, is not one of them. It grew up on the Short Line's branch south of the river, on land this proclamation did not reserve.

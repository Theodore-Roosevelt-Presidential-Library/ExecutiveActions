## At a glance
On January 16, 1908, Theodore Roosevelt set apart "the natural formations, known as the Pinnacles Rocks, with a series of caves underlying them" in California's Gabilan Range as a national monument. Like Devils Tower, Pinnacles stretched the Antiquities Act to cover a natural wonder rather than an ancient ruin. The push for it came from a homesteader and a local congressman, while a reluctant Forest Service supplied the paperwork. Californians took note and approved, and the small monument eventually became a national park and a refuge for the California condor.

## Background
Proclamation 796 reserved the Pinnacles Rocks and their caves because they were "of scientific interest." Like other monuments of the time, it gave no acreage, and the boundary is the diagram bound with it. The Department of Agriculture put the area at about 2,080 acres.

**A note on the date.** The *Statutes at Large* prints the proclamation's closing line as "this twenty-eighth day of January," and some references follow it. That is a printer's error. The signed original, now in the Pinnacles National Park museum collection, reads "this 16th day of January" and bears a State Department filing stamp of January 16, 1908. The boundary diagram and the *Statutes*' own margin note say January 16 as well. The France reciprocity proclamation printed directly below it is dated January 28 and carries an identically worded closing, so the typesetter evidently reused the wrong date line. The first newspaper reports, datelined Washington, January 17, said the President "has just signed" it.

The monument took in the scenic core of the Pinnacles National Forest, east of the Salinas Valley: a cluster of jagged rock spires, sheer crags and dark talus caves. In the Park Service's words, the caves "formed when massive boulders fell from above into narrow gorges or shear fractures, becoming wedged before hitting the ground. These boulders now form the ceilings of the caves." Roosevelt had created the surrounding 14,108-acre Pinnacles Forest Reserve on July 18, 1906, and the reserve became a national forest in 1907 when Congress renamed every reserve. As at Gila ([Gila Cliff-Dwellings National Monument, New Mexico](story:24)), the proclamation let forest uses continue but made the monument "the dominant reservation."

Pinnacles was proclaimed for its scenery and geology, but it does contain traces of ancient people. The Chalone Creek Archeological Sites, dating to A.D. 1000–1499, are listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Historical context
**A homesteader's campaign.** Schuyler Colfax Hain came to Bear Valley, east of the Pinnacles in southern San Benito County, from Michigan in 1891 with his young wife, Ida Cook Hain, following his parents, who had settled there in 1886. He ran the Bear Valley post office, named Cook after his wife's maiden name, and for years guided visitors through the caves and crags. He pitched the Central Coast Counties Association at Hollister in 1902–03, and on February 11, 1905 the California Assembly passed a joint resolution endorsing a Pinnacles national park. Writing in *Out West* in 1905, Hain told readers that "there is a movement under way to have 15,000 acres, embracing the heart of this interesting region, set aside as a national park," and that "The Secretary of the Interior has withdrawn the tract from entry, pending Congressional action." He was later remembered as "the Father of the Pinnacles."

Hain also enlisted his congressman, James Carson Needham, a Modesto Republican whose sprawling district took in nine counties from San Joaquin down to Monterey, among them San Benito, which contains the Pinnacles. Hain approached Needham around 1902, and in January 1903 he also wrote to David Starr Jordan, president of Stanford. Jordan sent Professor William Russell Dudley to inspect the site. On April 23, 1904, over Jordan's signature, Dudley forwarded the San Benito residents' petition to Needham, two weeks before Jordan himself visited Bear Valley on May 7. In the administrative history's words, "By the time Jordan returned to Stanford, Representative Needham had already acted." On May 23 Jordan wrote Hain that Secretary of the Interior Hitchcock had told him the Pinnacles were withdrawn from sale.

**A reluctant Forest Service.** Gifford Pinchot's Forest Service was no champion of the idea. Writing to Jordan on February 9, 1905, Pinchot reported that a Bureau agent had found the tract worth permanent reservation, "but that its picturesque qualities alone would not warrant the Government in spending money to protect and administer it." Jordan's reply was lukewarm too: the tract "has no value for other than scenic purposes… although none of it ranks with the scenery of the high Sierras." Pinchot approved the reserve anyway, but he stayed ambivalent, and after the monument was created he moved to abolish the reserve.

**The paper trail.** The formal recommendation came from Forest Inspector George Wilcox Peavy, who filed a favorable report on September 9, 1907. His reasoning was about place and cost. "If the objects described in this report were located in Yellowstone National Park they would excite but little comment, but by virtue of their position, remote from other natural objects of interest, they are peculiarly striking and should be preserved from private control, for the benefit of the people," he wrote, adding that "there would … be but little expense connected with the preservation of the Monument." Secretary of Agriculture James Wilson recommended the monument to the Secretary of the Interior on January 8, 1908, "with boundaries as indicated on the attached diagram. The area is, approximately, 2,080 acres." On January 14 Secretary of the Interior James R. Garfield sent the President a draft proclamation: "The extraordinary spire-like and other peculiar formations, and the large natural caves thereunder render the locality of much scientific interest. Its reservation for the benefit and enjoyment of the people is earnestly recommended." Roosevelt signed two days later.

So the monument came from a local push, carried through a Forest Service that was frankly unenthusiastic about scenery it could not justify on practical grounds. The land was public, and no commercial interest was fighting over it.

**The rock.** Nobody in 1908 understood where the rock came from. The spires are the eroded remains of a volcanic field that erupted about 23 million years ago far to the southeast. The San Andreas Fault later split the field and carried this piece roughly 195 miles northwest to where it stands today. The other half lies, in the words of a 2000 presidential proclamation, "195 miles to the southeast in northern Los Angeles County." Geologists matched the two halves in the 1970s, some six decades after Roosevelt signed the monument for its scenery.

## Political impact
Pinnacles caused no controversy, and it did not go unnoticed. The proclamation moved on the ordinary national wire and turned up in roughly twenty California papers between January and March 1908, more often than not on the front page. The *Salinas Index Journal* had it first, on January 18: "PINNACLES NATIONAL MONUMENT / THE JAGGED PEAKS PROTECTED BY PRESIDENT ROOSEVELT / Hon. J. C. Needham at Head of Movement." The *San Francisco Call* followed the next day, and the item spread from San Mateo and Ventura to Modesto, Los Angeles, Stockton, Hanford, Eureka, Riverside and Truckee. The *Truckee Republican* of February 8 ran the one openly editorial headline: "PINNACLES MADE NATIONAL MONUMENT / Another Thing Done By Roosevelt that is Worthy Our Applause." Beyond California the item ran as short filler in papers as far apart as South Dakota, Vermont, Illinois and Iowa.

Every item found is favorable or neutral. No litigation has turned up, and the monument displaced no rancher or logger. It was one of a cluster Roosevelt signed that winter, after Muir Woods on January 9 ([Muir Woods National Monument, California](story:4)) and the Grand Canyon on January 11 ([Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3)), and it passed without recorded opposition. Plenty of Californians noticed it, and they approved: a piece of the public domain had been set aside because a persistent local advocate made the case and nobody with the power to object had reason to.

## Legacy
**Out of the forest.** The Pinnacles National Forest did not last. Executive Order 908, effective July 1, 1908, folded it into the Monterey National Forest. In October 1909, Raymond Tyler, the forest supervisor, urged that the Pinnacles lands be dropped from that forest, the opposite of the role the forest officers behind the Lassen and Gila monuments had played. President Taft's proclamation of December 12, 1910 redrew the Monterey boundaries and removed the monument's land, while providing that this "does not affect the reservation of said land as a National Monument." From then on the monument was under the Department of the Interior.

**Growth.** Later presidents and Congresses enlarged Pinnacles again and again: Harding in 1923, Coolidge in 1924, Hoover in 1931, Franklin Roosevelt in 1933 and 1941, Congress in 1976 (which also created the Pinnacles Wilderness), and Clinton in 2000, with smaller adjustments in 1980 and 2002. The roughly 2,080 acres of 1908 have grown to about 26,700.

**Condors.** Since December 20, 2003, when the first two captive-bred California condors were released there, Pinnacles has been part of the California Condor Recovery Program. It is the only National Park Service unit that manages a release site for captive-bred condors, and it co-manages central California's wild condors with the Ventana Wildlife Society. At the end of 2025 the Fish and Wildlife Service counted 607 California condors, 392 in the wild and 215 in captivity.

**A national park.** On January 10, 2013, President Obama signed the Pinnacles National Park Act, which redesignated the monument a national park. The act also renamed the 1976 wilderness the Hain Wilderness, after the homesteader who started it all. The wilderness now covers about 16,048 acres, roughly two-thirds of the park.

Roosevelt signed this small scenic monument for its "scientific interest," and it turned out to be better founded than anyone in 1908 could have known. The two things it is now known for, plate tectonics written in the rock and a giant vulture brought back from the edge of extinction, came much later: the geology about six decades on, the condors nearly a century.

## At a glance
On June 8, 1908, Roosevelt appointed the National Conservation Commission to take stock of the country's natural resources. The same day he signed Executive Order 809, telling every federal department and bureau to hand the commission whatever information it asked for. The commission had no money and no staff of its own, so this order was how it would get its work done. Around it were a naval reservation in the Florida Keys, a commission on ship safety, a push to standardize federal pay, and a reshuffling of national forests in Idaho and Utah.

## Background
Executive Order 809 reads in full:

> A national conservation commission to consider and advise the President upon the condition and needs of the natural resources of the country has been appointed by the President. The heads of the executive departments, bureaus, and other government establishments are hereby instructed to secure, compile, and furnish to the said commission all such information and data relevant to its work as the commission may from time to time request, and as may be respectively within the lawful powers of such departments, bureaus, and government establishments to secure, compile, or furnish, and not inconsistent with express provisions of law.
>
> Theodore Roosevelt. The White House, *June 8, 1908.*

The order did not create the commission. Roosevelt did that by letter the same day, and the letter promised what came next: "I shall issue an executive order directing them to give such help as the commission may need." Executive Order 809 kept that promise. It opened the government's files to the commission and turned the existing bureaus into its research arm.

Like other orders of the time, these were issued without captions. The titles attached to them today are headings added by later compilers. The rest of the run included:

- **Man Key and Woman Key.** Executive Order 808 (June 8) reserved parts of these two keys in Key West Harbor for the Navy. Its text has not been found, but a later order describes the keys as "reserved for naval purposes by Executive Order of June 8, 1908." In 1909 the Navy's Chief of Ordnance reported fuel-oil tanks proposed there for the Key West station.
- **Ship safety.** Executive Order 794 (May 12) created a commission to examine the laws "for the better security of the lives of passengers and crews on board vessels of the United States, with a view to their revision."
- **Federal pay.** Executive Order 795 (May 13) directed department heads to bring salaries into line, "as nearly as may be, to the principles underlying" the report of the Committee on Grades and Salaries. Roosevelt admitted in the order that "it will, of course, be impossible to do more than approximate this plan." He framed it as action taken "In the absence of action by Congress on the general subject of departmental grades." Executive Order 804 (May 29) extended it to clerks and draftsmen at arsenals and navy yards. The committee itself had been created by Executive Order 651 of June 11, 1907, to carry out the Keep Commission's salary recommendations ([The Executive Orders of Summer 1907](story:93)).
- **Forests.** Two orders signed May 26, both effective July 1, together dissolved the Bear River National Forest. Executive Order 801 moved its western Malad Division, along with the whole Port Neuf National Forest, into the Pocatello National Forest. Executive Order 802 renamed what remained, the Bear River Range core, the Cache National Forest.
- **Routine business.** Appointments without examination, Navy yard hours, Interstate Commerce Commission staffing, and Executive Order 810½ (June 8), which canceled the orders naming Major General F. C. Ainsworth and Brigadier General J. Franklin Bell to act for the Secretary of War in his absence.

## Historical context
**The conservation moment.** Executive Order 809 sits at what historians treat as the high-water mark of the conservation movement. From May 13 to 15, 1908, Roosevelt gathered governors, his Cabinet, the Supreme Court, and members of Congress at the White House for a conference on the conservation of natural resources ([The Conference of Governors and the National Conservation Commission, 1908](story:28)). It was the first time a President had called the nation's governors together at the White House. The printed *Proceedings* list seven of the nine Cabinet members (Taft was in Panama, and Metcalf was on the Pacific coast with the fleet) and all nine justices. Fifty-two jurisdictions were represented, but twelve governors were absent, so roughly thirty-nine chief executives came in person.

Out of the conference came the National Conservation Commission, with Gifford Pinchot as chairman of the whole body and of its executive committee. It was organized in four sections: waters, forests, lands, and minerals. Congress had given it no money and no staff. The only way to do the work was to draw on the bureaus that already held the data, such as the Geological Survey, the Forest Service, the Census, and the General Land Office. Executive Order 809 made that lawful and routine.

**Safety at sea.** The ship-safety commission grew out of a different worry. When the excursion steamer *General Slocum* burned in New York's East River in June 1904, killing more than a thousand people, the investigating commission recommended a standing body to examine the laws protecting steamboat passengers. Roosevelt endorsed the idea with "particular emphasis" in his transmittal letter of October 12, 1904. Congress never created one. Nearly four years later, Roosevelt created one himself. Executive Order 794 named five men: Captain Adolph Marix of the Navy, chairman of the Light-House Board, as chairman; Solicitor Charles Earl of Commerce and Labor; Commissioner of Navigation Eugene T. Chamberlain; Supervising Inspector-General George Uhler of the Steamboat-Inspection Service, who had also served on the *Slocum* commission; and Commander William Strother Smith. Neither the order nor the commission's February 1909 report, *Better Security of Life at Sea*, mentions the *Slocum*. The sequence is clear, but the documents do not say the one caused the other.

## Political impact
The conservation orders stirred no controversy at first; the fight came later. Congress resented Roosevelt's habit of creating bodies it had not authorized. On March 4, 1909, his last day in office, it answered with section 9 of the sundry civil appropriation act, the Tawney amendment. The amendment barred public money for any commission, council, or board "unless the creation of the same shall be or shall have been authorized by law." It also barred lending staff from executive departments to such bodies. That second part killed the National Conservation Commission, which ran entirely on unpaid commissioners and borrowed federal staff.

In his *Autobiography*, Roosevelt wrote that the amendment "forbade the President to appoint any further Commissions unless specifically authorized by Congress to do so," and that had he remained in office he "would refuse to obey it." That was his reading. The law itself cut off money and staff rather than banning appointments outright. In June 1908 that reckoning was still ahead, and Executive Order 809 passed as a simple administrative instruction.

The pay orders were part of the Keep Commission's quiet drive for efficiency and drew little notice. The forest changes in Idaho and Utah were routine boundary work, coming in a year when the great forest-reserve battles were already behind ([The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)).

## Legacy
**The inventory.** The commission did not last, but its work did. Its three-volume *Report*, transmitted January 22, 1909, and printed as a Senate document, was in Roosevelt's words "the first inventory of its natural resources ever made by any nation." In the same message he granted that "it is not necessary that this inventory should be exact in every minute detail." Congress refused to fund the commission's continuation. Even so, the report and the conference behind it shaped resource policy for decades and are usually counted among the lasting achievements of his presidency.

**The forests.** The Cache National Forest was never abolished. In 1973 its administration was split along the Utah–Idaho line. The Utah portion, roughly four-fifths of the forest, merged administratively with the Wasatch National Forest, and in 2007 that pairing joined the Uinta to become today's Uinta-Wasatch-Cache National Forest, where the Cache lands make up the Logan and Ogden Ranger Districts. The Idaho portion went to the Caribou National Forest, now the Caribou-Targhee, as the Montpelier Ranger District. The Pocatello name was dropped in 1915, when that forest went into the Cache. The old Port Neuf lands are today the Westside Ranger District of the Caribou-Targhee.

**Man Key and Woman Key.** These two keys now lie within the Key West National Wildlife Refuge and the Florida Keys Wilderness, designated by Congress in 1975. The refuge was Roosevelt's own creation: Executive Order 923 of August 8, 1908, made the Key West Reservation a preserve and breeding ground for native birds, and expressly left standing the naval reservation he had made on the same two keys two months earlier. He wrapped a bird sanctuary around a Navy claim and left both in place. The two overlapped on paper for seventy-four years. In 1982, Public Land Order 6214 revoked Executive Order 808, along with a 1924 naval reservation covering the same area, and confirmed that the lands remain withdrawn for the Key West National Wildlife Refuge under Executive Order 923.

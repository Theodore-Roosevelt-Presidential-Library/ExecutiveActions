## At a glance
In the last week of June 1903, with a one-year window about to close, Roosevelt decided which government land in Puerto Rico the United States would keep. Proclamation 502 reserved naval ground in San Juan harbor and on the island of Culebra. Proclamation 503 kept sites for a federal building, a marine hospital, custom houses and a ring of light-house stations. Everything left over passed to the people of Puerto Rico.

## Background
When the United States took Puerto Rico under the Treaty of Paris in 1898, it inherited the Spanish crown's public lands and buildings. The Foraker Act of 1900 had handed the island government roads, bridges, harbor shores and similar property, but left the public lands and buildings themselves unsettled. The Act of July 1, 1902 closed that gap. It gave the President one year to reserve whatever he deemed necessary "for military, naval, light-house, marine-hospital, post-offices, custom-houses, United States courts, and other public purposes." Everything else went to the government of Puerto Rico, "to be held or disposed of for the use and benefit of the people of said island," on condition that the island release its claim to whatever the President kept. The island's Legislative Assembly authorized that release by an act approved February 16, 1903.

Roosevelt used the power at least six times. The same statute supported the Luquillo Forest Reserve in January 1903 (see [The Forest Reserves of 1903](story:42)). Then came a burst of orders in the window's final days:

- **Proclamation 502 (June 26)** reserved for naval purposes an 80-acre strip along the Caguas Road in Puerta de Tierra, running from 250 feet north of the road south to the harbor shore, with an exception for railroad or highway access the Secretary of the Navy might allow; the whole of Isla Grande (or Manglar) in San Juan harbor; the public lands and structures on Puntilla Point; the Park and the Presidio south of the "Scarf wall"; and all federal lands and buildings on Culebra and its keys, defined by a box of latitude and longitude.
- **Executive Order 218 (June 29)** reserved property at Ponce, Mayagüez and San Juan for the federal courts, district attorney and marshal.
- **Proclamation 503 (June 30)** reserved the civilian federal footprint: a vacant public-building site in San Juan, a marine-hospital parcel near San Antonio bridge, the custom houses at Ponce, Mayagüez and Humacao, a string of light-house and buoy tracts at San Juan, and light-house stations ringing the coast from Cape San Juan to Mona and Cardona islands.
- **Executive Order 219 (June 30)** added no land. It kept for the President the right to amend Proclamation 502, and invited the Secretary of the Navy and the Governor of Puerto Rico to set down on paper how the reserved ground would be used.
- **Executive Order 220 (June 30)** pulled the Presidio back out of the naval reservation.
- **An unnumbered order of June 30** reserved lands "for military purposes": the Aibonito and Aguadilla reservations, a Fajardo reservation taking in Palominos island and the Cordilleras, Henry Barracks near Cayey, the Mayagüez reservations, the San Juan military reservations, and a San Juan harbor reservation of Cabras, Cañuelo and Punta Salinas.

Two details in the texts stand out. The first is the Presidio. It was San Juan's penitentiary, the island's prison, and it was swept into the naval reservation on June 26. Four days later Roosevelt ordered "that certain building and tract of land known as The Presidio or penitentiary at San Juan" withdrawn "from any and all effect" of the proclamation. The order gives no reason, and who asked for it is not known.

The second is San Juan's custom house. Proclamation 503 reserves the custom houses at Ponce, Mayagüez and Humacao outright. At San Juan it gives the Customs service only "the right until otherwise ordered to the use of the lots and buildings within the Puntilla point," because the Puntilla had just been reserved to the Navy. Read together, the two proclamations show the Treasury as a tenant on Navy ground. Neither proclamation reserves anything for the Army; the Army's share came in the separate unnumbered order.

## Historical context
The question was who would own the government property of a former Spanish colony, and some of the people asking for a naval share were Puerto Ricans. On January 7, 1902, the island's Executive Council and House of Delegates petitioned Congress for "the naval station projected by the Federal Government in the waters of Porto Rico." The island, they argued, lay "between the mainland of the United States and South America and on the routes to the isthmian canal, with harbors which afford protection to vessels at all times and in circumstances of war or peace," which gave it "unequaled claims." They resolved that "the people of Porto Rico will cede possession to any property, buildings, or appurtenances belonging to the government of Porto Rico, as they may be required from time to time." The February 1903 release act followed through on that offer. Island leaders wanted the base, and the jobs and federal spending it promised.

The Navy's reasons were strategic. Congress had committed the country to an isthmian canal by the Spooner Act of June 28, 1902, though in June 1903 the treaty with Colombia still awaited action in Bogotá and nothing about the route was settled.

Culebra, a small island between Puerto Rico and St. Thomas with the sheltered harbor of Ensenada Honda, was not a new choice. An executive order of December 19, 1901 had already placed its public lands "under the jurisdiction of the Navy Department," at the Navy's request (see [The Unnumbered Orders of 1901–1902](story:111)). That winter of 1902–1903, as the Venezuela blockade crisis brought Admiral Dewey's combined fleet into the Caribbean, a Marine battalion landed on Culebra for advance-base maneuvers. The 1903 proclamation confirmed and fixed the boundaries of a Navy presence that already existed, and its box ran slightly less far east than the 1901 order's.

The light-house tracts came from the Light-House Board: the proclamation says their maps and plats were "prepared by the Light House Bureau of the Third Light House District of Porto Rico."

## Political impact
The proclamations made the wire, though only as a brief. On June 30, 1903, a dispatch on Proclamation 502 ran in the *Los Angeles Herald* under "PORTO RICO LANDS / President Makes First Reservation For Naval Purposes," and on page one of the *San Bernardino Sun* and the Bakersfield *Morning Echo*. The *Santa Barbara Weekly Press* carried it on July 2. The *Sun* garbled it into "the Park and Presidio on the Island of Culebra." No editorial comment has been found, and searches of Colorado and New York papers turned up nothing on the reservations. What the record shows is a short wire item, without national attention or controversy.

Governor William H. Hunt's next annual report noted the deadline's force: the act of Congress gave the island "all lands not reserved by the President prior to July 1, 1903." The debates over Puerto Rico in these years concerned its political status, tariffs and citizenship. No organized objection to these reservations has been found.

Still, the settlement was less tidy than its texts look. The Presidio went in and came out within four days. And the Caguas Road tract did not hold: an act of March 4, 1907 readjusted the naval boundaries, and Roosevelt's Proclamation 819 of August 4, 1908 returned most of the 80 acres to the island, keeping the naval hospital and wireless station (see [The Residual 1908 Proclamations](story:107)).

## Legacy
The civilian reservations became the ordinary furniture of federal Puerto Rico: custom houses, the Marine Hospital Service, and a light-house system, several of whose stations named in the proclamation still stand.

The naval reservations had a longer and more contested afterlife. The Navy did not build on Isla Grande for more than three decades. The Navy's own wartime construction history states that "Except for a radio station and hydrographic office, the Navy had no installation on the island of Puerto Rico prior to 1939." On October 30, 1939, a contract was awarded to build a naval air station there, on ground that held a Pan American Airlines airstrip and a Public Health Service quarantine station and was otherwise "a combination of mangrove swamp and tidal mud flats." The site later became San Juan's Isla Grande Airport, today Fernando Luis Ribas Dominicci Airport.

Culebra's story ran longest. In 1909 Roosevelt laid a bird reservation over the surrounding islands of the Culebra group, the root of today's Culebra National Wildlife Refuge (see [The Bird Reservations of February 1909](story:105)). The Navy and Marines used the archipelago for landing exercises and, from the late 1930s, as a gunnery and bombing range. By 1970 residents were openly demanding that the Navy leave. Senator Edmund Muskie entered press accounts into the *Congressional Record* reporting that 304 of 313 families had asked the Navy to go, and quoting Mayor Ramón Feliciano: "The Navy must leave Culebra in peace." Protest and civil disobedience followed in 1971. Training ended on September 30, 1975, and operations moved to Vieques. That long dispute did not begin in June 1903, since the Navy was already on Culebra, but Proclamation 502 is the instrument that fixed its boundaries under the law that settled title.

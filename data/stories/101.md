## At a glance
Between June 30 and July 2, 1908, Roosevelt signed about fifty more executive orders that finished reorganizing the national forests, work begun two weeks earlier ([The Great Reorganization of the National Forests, June 1908](story:99)). Nearly all took effect on July 1, the first day of the 1909 fiscal year. The run ended with two capstone orders: Executive Order 908, which merged or renamed dozens of forests in one stroke, and Executive Order 909, which split the old Grand Canyon National Forest along the Colorado River into the Kaibab on the north side and the Coconino on the south.

## Background
The orders were drafted to land on the start of the fiscal year, and they say so on their face. The June 30 orders, the July 1 block and even Executive Order 909, signed July 2, all read "on and after July 1, 1908."

The work came in three waves:

- **June 30: Oregon and Wyoming.** The Siuslaw was carved from parts of the Tillamook and Umpqua (Executive Order 860); the Cheyenne in Wyoming from part of the Medicine Bow plus the whole Crow Creek (861); the Oregon from part of the Cascade plus the whole Bull Run (864); and the Crater, which spanned Oregon and California, from the Ashland and parts of the Cascade, Siskiyou and Klamath (867). Another order dealt with the Cascade (863). Executive Order 896, for the Beartooth in Montana, was signed the same day; its number falls out of order among the July 1 orders, but it too took effect July 1.
- **July 1: the interior West.** A long block redrew forests in Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Utah and Wyoming, among them the Sitgreaves, Tonto, Apache, Targhee, Teton, Absaroka, Beaverhead, Rio Grande, Pike, San Juan, Uncompahgre, Gunnison and Shoshone.
- **July 2: California and the capstones.** Orders squared up the Tahoe, Mono, Inyo, Trinity, Stanislaus, Sierra, Shasta, Sequoia, Plumas, Lassen and California forests.

Like the June orders, these were mostly about names and lines. Each turns on the phrase "shall be known as," and most say they release no land and reserve no new land. But land did change hands at the margins. Executive Order 865 releases no land "except the areas indicated as eliminations," and directs that the excluded Umpqua land "shall be restored to the public domain and become subject to settlement under the general provisions of the homestead laws." Even the Grand Canyon split shed some acreage, returned to the public domain by a companion proclamation (Proclamation 818).

**The omnibus order.** Executive Order 908 of July 2 reorganized forests wholesale in a single document. The government's numbered index summarizes it as "55 specified national forests consolidated and/or renamed." A Forest Service appendix cites it in 79 individual forest entries, counting both sides of each merger, so the two figures measure different things. No text of the order has been found. Among its results:

- New forests assembled from smaller ones: the Alamo in New Mexico (from the Guadalupe and Sacramento); the Coronado in Arizona and New Mexico (from the Dragoon, Santa Catalina and Santa Rita); and the Sioux in Montana and South Dakota (from the Cave Hills, Short Pine, Slim Buttes, Ekalaka and Long Pine). The Cleveland, Humboldt, Moapa, Minidoka, Jefferson and Nebraska also date from it.
- Alaska consolidations: the Afognak into the Chugach, and the Alexander Archipelago into the Tongass.
- Pure name changes: Big Horn to Bighorn, Fish Lake to Fishlake, Aquarius to Powell, Otter to Custer, Imnaha to Wallowa, and Pecos River to Pecos.

**The Grand Canyon order.** Executive Order 909 reads in full, as transcribed:

> It is hereby ordered that on and after July 1, 1908, the land within the boundary shown on the attached diagram, heretofore set apart, reserved, and proclaimed as a part of the Grand Canyon National Forest, shall be known as the Kaibab National Forest. The remaining portion of the Grand Canyon National Forest, with the exception of the area indicated as eliminated on Part Two of the diagram of the Coconino National Forest, shall remain reserved and constitute a part of the Coconino National Forest. It is not intended by this order to modify the proclamations hertofore issued establishing the Grand Canyon National Game Preserve and the Grand Canyon National Monument, both of which include land embraced in the boundaries of such Kaibab National Forest. It is not intended by this order to release any land from reservation or to reserve any land not heretofore embraced in a National Forest.
>
> Theodore Roosevelt. The White House, *July 2, 1908.*

The order took unusual care to say that two special reservations already overlapping that ground were untouched: the game preserve of 1906 ([Grand Canyon National Game Preserve, Arizona](story:31)) and the national monument of January 1908 ([Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3)). The forest boundaries moved; the preserve and the monument stayed exactly as proclaimed. ("National Game Preserve" is Executive Order 909's name for the 1906 reservation; the 1906 proclamation itself speaks of lands "within the Grand Canyon Forest Reserve, lying north and west of the Colorado River.")

## Historical context
The reasoning was the same as in June and came from Gifford Pinchot's Forest Service: break the enormous early reserves into units small enough for one supervisor to know and manage on the ground, and turn the whole system over at the start of the fiscal year. The term "national forests" was only a year old. An appropriations act of March 4, 1907 had funded the Secretary of Agriculture to study "forestry, forest reserves, which shall be known hereafter as national forests, forest fires, and lumbering," a renaming tucked into a funding line rather than written as a law of its own.

Geography set the July orders apart. The June work fell heavily on the six "Fulton Amendment" states (Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado and Wyoming), where Roosevelt could no longer create or enlarge reserves and could only rename what already existed. The July orders reached beyond them into Arizona, California and Nevada. New Mexico and South Dakota came in only through the omnibus Executive Order 908; their other 1908 changes had been made on June 26, to the Jemez and the Black Hills. Either way, these were reorganizations of existing reserves, not new withdrawals.

## Political impact
The July reorganization, like the June one, drew little public notice, and no press reaction of its own has been found. It moved names and boundaries on ground that was already federal, so there was no local "lock-up" grievance to rally against. That fight had been fought the year before over the Fulton Amendment and the "midnight forests" of 1907 ([The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)). The little land that left the forests went back to the public domain. Executive Order 865 directed that the Umpqua land it cut out "shall be restored to the public domain and become subject to settlement under the general provisions of the homestead laws," and part of the old Grand Canyon forest went back to the public domain under the companion Proclamation 818.

Where the orders mattered most was inside the Forest Service, which ended the summer of 1908 with a rationalized map of supervisor-sized national forests. The sequence is worth getting straight. The Forest Service's six numbered districts had been created in May 1907, fourteen months before these orders, but they were run from Washington. Staffed district headquarters at Missoula, Denver, Albuquerque, Ogden, San Francisco and Portland opened on December 1, 1908, five months after. The boundary work came first; the field offices to run the result came after.

## Legacy
Many of the names signed in these three days are still on the map, though "still on the map" and "still its own national forest" are not the same thing.

### The forests today
- **Separate national forests:** the Kaibab, Coconino and Tonto in the Southwest; the Sequoia, Lassen, Inyo and Stanislaus in California; and the Shoshone, Rio Grande and San Juan in the Rocky Mountains.
- **Still legally distinct, but sharing a supervisor with a neighbor:** the Sitgreaves (with the Apache since 1974, as the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests, the plural "Forests" being the tell), the Shasta (with the Trinity, 1954), the Pike (with the San Isabel, 1975) and the Gunnison (with the Grand Mesa and Uncompahgre, 1976, the forests known as the GMUG). Their proclaimed boundaries still exist. What changed is who runs them; none was abolished or renamed.

### Names that did not last
The Alamo folded into the Lincoln in 1919. The Cheyenne went back to the Medicine Bow in 1910. The Oregon became Mount Hood in 1924, by Executive Order 3944. The Crater became the Rogue River in 1932, the Cascade merged into the Willamette in 1933, and the Mono was split between the Inyo and the Toiyabe in 1945.

### The rest, as of 1981
A listing of the national forests published in 1983 traces the other forests named in these orders through 1981:

- **Still separate under their 1908 names:** the Siuslaw, Targhee, Beaverhead, Tahoe, Sierra, Plumas, Siskiyou, Cleveland, Coronado, Humboldt, Bighorn, Fishlake, Custer, Chugach and Tongass. The Tongass was divided administratively into three units in 1974.
- **Combined administratively with a neighbor:** the Teton (with the Bridger, 1973), the Apache (with the Sitgreaves, 1974), the Uncompahgre (with the Grand Mesa and Gunnison, 1976), the Trinity (with the Shasta, 1954), the Wallowa (with the Whitman, 1954) and the Nebraska (administered with the Samuel R. McKelvie from 1971 as the Nebraska National Forests).
- **Renamed:** the California became the Mendocino in 1932.
- **Absorbed or divided:** the Moapa into the Toiyabe (1915); the Pecos with the Jemez into the Santa Fe (1915); the Sioux into the Custer (1920); the Beartooth between the Absaroka and the Custer (1932); the Montana Jefferson into the Lewis and Clark (1932); the Powell into the Dixie (1944); the Absaroka between the Gallatin and the Lewis and Clark (1945); and the Minidoka into the Sawtooth (1953).

That listing stops in 1981, and later combinations are not traced here. Present-day acreages for each forest belong to the Forest Service's annual *Land Areas Report*.

### The Grand Canyon line
Executive Order 909's line along the Colorado River still shapes how the canyon country is managed. The Kaibab National Forest (about 1.6 million acres) and the Coconino (more than 1.8 million acres) remain separate, and the Kaibab wraps Grand Canyon National Park on both rims: the North Kaibab Ranger District runs right up to the park boundary on the north, and the Tusayan district sits on the south.

### The unit that lasted
The compact, individually named national forest that these orders finished building is still the Forest Service's basic unit of administration. The districts that grouped them became regions in 1930.

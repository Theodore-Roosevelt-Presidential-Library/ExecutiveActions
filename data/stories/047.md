## At a glance
On October 31, 1903, Roosevelt named Thursday, November 26, as the national day of thanksgiving. His proclamation reads less like a boast than a short civic sermon: it treats the country's good fortune as a trust, borrows Lincoln's "government of the people, by the people, for the people," and says nothing at all about plenty in a year when the economy was sliding. Newspapers from California to Colorado to New York printed it, and at least one editor read it as a jab at the postal scandal then unfolding in Washington.

## Background
Each autumn the President named a day and asked the country to stop and give thanks. That custom had not always been steady. After George Washington's proclamation of 1789 the observance came and went for decades, and no President issued one at all between 1815 and 1863. The unbroken yearly chain began with Abraham Lincoln's proclamation of October 3, 1863. Lincoln named "the last Thursday of November next," but he fixed no permanent date: Andrew Johnson chose the first Thursday of December in 1865, and Ulysses S. Grant chose November 18 in 1869. What Roosevelt inherited was a habit unbroken since 1863, on a date set by custom alone. The fuller story is told in [Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20).

Proclamation 508, Roosevelt's third, followed custom and picked the last Thursday of November. Like every proclamation of the period, it has no title of its own. In the *Statutes at Large* (33 Stat. 2322) it is simply "[No. 10.] By the President of the United States of America. A PROCLAMATION.," with a margin note reading "Thursday, November 26, 1903, set apart as a day of national thanksgiving." "Thanksgiving Day, 1903" is a caption added by later editors. Secretary of State John Hay countersigned it.

The tone had shifted from year to year. The 1901 message was written in mourning for McKinley. The 1902 message ([Thanksgiving Day, 1902](story:41)) spoke of a year "of peace and of overflowing plenty." The 1903 text gives thanks for peace and for "the chance for our citizens to work for their welfare unhindered by war, famine or plague," and leaves plenty out.

## Historical context
Roosevelt framed the country's blessings as something to be earned after the fact. "It behooves us not only to rejoice greatly because of what has been given us," the proclamation reads, "but to accept it with a solemn sense of responsibility." The American experiment in self-government, it says, was being tried "on so vast a scale" that failure "would mean loss of hope for all who believe in the power and the righteousness of liberty." The phrase "government of the people, by the people, for the people" comes word for word from the Gettysburg Address, though the proclamation does not say so. Turning a ceremonial occasion into a lesson in civic duty was very much Roosevelt's habit.

The silence on prosperity fit the year. 1903 was a year of contraction. The National Bureau of Economic Research dates a business-cycle peak to September 1902 and the next trough to August 1904. Stock prices spent 1903 in the slide later nicknamed the "rich man's panic," bottoming on November 9, 1903, after the Dow Jones average had lost nearly half its value from its 1901 peak.

Washington was not calm either. The Post Office Department was in the middle of a corruption scandal. In May Roosevelt had agreed to the removal of August W. Machen, head of the free-delivery service, and an investigation was producing bribery indictments through the summer and fall. A plea that "our hearts may be roused to war steadfastly for good and against all the forces of evil, public and private" landed in that atmosphere.

Abroad, the "peace" the proclamation gives thanks for was real but recent and qualified. An international tribunal had signed its award on the Alaska boundary dispute with Great Britain and Canada only eleven days earlier, on October 20. The canal question was unsettled after Colombia's congress rejected the Hay–Herrán treaty that summer. Three days after the proclamation was signed, Panama declared its independence from Colombia (November 3, 1903), with United States warships already off the isthmus. "Peace at home and abroad" was accurate on the day the proclamation was dated, and under strain within the week.

## Political impact
The proclamation carried no policy, but it was a wire story the day it was signed. The *Los Angeles Herald* printed it in full on November 1 under a Washington dateline of October 31, with headlines announcing that the President had set Thursday, November 26. The *Stockton Mail* put it on page one on November 2 ("THANKSGIVING PROCLAMATION. Roosevelt Tells Why the People Should Be Grateful"), the *Santa Cruz Surf* and *Marysville Democrat* ran it the same day, and small-town California papers from Marin and Hanford to Folsom and Alturas followed over the next ten days. In the California Digital Newspaper Collection alone, "Thanksgiving proclamation" turns up fifty-one times in November 1903, though some of those are Governor George Pardee's state proclamation or advertisements borrowing the form.

The coverage was national. On November 1 the Denver *Rocky Mountain News* ran it as "PRESIDENT ROOSEVELT CALLS ON NATION TO GIVE THANKS," the Leadville *Herald Democrat* as "PRAISED GOD FOR BLESSINGS SHOWERED ON LAND OF THE FREE," and the Pueblo *Colorado Daily Chieftain* as "THANKSGIVING DAY President Appoints Thursday, Nov. 26, by Proclamation." The *Ouray Herald* followed on November 13. In New York State it reached page one of the *Otsego Republican* (November 4) and the *Massena Observer* (November 5), along with papers from Plattsburgh and Johnstown to New Rochelle.

Some editors read between the lines. The *Alameda Encinal* of November 10 tied the proclamation to the postal scandal, suggesting the President had the grafters in mind when he asked for hearts roused to war against "the forces of evil, public and private," and predicting that the administration's postal cleanup would be so thorough that Congress would have little left to do but listen to Democratic speeches. The *San Francisco Call*, in a Sunday piece on November 22, urged readers to study the proclamation and notice its strongly religious tone. A partisan jab from the *Woodland Democrat*, reprinted in the *Napa Journal* the same day, forecast that Roosevelt would write only one more Thanksgiving proclamation, in other words that he would lose in 1904. One headline got it wrong: the *Stanislaus County Weekly News* of November 13 said the President "Sets Forth Our Great Prosperity" in his proclamation, which the text never does.

On Thanksgiving Day congregations in Stockton, Truckee, Santa Cruz, Cloverdale, Napa and Eureka heard it read aloud. No objection to the proclamation itself has turned up in the California, Colorado or New York collections.

## Legacy
Like the other yearly Thanksgiving proclamations, this one has no separate legacy of its own. It is one link in the continuous chain of presidential Thanksgiving messages told more fully in [Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20). The date went on resting on custom, usually the last Thursday of November, until Franklin Roosevelt moved the holiday a week earlier in 1939, 1940 and 1941. A joint resolution of Congress approved December 26, 1941, then fixed it on the fourth Thursday.

Two cautions for anyone quoting the 1903 proclamation today. First, take the text from the *Statutes at Large*. The widely used American Presidency Project transcription opens with "it fails upon the President" where the official printing reads "it falls upon the President," and it drops the seal. Newspaper reprints of the wire text also differ slightly, most often printing "with cleanliness, fearlessness and wisdom do our allotted work on earth." ("Cleanliness" is the *Statutes*' own word.) Second, credit "of the people, by the people, for the people" to Lincoln, whose words Roosevelt borrowed.

## At a glance
On September 14, 1901, hours after taking the oath of office in a private library in Buffalo, Theodore Roosevelt signed his first proclamation as President. It announced the murder of William McKinley and set aside Thursday, September 19, the day of the funeral at Canton, as a national day of mourning and prayer. It made no policy. Its whole job was to help a stunned country grieve together.

## Background
President William McKinley was shot shortly after four in the afternoon of September 6, 1901, while greeting the public in the Temple of Music at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York. His attacker, Leon Czolgosz, admitted to Buffalo police under questioning that he was an anarchist. McKinley lingered for eight days and died at 2:15 in the morning of September 14 at the Milburn house on Delaware Avenue.

Roosevelt was far from Buffalo when the end came. He heard of the shooting at Isle La Motte on Lake Champlain in Vermont, hurried to Buffalo, and stayed four days. When the doctors grew hopeful, he left for the Adirondacks on purpose. As his host Ansley Wilcox recorded, the Vice President went away "in order to impress the public with that confidence in the outcome which everyone then felt." When the summons came, Roosevelt was on the summit of Mount Marcy with guides, and messengers climbed up with the telegram. He rode some thirty-five miles through the night by relays of horses to the railroad at North Creek, and at the station, at about 5:20 a.m., he learned McKinley was already dead.

He reached Buffalo at ten minutes before two that afternoon. At about half past three, "at precisely 3:32 o'clock" by the Associated Press account, Judge John R. Hazel of the United States District Court gave him the oath in the library of the Wilcox house on Delaware Avenue, now the Theodore Roosevelt Inaugural National Historic Site. The proclamation was drafted in that same house later in the afternoon and given to the press that evening.

Newspapers called it his first official act, and it was his first proclamation and the first official document of his presidency. It was not quite the first thing he signed as President, though. Wilcox's account of the day, written in 1902 and, according to the National Park Service, reviewed and approved by Roosevelt himself, notes that once the oath was given, "the written oath, which Judge Hazel produced… was then signed." And just before the oath he had already made his first public commitment, more on that below.

**Who wrote it.** Roosevelt supplied the substance and George B. Cortelyou, McKinley's secretary, whom Roosevelt kept on, put it into official form. Governor B. B. Odell of New York called at the Wilcox house that evening and mentioned his own state proclamation; Roosevelt told him he too would issue one and had handed it to Cortelyou to prepare as to form after drafting the substance himself. Secretary of State John Hay, in Washington, had no hand in it. Wilcox later described Roosevelt rewriting a heavily corrected first draft, tossing it in the wastebasket, and issuing a typed copy; Wilcox fished out the discarded sheets and pasted them into his scrapbook.

A four-page handwritten draft does survive in the Theodore Roosevelt Papers at the Library of Congress, docketed in another hand "Draft of President Roosevelt's 1st Proclamation, Sept. 14, 1901," along with the typed text that came next. The small changes on its pages show the document taking shape:

- "a crime ~~committed~~ not only against the Chief Magistrate" — "committed" written, then struck.
- "of ~~most~~ earnest endeavor for their welfare" — "most" struck.
- "our deep and abiding sorrow" — "and abiding" survives in the draft but is gone from the final text, probably because "our abiding love" already sits one line above.
- The date first read, as best the struck line can be made out, "Friday next, September Twentieth," crossed out and replaced with "Thursday next, September Nineteenth." The day of mourning was settled right on the page.
- "the memory of" and "sorely" were squeezed in between the lines, and a closing "with bitter grief" was struck.

On the typed copy, "next" is added by hand after "Thursday," and "nineteenth" is written in the margin beside the typed "19." The printed text kept "19."

**What it said.** The proclamation opens plainly: "A terrible bereavement has befallen our people. The President of the United States has been struck down; a crime not only against the Chief Magistrate, but against every law-abiding and liberty-loving citizen." McKinley, it says, "crowned a life of largest love for his fellow men, of earnest endeavor for their welfare, by a death of Christian fortitude." It then did one concrete thing. Roosevelt appointed "Thursday next, September 19, the day in which the body of the dead President will be laid in its last earthly resting place, as a day of mourning and prayer throughout the United States," and asked Americans to gather "in their respective places of divine worship." Nothing more was ordered.

**"Done at the City of Washington."** The closing line places the signing in Washington, though Roosevelt was four hundred miles away in Buffalo. That was an engrossing convention, not a statement of where the President stood, and here there is a specific reason for it. At the Cabinet meeting held minutes before the drafting, Wilcox was asked to fetch the volume of *Messages and Papers of the Presidents* containing Chester Arthur's 1881 proclamation on the death of Garfield, and "this was considered in the cabinet meeting." Arthur's proclamation reads "Done at the city of Washington." The model text was on the table. The full seventeen-word dating formula, "in the year of our Lord, one thousand nine hundred and one, and of the independence of the United States the one hundred and twenty-sixth," was already in the text released in Buffalo that night; the *New-York Tribune* printed it in full the next morning.

The convention was never a fixed rule. No regulation set a closing formula until 1967, and the form adopted then names no place at all.

**Hay's signature.** John Hay countersigned, as Secretaries of State countersigned every proclamation of the period; the Secretary keeps the Great Seal, and whoever held the office that day signed. When Hay was unavailable in April 1902, Roosevelt's San Isabel forest-reserve proclamation went out over the name of David J. Hill as Acting Secretary ([The Forest Reserves of 1902](story:33)). The real sign of continuity was not Hay's signature but the fact that Roosevelt kept Hay, and the rest of McKinley's Cabinet, in office.

## Historical context
McKinley was the third American president murdered in thirty-six years, after Abraham Lincoln in 1865 and James A. Garfield in 1881. This time the shock came with an added fear. Czolgosz's anarchism fed a national dread of violent radicalism loose in the industrial cities. A day of mourning fixed to the funeral was a familiar way for a shaken country to grieve together and to remind itself that the government still stood.

The precedents were recent and specific. All of them were formal proclamations, sealed, countersigned and printed in the *Statutes at Large*. (The common belief that Lincoln's was an executive order is mistaken.) Andrew Johnson issued two: one on April 25, 1865 appointing Thursday, May 25 as a day of fasting, humiliation and mourning, and a second on April 29 moving it to Thursday, June 1, because May 25 "is sacred to large numbers of Christians as one of rejoicing for the ascension of the Savior." Chester Arthur issued one on September 22, 1881, appointing Monday, September 26, Garfield's interment day, countersigned by James G. Blaine.

The differences tell a story. Johnson set his fast day weeks after Lincoln's funeral; Arthur and Roosevelt each chose the funeral day itself. Arthur's proclamation only named the day, with the death announced separately over Blaine's signature, while Roosevelt's single document did both. And the form does not go back further than Lincoln. John Tyler's 1841 paper on William Henry Harrison's death is headed "A Recommendation" and carries no seal; Millard Fillmore's 1850 order on Zachary Taylor closed the departments and prescribed mourning badges but called for no public day of prayer at all.

Roosevelt, at forty-two, was the youngest man ever to become President: 42 years and 322 days old on September 14, 1901. (John F. Kennedy, at 43, was the youngest ever *elected*.) A young, famously energetic successor had every reason to lean on the reassurance of ritual. The proclamation's promise that McKinley's life and death "will remain forever a precious heritage of our people" fit the message Roosevelt pressed in those first days: the office had passed in good order, the late President's memory was safe, and the country's course would hold.

## Political impact
That message had already been spoken aloud before the oath. When Secretary of War Elihu Root formally asked him, on the Cabinet's behalf, to take the oath, Roosevelt replied off the cuff. No official text exists, and the remark does not appear in his collected *Presidential Addresses and State Papers*; reporters took it down by ear. The fullest contemporary version, printed in the *Buffalo Courier* on September 15, 1901, reads:

> "I shall take the oath at once in accord with the request of you members of the Cabinet, and in this hour of our deep and terrible national bereavement I wish to state that it shall be my aim to continue absolutely unbroken the policy of President McKinley for the peace, the prosperity and the honor of our beloved country."

Other papers set the same sentence down differently; the *New-York Tribune*'s version varies in its wording. Only after the oath did Roosevelt ask the Cabinet members present to stay in their places, a separate act he himself described to reporters as "following out the brief statement I made when taking the oath." He kept McKinley's whole Cabinet, John Hay among them; Hay stayed at the State Department until his death in 1905. That pledge did the political work of steadying markets and party. The mourning proclamation was the ceremonial face of the same effort.

The proclamation itself went out fast. The Associated Press carried the full text from Buffalo that night, "President Roosevelt to-night issued the following proclamation," already closing with the Seal and Hay's name, and papers from New York to San Francisco printed it on September 15.

The country answered it with remarkable precision. The Canton service of September 19 was the last stage of a five-day national farewell: a private service at the Milburn house and lying in state at Buffalo City Hall on September 15; the funeral train to Washington on the 16th; state ceremonies under the Capitol dome on the 17th, with a eulogy by Bishop Edward G. Andrews; the train to Canton on the 18th; and the Canton service on the 19th, where the procession entered West Lawn Cemetery at 4:04 in the afternoon. McKinley's body was not buried that day. It was placed in the Werts Receiving Vault there, "pending the time it shall be laid to rest," as the *Saint Paul Globe* put it, with regular Army soldiers on guard until the final interment.

Across the country, people stopped. The *New-York Tribune* of September 20 headlined it "MOTIONLESS AND SILENT / PEOPLE BARE AND BOW THEIR HEADS WHILE BELLS TOLL… ELECTRIC CURRENTS CEASE." At half past three in New York, trolleys, elevated trains, trains on the Brooklyn Bridge, power-house dynamos, and boats on the river and bay all halted for five minutes, and "motionless and silent, with the light wind blowing upon uncovered heads, the city stood for the full five minutes." Telegraph operators nationwide paused for the same five minutes. In Washington, the *Evening Star* reported, "services were held in nearly every church in the city. Stores and business places were closed." Overseas, Westminster Abbey held a service by command of King Edward, and most business houses in Manila closed.

No organized objection to the proclamation's call to worship has turned up in the period press. That silence is weaker evidence than it looks, because the three groups most likely to object were out of print, barely digitized, or shut down. *The American Sentinel*, the leading church-and-state weekly that reliably criticized federal religious proclamations, had stopped publishing in December 1900 and did not return until December 1901, so no religious-liberty paper was in print during the whole mourning period. The freethought press of those weeks is barely digitized. And the anarchist press had been silenced: Abraham Isaak, editor of *Free Society* in Chicago, was arrested with his family within forty-eight hours of the shooting, and police destroyed his presses and seized his library.

For a new and famously energetic President, the first message to the country was deliberately self-effacing: a call to grieve for the man he had replaced.

## Legacy
The proclamation is remembered less for its words than for the moment it marks, the start of the Roosevelt presidency and of the activist, reform-minded government of the Progressive Era. McKinley was entombed in the McKinley National Memorial in Canton in 1907; Roosevelt, by then President in his own right, gave the oration at its dedication that September.

### Closing the door to anarchists
The assassination hardened the country's response to anarchism within eighteen months. The immigration act of March 3, 1903, often called the Anarchist Exclusion Act though the law never uses the phrase, added to the classes barred from entry "anarchists, or persons who believe in or advocate the overthrow by force or violence of the Government of the United States or of all government or of all forms of law, or the assassination of public officials." Another section, reading like a direct answer to Czolgosz, barred anyone "who disbelieves in or who is opposed to all organized government," or who taught the duty or propriety of killing officials because of their office, and the same test was applied to naturalization. These sections governed who could enter the country and become a citizen; they did not, on their own terms, provide for deporting anarchists already living here.

### Guarding the President
Protecting the President came more slowly, and the law lagged behind the practice. The Secret Service had been founded in 1865 to chase counterfeiters. It began guarding presidents informally at Grover Cleveland's request in 1894, beyond what its appropriations allowed. Three agents were present in the Temple of Music, and they were reportedly not fully in charge of protecting McKinley. After the assassination, congressional leaders informally asked the Service to guard the President, and by 1902 a two-man White House detail was in place, still with no legal authority. Congress first paid for presidential protection in the sundry civil act of June 30, 1906, and first authorized it by statute on June 23, 1913. Permanent authorization waited until 1951.

### A form that outlived the moment
When Roosevelt himself died in January 1919, President Wilson, then in Paris, cabled home a proclamation announcing the death of the former President, and the State Department issued it the night of January 7. It was the narrower form used for ex-Presidents: flags at half staff on the White House and department buildings for thirty days, and military and naval honors on the day of the funeral. There was no day of mourning, no call to prayer, and no religious language at all; the wording was nearly identical to McKinley's own 1901 proclamation on the death of Benjamin Harrison. The *Statutes at Large* print it over the name of Secretary of State Robert Lansing. Washington's government offices did not close for the funeral. "DEPARTMENTS OPEN DURING T.R. FUNERAL," the *Washington Times* reported on January 8; the chief clerks said their offices would stay open without an executive order, and none came. The Interior Department shut for fifteen minutes. The fuller tribute came from Congress, which met in joint session on Sunday, February 9, 1919, in the House chamber, to hear Henry Cabot Lodge eulogize his friend. Wilson, still in Paris, was not there.

The McKinley proclamation remains the first entry in the record of executive actions of the Roosevelt presidency, a somber beginning to one of the most consequential presidencies in American history.

## At a glance
In January and February 1902, Roosevelt signed thirteen numbered executive orders. Most were routine civil-service adjustments, and one named the official welcoming party for Prince Henry of Prussia. But the order of January 31, soon called the "gag order," was something new. In a single sentence it forbade every federal employee in the executive departments to lobby Congress for a raise or for any other legislation in their own interest, except through their department heads, on pain of dismissal. It became a long-running grievance of federal workers, and in 1912 Congress wrote a law to undo it.

## Background
Most of these orders continue the housekeeping of [The First Numbered Executive Orders: Civil-Service Rules and Exceptions, Late 1901](story:77):

- **Exceptions from examination (155, 157, 165, 166):** Navy draftsmen; four Interior Department special inspectors; John P. Green, postage-stamp agent since 1897, to be treated as holding a competitive position without an exam; and certain Marine-Hospital Service posts.
- **Age limits, transfers and temporary jobs (156, 159, 161, 164):** age limits in the Coast and Geodetic Survey; transfers allowed only to jobs doing the same class of work; temporary appointments; and certain Treasury transfers.
- **Reporting and reinstatement (158, 160):** one order removed a provision allowing reinstatement after dismissal; another required lists of all unclassified positions to be sent to the Commission.
- **EO 154:** an exception for an assistant commissioner of immigration at New York. Its January 18 date rests on one transcription, and it is probably a misdated copy of a nearly identical order of June 2, 1902.
- **EO 162:** the order naming Assistant Secretary of State David J. Hill, Maj. Gen. Henry C. Corbin and Rear Adm. Robley D. Evans as the President's delegates to receive Prince Henry of Prussia, the German Kaiser's brother, on his widely covered visit in February and March 1902. It let them draw on the Secretary of State for expenses. The order carries no date of its own; it was printed in 1903 under February 5, 1902, and one modern transcription dates it January 24. The Associated Press reported the delegates' designation on February 7.

Then there is EO 163. The Civil Service Commission printed it under the heading "Attempts of Employees to Influence Legislation." Its whole text is one sentence:

> All officers and employees of the United States of every description, serving in or under any of the Executive Departments, and whether so serving in or out of Washington, are hereby forbidden, either directly or indirectly, individually or through associations, to solicit an increase of pay or to influence or attempt to influence in their own interest any other legislation whatever, either before Congress or its committees, or in any way save through the heads of the Departments in or under which they serve, on penalty of dismissal from the Government service.

The word "other" matters: it makes a pay raise itself one kind of legislation the order reaches. Some printings, including the text telegraphed to the press that day, leave it out, but the Commission's official text and the 1906 amendment both include it. The order gives no reasons.

## Historical context
Federal workers, postal clerks and letter carriers above all, had been pressing Congress directly for better pay and conditions, often through their associations. The live fight was the letter carriers' classification-and-salary bill, opposed by Representative Eugene F. Loud of California, chairman of the House Committee on the Post-Office and Post-Roads. On its face, the order sent all such pressure back up the executive chain of command.

The press offered explanations. The *San Francisco Call*'s Washington bureau reported that the President acted "after consultation with the Cabinet," that "the matter was first called to the attention of the President by officials of the Postoffice Department, and later other Cabinet members impressed on him the necessity of taking some action," and that Joseph G. Cannon, chairman of House Appropriations, called the order "a great necessity" (February 1, 1902). The Associated Press said "it is understood" the order grew out of complaints from members of Congress that postal employees were pushing for favorable legislation, "in some instances" with threats that unfriendly members "would not be re-elected." Those are press accounts, not Roosevelt's own words.

Narrower rules had come before. In 1895 Postmaster General William L. Wilson ordered that no postal employee "shall visit Washington, whether on leave with or without pay, for the purpose of influencing legislation before Congress." What set Roosevelt's order apart was its sweep: every executive department, in Washington and out.

It was applied within three weeks. On February 21, 1902, the Attorney General ruled that it barred the Navy-Yard and Arsenal Employees' Protective Association of Washington from lobbying Congress for a bill giving its members fifteen more days of annual leave.

## Political impact
The gag order was put to work at once. Joseph G. Cannon, chairman of House Appropriations, called it "a great necessity" the day it was issued. On February 21, 1902, the Attorney General ruled that it barred the Navy-Yard and Arsenal Employees' Protective Association of Washington from lobbying Congress for its leave bill. And the first employee reactions found were compliance, not protest: on March 9, 1902, the Chicago Post Office clerks' union addressed its appeal for an eight-hour day and a graded raise to Roosevelt and Postmaster General Henry C. Payne instead of Congress, "because the clerks desired to comply with the recent order." That picture comes from California papers; the letter carriers' and clerks' own journals for February 1902 have not been read.

The order soon pulled the Civil Service Commission into postal politics. In the fall 1902 elections Loud lost his San Francisco seat, a 6,000-vote majority in 1900 turned into a 6,000-vote defeat, and members of the National Association of Letter Carriers were accused of conspiring to beat him, an indirect violation of the order. The Commission's investigator, Fred Wannamaker, took three weeks of testimony in February 1903. He found nearly the whole San Francisco postal force had campaigned, carriers against Loud and clerks and officials for him, "each side battling for a selfish interest," and judged it "a labor-union affair" the carriers alone had not decided. The Commission recommended a warning rather than mass firings, and in July 1903 Payne had one posted in the San Francisco office.

Organized postal workers came to call it the "gag" order, and the name stuck to it and its successors. In 1905 the American Federation of Labor demanded that public employees "retain all the rights of citizenship," and Samuel Gompers's 1906 Bill of Grievances listed the order among labor's complaints. Rather than loosen it, Roosevelt widened it on January 25, 1906 (EO 402), adding "or independent Government establishments" to the departments it covered. On November 26, 1909, President Taft went further, forbidding bureau, office and division chiefs and their staffs, and Army, Navy and Marine officers stationed in Washington, to seek legislation or appropriations from Congress, or even to "respond to any request for information" from either House or any Member, "except through, or as authorized by, the head of his department." Through the Roosevelt and Taft years, the gag orders were a running grievance of the federal-employee movement.

Apart from Prince Henry's reception, the other orders in the group were routine. For that one, an Associated Press item of February 7, 1902, reported that Hill, Corbin and Evans "have been officially designated as the President's delegates" to receive Prince Henry.

## Legacy
Of the thirteen orders, only the gag order had a lasting afterlife. Taft restated the policy on April 8, 1912, expressly replacing the orders of 1902, 1906 and 1909 but still requiring that employees' petitions to Congress go through their department heads and strictly prohibiting any other attempt to secure legislation.

The federal unions took the fight to Congress, and four months later it produced the Lloyd–La Follette Act, section 6 of the Post Office appropriation act of August 24, 1912. It barred removal from the classified service except "for such cause as will promote the efficiency of said service," with written charges and a chance to answer. It protected postal employees' membership in organizations that did not bind them to strike; that protection was written for postal workers only. And for the whole civil service it declared that "the right of persons employed in the civil service of the United States, either individually or collectively, to petition Congress, or any Member thereof, or to furnish information to either House of Congress, or to any committee or Member thereof, shall not be denied or interfered with." In 1916 the Civil Service Commission noted that the law "had the effect of nullifying the Executive order of April 8, 1912."

Roosevelt's 1902 order, with Taft's successors, is remembered as the provocation the act was written to undo. It turns up today in histories of federal employees' speech rights and of whistleblower protection; the Knight First Amendment Institute calls the act's petition clause the first statutory whistleblower protection. The other twelve orders in the group carry no legacy of their own.

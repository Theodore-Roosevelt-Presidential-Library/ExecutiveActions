## At a glance
These four proclamations of 1906 have little in common except that each does one small, self-contained thing. One invited the states to send militia to the 1907 Jamestown celebration. One cleared land for three government towns on Idaho's Minidoka reclamation project. One asked Americans to help Chile after the Valparaíso earthquake, as the world had helped San Francisco that spring. And the Thanksgiving proclamation turned the yearly ritual into a short sermon, printed in Roosevelt's new simplified spelling.

## Background
**The Jamestown militia (Proclamation 626, May 28).** Congress had authorized "an international naval, marine, and military celebration" on Hampton Roads for 1907, and Roosevelt's proclamation of March 29, 1905 ([Celebration of the 300th Anniversary of the Founding of Jamestown, Virginia](story:58)) had declared it and invited the world's navies. The law also let the President invite the militia of the states, "but at their own expense." Proclamation 626 did that. It invited the Governors of the states and territories to send such militia organizations as "in their judgment, will afford proper military representation" at the celebration, set for May 13 to November 1, 1907, and announced that the Secretaries of War and the Navy would be directed to assemble regular forces as well. It closed with praise for the nation's "citizen-soldiery who have in the many ordeals of our Colonial and National life met their military obligations with superb self-sacrifice and devotion."

**The Hailey town sites (Proclamation 633, June 15).** In 1904 Roosevelt had reserved certain tracts in Idaho's Hailey Land District as town sites ([Reserving Town Sites in the Hailey Land District](story:53)), because they lay along a proposed railroad through the Minidoka reclamation lands. Then, on April 16, 1906, Congress passed a law aimed squarely at reclamation towns. It let the Secretary of the Interior withdraw up to 160 acres per town, survey it into lots with public grounds reserved, and sell the lots at auction for no less than their appraised value, with the money going into the reclamation fund. Proclamation 633 pulled 480 acres out of the 1904 reservation and held them closed for ten days so the Secretary could withdraw them under the new law before anyone else could file. The land fell in three 160-acre blocks. The proclamation names no towns, but on the map those blocks are the sites of today's **Acequia, Rupert and Heyburn**, all government towns of the Minidoka Project.

**The Valparaíso appeal (Proclamation 646, August 25).** An earthquake struck Valparaíso and central Chile on the evening of August 16, 1906. Roosevelt's proclamation asked Americans to help and pointed them to "The National Red Cross Association," which "has already taken measures to collect any subscriptions."

**Thanksgiving (Proclamation 665, October 22).** Roosevelt's yearly Thanksgiving proclamation set apart Thursday, November 29, 1906, the last Thursday of the month.

## Historical context
**A debt repaid.** The relief proclamation carried real weight. Barely four months earlier, on April 18, 1906, San Francisco had been leveled by earthquake and fire, and help had poured in from around the world. Roosevelt framed the appeal as repayment, asking that "our people out of their abundance now strive to do to another as others last spring did to us."

The diplomatic record shows how it came together. On August 20 Roosevelt cabled Chile's President Germán Riesco his "intense sympathy." The next day the American minister at Santiago, John Hicks, passed on the Chilean foreign minister's first estimate of the dead, 1,000 at Valparaíso and 400 elsewhere, and judged the loss "much worse than that of San Francisco." On August 23 he raised the estimate to 2,000 and suggested American subscriptions. Private giving was already moving: that same day Hicks delivered $10,000 from the people of San Francisco, the "first American subscription." The proclamation followed two days later. A wire story datelined Oyster Bay said it was issued "after consultation with Acting Secretary of State Bacon, at Sagamore Hill." Secretary of State Elihu Root was in South America on a goodwill tour and was expected at Valparaíso about September 2.

**Building toward Jamestown.** The militia invitation was one step in the long run-up to the 1907 tercentennial, which its federal charter cast as "an international naval, marine, and military celebration."

**Government towns in the sagebrush.** The Hailey order belongs to the Reclamation Service's remaking of the Snake River country around the Minidoka Project, where the government planned to lay out and sell its own towns.

**A sermon, in simplified spelling.** Roosevelt used the Thanksgiving proclamation to warn against complacency. "Material well-being, indispensable tho it is, can never be anything but the foundation of true national greatness and happiness," he wrote in the proclamation of October 22, 1906. "If we build nothing upon this foundation, then our national life will be as meaningless and empty as a house where only the foundation has been laid." Look closely at the spelling. "Tho," "past" (for "passed") and "affixt" were all on the Simplified Spelling Board's list of 300 words. On August 27, 1906, Roosevelt had ordered the Public Printer to use that list in executive-department publications, a public controversy that autumn, and this proclamation was one of the more visible documents to carry the new spellings.

## Political impact
**Valparaíso.** The appeal went out as a wire story. The *Sunday Oregonian* printed it on August 26 beside the relief news from Chile, and the same page carried a Paris dispatch on French reaction to Roosevelt's spelling order. The money it raised through the Red Cross was modest. By December 31, 1906, Hicks reported, he had received $8,943.38 from the Red Cross societies through the State Department, with a further $3,410 authorized: about $12,350 in all, against $15,000 from the city of San Francisco and $5,000 from Los Angeles, which he had handed straight to the Chilean government. He also reported that "There are no Red Cross societies in Chile." So instead of distributing the money through a Chilean Red Cross as Washington had instructed, he paid it out himself in small sums with a Santiago charity leader, Señora de Jordan, "giving preference always to Americans or descendants of Americans." The Chilean government's thanks for American sympathy are in the same record.

**Jamestown.** The militia invitation ran as a short news item. The *Los Angeles Herald* of May 30, 1906 headed it "VIRGINIA INVITES SOLDIERS."

**Thanksgiving.** The proclamation was printed everywhere: fifteen items in fourteen California papers between October 23 and November 30 alone, among them the *Fresno Evening Democrat*, the *Sacramento Daily Union* and the *Salinas Index Journal*. But most readers never saw the simplified spellings. The Associated Press text, as printed in Fresno, reads "has passed" and "indispensable though it is," and it strays from the signed original in other places too. No California paper that fall remarked on the spellings in this proclamation. The spelling experiment survived in the official printing and the signed original, not in what most Americans read.

**Hailey.** No newspaper notice of Proclamation 633 itself has turned up, but the Salt Lake papers followed the town-lot sales it made possible. In July 1906 the *Deseret Evening News* reported the General Land Office's announcement of a public sale of lots in Heyburn and Rupert, and the *Salt Lake Tribune*'s Rupert correspondent gave the dates: August 20 at Heyburn and August 27 at Rupert. The Heyburn auction went ahead on August 20 and flopped. The business lots, appraised at "$400 and $500, each," drew no bids, since "no bid under the appraised price could be considered." The next day the business men bought residence lots appraised "at only $75" and planned to build there instead, so that "the town of Heyburn will be slightly out of its official position on the map." The *Deseret Evening News* called it "HEYBURN TOWN LOT SALE WAS A FIZZLE."

## Legacy
The Jamestown Exposition opened April 26, 1907, and despite heavy financial losses it left a lasting mark. On December 16, 1907, the battleships of the "Great White Fleet" sailed from Hampton Roads on their voyage around the world. After the United States entered the First World War in 1917, Congress voted to buy 474 acres of the exposition grounds at Sewell's Point, which became Naval Station Norfolk.

Rupert, Heyburn and Acequia grew up as government towns of the Minidoka Project, their lot sales paying into the reclamation fund.

The Valparaíso appeal is a modest early chapter in American international disaster relief through the Red Cross, and the record shows how improvised that chapter still was: a few thousand dollars, handed out by a diplomat because no Chilean Red Cross existed to receive them.

The Thanksgiving proclamation is one link in the unbroken chain of yearly presidential Thanksgiving proclamations going back to Lincoln's of October 3, 1863 (see [Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20) and [Thanksgiving Day, 1902](story:41)). The date rested on custom until Franklin Roosevelt moved the holiday a week earlier in 1939–1941, and a joint resolution of December 26, 1941 fixed it on the fourth Thursday. Under that rule the 1906 holiday would have fallen on November 22. The proclamation is also a lasting specimen of the simplified-spelling experiment. After the House voted against the new spellings in December 1906, Roosevelt withdrew his order, and the spellings preserved in the *Statutes at Large* outlived it.

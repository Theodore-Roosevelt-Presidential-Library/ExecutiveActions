## At a glance
The executive orders of spring 1907 carry more weight than any run before them. Executive Order 589 of March 14 barred Japanese and Korean laborers from reaching the mainland by way of Hawaii, Mexico or Canada, putting the American half of the Gentlemen's Agreement with Japan into force. In the same weeks the Panama Canal passed from John F. Stevens to the Army engineer George W. Goethals, the Canal Zone got a new government, and Roosevelt called the election for the first Philippine Assembly.

## Background
**Closing the back door.** The immigration act of February 20, 1907, gave the President power to turn away aliens who used passports issued for some other country to reach the American mainland "to the detriment of labor conditions." Japanese laborers had been arriving on the West Coast that way, by way of Hawaii, Mexico and Canada, on passports issued for those places. Executive Order 589 shut that route. Its ordering clause reads:

> I hereby order that such citizens of Japan or Korea, to-wit: Japanese or Korean laborers, skilled and unskilled, who have received passports to go to Mexico, Canada or Hawaii, and come therefrom, be refused permission to enter the continental territory of the United States.

The Secretary of Commerce and Labor was told to enforce it "thru the Bureau of Immigration and Naturalization." That spelling is genuine, a leftover of Roosevelt's simplified-spelling campaign. The order was the enforcement half of the **Gentlemen's Agreement**. Under it, Japan agreed to stop issuing passports to laborers bound for the mainland, and the United States agreed not to pass an openly discriminatory exclusion law like the one already applied to the Chinese. Japanese already lawfully in the country could still send for their parents, wives and children.

**A new command at Panama.** Stevens had written a letter on January 30, 1907. It was never styled as a resignation, but it was read as one, and Roosevelt accepted it on February 14. The change was announced on February 26. Even so, Executive Order 577½ (March 4) made Stevens chairman of the Isthmian Canal Commission, "vice Theodore P. Shonts, resigned, to take effect this day," and named Lt. Col. George W. Goethals to the seat Stevens left, at $15,000 a year. The *New-York Tribune* of March 5 called Stevens's appointment "a temporary one until Lieutenant Colonel George W. Goethals … takes charge on the isthmus." Goethals reached the Isthmus on March 12, and the reorganized Commission took up its duties on April 1. Several orders followed in quick succession:
- **EO 593 (March 16)** appointed Sibert, Gaillard and Rousseau in place of Harrod, Hains and Endicott, and fixed their pay.
- **EO 595 (March 18)** gave Goethals a furnished house on the Isthmus and travel expenses.
- **EO 606 (April 1)** made Goethals chairman, "vice John F. Stevens, resigned." Stevens had held the chair less than four weeks.
- **EO 607 (April 1)** put former Kentucky senator Joseph C. S. Blackburn on the Commission as its political counterweight.

**A new government for the Zone.** Executive Order 586 (March 13) divided the Canal Zone into four administrative districts: Ancon, Emperador, Gorgona and Cristobal. It abolished every existing municipal council and office and took effect April 15, 1907. Companion orders amended the Zone's penal code and criminal procedure (EO 587) and let ministers and judges perform marriages there (EO 588, replaced by Executive Order 647 on May 31).

**Other notable orders:**
- **The Philippine Assembly (EO 602, March 28).** The Philippine Commission was directed "to call a general election for the choice of delegates to a popular assembly … which shall be known as the Philippine Assembly." This was the elected lower house promised in the Organic Act of 1902, and a real step toward Filipino self-government. The Organic Act itself limited the vote to provinces not inhabited by Moros or other non-Christian peoples.
- **Expatriation and passports (EO 610, April 6).** The order amended diplomatic and consular rules on expatriation, citizenship, naturalization and passports, putting the Expatriation Act of March 2, 1907, into practice. Among other things, that act declared that an American woman who married a foreigner took her husband's nationality.
- **Justice Department opinions (EO 579, March 5).** No subordinate officer of the Department of Justice except the acting Attorney-General could publish an opinion or statement of law until the Attorney-General approved it.
- **Two ceremonies.** Executive Order 613 excused Spanish War veterans working in the executive departments and independent establishments in the District of Columbia from duty on April 12, so they could attend the dedication of the Rough Riders Memorial at Arlington. The date of the order is not certain. The *Evening Star* reported that afternoon that those "in government employ will be excused at 3 o'clock." Thirteen days later, Executive Order 619 (April 25) did the same for the Civil War societies at the unveiling of the George B. McClellan statue. Those were the Armies of the Potomac, Tennessee, Cumberland and Ohio, the Grand Army of the Republic, the Sons of Veterans and the Loyal Legion.
- **Summer Saturdays (EO 609, April 4).** In July, August and September, a Saturday's work became four hours, not counting lunch, for mechanics, laborers and other civil-service employees at the War Department's manufacturing and supply arsenals and depots, at Army headquarters offices and in the Engineer Department at Large.
- **The ledger.** The rest is the usual work: appointments and reinstatements outside the competitive rules, consular-regulation changes (EO 611 on registering Americans abroad, EO 617 on appointment and promotion in the Consular Service), civil-service rule changes (EO 585, EO 615), and land-district mergers (Gunnison, Colorado, into Montrose, EO 616; Great Falls into Glasgow, Montana, EO 592).

## Historical context
Executive Order 589 sat at the center of one of the hardest problems of Roosevelt's second term. Japan was rising, freshly victorious over Russia and proud of it, while anti-Japanese feeling was boiling over on the Pacific Coast. On October 11, 1906, the San Francisco school board ordered Japanese, Chinese and Korean children into a segregated "Oriental" school. Tokyo took the insult seriously. In his Sixth Annual Message of December 3, 1906, Roosevelt called the school order what he thought it was: "To shut them out from the public schools is a wicked absurdity."

He also accepted that the Coast would not tolerate large-scale Japanese labor immigration. So he made a trade. He brought the school board's delegation to the White House in February 1907 and struck the bargain on February 15. In return for the face-saving Gentlemen's Agreement, rather than an exclusion law that would have humiliated Japan, San Francisco gave way, but only partly. The resolution it adopted on March 13, 1907, the day before Executive Order 589, repealed the October order "excepting in so far as it applies to Chinese and Korean children." Chinese and Korean pupils stayed segregated, and new age and English-proficiency rules kept most older Japanese students out of neighborhood schools anyway. The relief went to Japanese children because Japan was the power whose feelings had to be managed. The executive order made the American side of the deal enforceable without ever naming Japan in an insulting law.

The Panama change was a different kind of problem: how to push the biggest engineering job the country had ever tried through to the end. Shonts had left three weeks before Stevens. Roosevelt decided to put the canal under Army engineers, who could not resign. Soldiers under orders would finish what civilians kept walking away from. The Canal Zone reorganization and the Philippine election order both belonged to the machinery of a young overseas empire: running the Isthmus as a going concern, and beginning, carefully, to share power in the Philippines. The expatriation order reflected the citizenship law of the day, under which marriage could strip a woman of her nationality.

## Political impact
This time the reaction was real and national.

**Japan and the Coast.** The Gentlemen's Agreement and the order enforcing it were watched closely on both sides of the Pacific. Californians and organized labor on the Coast wanted the Japanese barred outright and thought the deal too soft. Japan resented any restriction at all. The whole affair fed the periodic "war scare" talk about the two navies. Roosevelt defended the middle course as the only one that protected the Coast's labor market without needlessly insulting a proud and capable rival, the same position between two flanks that he liked to hold.

**Panama.** The change of command was reported as a major administrative shakeup and a gamble. Critics wondered whether the Army could finish what the celebrated Stevens had not stayed to complete. The answer would take years to arrive. Meanwhile the reorganized Commission took up its duties on April 1, 1907, with Goethals in the chair and Blackburn added as its political counterweight.

**The Philippines.** The election order was noticed as a real, if limited, extension of self-government. The election it called was held on July 30, 1907, and the first Philippine Assembly, eighty delegates, opened at the Grand Opera House in Manila on October 16, 1907. Secretary of War William Howard Taft called it to order, Sergio Osmeña was elected Speaker that afternoon, and Manuel Quezon became majority floor leader.

**Quieter orders.** The Canal Zone code, expatriation and Justice Department orders drew little attention of their own.

**Arlington.** The Rough Riders dedication of April 12 got full coverage in the Washington papers. Roosevelt, "sometime colonel of the 1st United States Volunteer Cavalry," was the central figure and gave the address. Mrs. Allyn K. Capron, who designed the monument, pulled the halliards, and a twenty-one-gun salute closed the afternoon. The ceremony had one sour note: the Spanish War veterans the order had excused were marched off the field partway through by their own department commander, over a slight in protocol. The order that emptied the government offices for the event went unremarked, as such orders usually did.

## Legacy
### The end of Executive Order 589
Executive Order 589 stayed in force for decades, and the way it finally came apart is easy to get wrong. The law behind it, the Act of February 20, 1907, was repealed by section 38 of the Immigration Act of February 5, 1917, effective May 1, 1917. Section 3 of that act re-enacted the same passport power, so the order stood on new footing without a break. The 1917 act was repealed in turn by the Immigration and Nationality Act of June 27, 1952. Along the way, President Truman's Executive Order 10009 of October 18, 1948, revoked Roosevelt's order only in part. It lifted the bar for "Japanese or Korean citizens or subjects who entered the Territory of Hawaii under the aforesaid passports," reasoning that the group was "so small, and the age of such persons … so advanced," that admitting them "would no longer be a detriment to labor conditions." The Mexico and Canada parts were left standing.

The Gentlemen's Agreement governed Japanese immigration until the Immigration Act of 1924 replaced it with outright exclusion, a far harsher break that soured U.S.–Japan relations for a generation. The order and the whole episode are now read as a case study in how a president handled racial politics and great-power diplomacy at once, and in the human cost paid by the immigrants caught in between.

### Panama
Goethals finished the Panama Canal in 1914, and the Army-engineer command that Executive Order 606 put in place is remembered as the decision that got it built. The Canal Zone government set up in these weeks lasted, in changing form, until the Torrijos–Carter treaties of 1977 began handing the Zone to Panama.

### The Philippine Assembly
The Assembly that Executive Order 602 called into being was a milestone on the long road to Philippine independence, granted in 1946. It was also the launching point for Osmeña and Quezon, two careers that would run the rest of the way.

### Citizenship
The Expatriation Act's rule that a woman who married a foreigner took his nationality, which Executive Order 610 carried into consular practice, was undone in stages, beginning with the Cable Act of 1922.

### A note on the numbers
Executive orders of this era were not printed in the *Statutes at Large*, which carried only proclamations; systematic publication began with the Federal Register Act of 1935. The numbers used here were assigned starting in 1907 by the Department of State, which numbered all the orders in its files back to 1862. They were in official use well before the 1944 published list: a 1914 government compilation, *Laws Relating to Insular and Military Affairs*, prints this order as number 589 and the Philippine election order as 602. They are filing numbers, though, not part of the signed text. The 1911 government printing of the Canal orders gives Executive Order 589 with no number and no title.

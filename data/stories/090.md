## At a glance
Roosevelt's last forty-three executive orders of 1906, signed from August 29 to December 31, are mostly routine. One is not. On October 23, with Cuba's government collapsed and an American provisional governor in Havana, Executive Order 518 set out who would answer to whom. Others enforced the eight-hour law on federal contractors, set rules for presenting the Navy's Medal of Honor, and reserved land in New Mexico for the people of Jemez Pueblo.

## Background
In the summer of 1906 a disputed election and an armed revolt threw the young Republic of Cuba into crisis. President Tomás Estrada Palma, working through Consul General Frank Steinhart, cabled Washington in early September asking confidentially for ships and troops. Roosevelt sent Secretary of War William Howard Taft and Assistant Secretary of State Robert Bacon to Havana; they arrived September 19. On September 28, Estrada Palma and his cabinet resigned. The Cuban Congress could not muster a quorum to accept the resignations or choose a successor, and Cuba was left with no government at all.

Article III of the treaty with Cuba of May 22, 1903, the treaty that embodied the Platt Amendment, gave the United States a right to intervene to preserve Cuban independence and order. On September 29, Taft proclaimed a Provisional Government of Cuba and served as Provisional Governor himself until October 13, when the lawyer and colonial administrator Charles E. Magoon took over.

Magoon had been governing for ten days when Roosevelt signed Executive Order 518. The order did not create the provisional government or install its governor. It set the machinery behind them:

> It is hereby ordered that the temporary administration of the government of the Republic of Cuba in virtue of the requirements of Article three of the Treaty of May twenty-second, one thousand nine hundred and three, shall be conducted in Havana by the Provisional Governor subject to the supervision of the Secretary of War; and all business in relation thereto in this country will be transacted in the Bureau of Insular Affairs of the War Department, where it will be made a matter of official record.

The Bureau of Insular Affairs was the same War Department office that ran Puerto Rico and the Philippines.

**Other notable orders in the run:**

- **Eight-hour law (EO 504, September 19)** required departments to report cases of supervised contractors working men more than eight hours a day.
- **Medal of Honor (EO 517, October 19)** set the rules for presenting the medal to Navy enlisted men and officers.
- **Panama (EO 527, November 17)** amended the organization and duties of the Isthmian Canal Commission, modifying an order of April 1, 1905.
- **Jemez Pueblo (EO 537, December 19)** reserved described lands in New Mexico for the use and benefit of the Indians of the Jemez Pueblo.
- **Lighthouses.** Small parcels were reserved at Cape Hinchinbrook, Alaska (EO 525), at the rock known as "Rock of Ages" in Lake Superior (EO 529), and from the Fort San Pedro military reservation at Iloilo in the Philippines (EO 512).
- **Military reservations.** Other orders took land from the Fort Robinson (Nebraska), New Fort Lyon (Colorado), Fort Liscum target-range (Alaska) and Fort Davis (Texas) reservations.
- **Navy personnel.** EO 533 raised pay for men serving past their first enlistment, and EO 534 set rules for buying a discharge in peacetime.
- **Land offices.** EO 522 abolished Minnesota's Marshall land district, whose office by then sat at St. Cloud, and moved its business to Duluth; EO 510 moved Alaska's surveyor-general from Sitka to Juneau.

The rest are consular-fee changes and the usual appointments outside the competitive rules. One note for anyone reading the run as a timeline: the numbers were assigned later and are not in date order. EO 527 is dated November 17, for example, before EO 526 of December 6.

## Historical context
Executive Order 518 is the paper hinge of what became the Second Occupation of Cuba, 1906 to 1909, the clearest use of the Platt Amendment's intervention clause during Roosevelt's presidency. Roosevelt went in reluctantly. He had no wish to annex Cuba and worried that a heavy hand would confirm Latin American suspicions of the "Colossus of the North." But he judged that the treaty and the collapse of Cuban self-government left him no choice but to restore order and then withdraw. Routing the work through the Bureau of Insular Affairs fit the settled machinery of an overseas empire that had grown out of the War of 1898.

The eight-hour order belongs to the labor politics of the Square Deal: an administration keeping faith with organized labor by watching the hours federal contractors worked their men. The Jemez order reflects the distinct legal standing of the New Mexico Pueblos, long-settled farming communities whose lands the federal government reserved and protected. The lighthouse and military orders were the ordinary business of a government with a growing coastline and an empire abroad.

## Political impact
The intervention in Cuba was the autumn's real news, argued over in the national press and watched closely abroad. Critics at home and in Latin America saw fresh proof of American overreach under the Platt Amendment. Defenders pointed to Estrada Palma's own request and to Roosevelt's stated plan to restore order and leave.

The order itself drew little separate attention, but it was printed. The Washington *Evening Star* of October 29, 1906, ran it on page 1 under the head "RELATIONS WITH CUBA / Executive Order Regarding the Administration of Affairs," with the operative text in full. That six-day gap says something about how obscure such orders could be: nothing yet required executive orders to be published at all.

The eight-hour order was welcomed by labor and unremarked elsewhere. The Medal of Honor, canal, Pueblo, lighthouse, military and personnel orders drew no recorded press notice.

## Legacy
The provisional government governed Cuba until January 28, 1909, when it was abolished on the installation of the elected president, José Miguel Gómez. The Army of Cuban Pacification completed its withdrawal on April 1 that year. It was one of several U.S. interventions the Platt Amendment allowed before the amendment was abrogated in 1934. Magoon's administration remains contested in Cuban memory, remembered by many for patronage and graft even as it built roads and reorganized the government.

The Bureau of Insular Affairs, created in 1902, kept administering America's overseas territories until Reorganization Plan No. II of 1939 abolished it and moved its work to the Interior Department.

No order revoking the Jemez reservation is known. Executive Order 1406 of 1911 corrected its survey reference, and Executive Order 2250 of 1915 reserved more land for the same pueblo. The Pueblo of Jemez today describes its holdings as more than 89,000 acres: the Spanish grant, the executive-order additions and later purchases.

The two American lighthouse sites became working lights. Rock of Ages began operating in 1908, about two years after the reservation, received its Fresnel lens in 1910, and was automated in 1978. It remains an active aid to navigation, inside Isle Royale National Park and closed to the public. Cape Hinchinbrook's first light shone on November 15, 1910; a new tower followed in 1934, the station was automated in 1974, and the Coast Guard owns it today.

## At a glance
In 1905 Roosevelt signed two reciprocity proclamations, one for shipping and one for authors. In May he lifted the extra duties the United States charged foreign ships, so far as vessels of the young Republic of Panama were concerned, because Panama charged no such duties on American ships. On July 1 he declared that Norway gave Americans copyright on the same terms as its own citizens, opening American copyright to Norwegians. Norway had issued a matching decree timed to take effect that very day.

## Background
**Panama (Proclamation 556, May 15).** Section 4228 of the Revised Statutes let the President drop the "discriminating" tonnage and import duties the United States levied on foreign vessels, one country at a time, once that country proved it treated American ships the same way. Panama's government gave that proof, and Roosevelt suspended the duties for Panamanian vessels and their cargoes from the day he signed, to last "so long as the reciprocal exemption of vessels belonging to citizens of the United States, and their cargoes, shall be continued, and no longer." It was the same law used for Cuban vessels in 1902 ([Suspending Duties on Cuban Vessels](story:38)), though the two proclamations word their key clauses differently. Francis B. Loomis signed as Acting Secretary of State.

**Norway (Proclamation 577, July 1).** The International Copyright Act of March 3, 1891, often called the Chace Act, opened American copyright to foreigners only when their country met one of two conditions, and section 13 left it to the President to say so by proclamation. Roosevelt declared that "the first of the conditions specified in section 13 of the act of March 3, 1891, is now fulfilled in respect to the subjects of Norway." The first condition was that the foreign nation give American citizens copyright "on substantially the same basis as its own citizens." Norwegian authors and artists could now secure American copyright on the same footing as other qualifying foreigners. The proclamation names no particular kinds of works or creators. It used the same route that had opened American copyright to Cubans in 1903 ([Extending Copyright Protection to Citizens of Cuba](story:43)). Herbert H. D. Peirce, Third Assistant Secretary of State, signed as Acting Secretary of State.

## Historical context
Panama had become independent in November 1903, and the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty of that month gave the United States the use, occupation and control of the Canal Zone. By May 1905 American work on the Isthmus was a year old. Settling ordinary shipping duties was one small part of tying the new republic into American commerce. By the proclamation's own account, the proof came from "the Government of the Republic of Panama"; when and how it was offered is not known.

The copyright law worked one country at a time too, though not always singly. The very first section-13 proclamation, on July 1, 1891, took in Belgium, France, Great Britain and the British possessions, and Switzerland together. Germany and Italy followed in 1892, Denmark and Portugal in 1893, Spain in 1895, Mexico and Chile in 1896, Costa Rica and the Netherlands in 1899, and Cuba in 1903. Norway made fifteen.

The Norway arrangement was settled while Norway and Sweden still shared a king. On May 25, 1905, a Norwegian royal decree, issued in the name of King Oscar and signed at Stockholm by Crown Prince Gustaf as regent during the King's illness, provided that Norway's copyright law of July 4, 1893, would apply to works American citizens published from July 1, 1905, on the assumption that the American President would issue a proclamation. Roosevelt's proclamation is dated that same July 1. The two documents were a matched pair, timed to take effect together. Norway's parliament, the Storting, broke with Sweden on June 7, between the decree and the proclamation, but by then the exchange was already arranged. Norway belonged to the Berne copyright union and the United States did not, which is why the national-treatment route, the "first" condition, was the one used.

Two details of the moment stand out. The proclamation speaks of "the subjects of Norway" at a time when the United States had not yet recognized Norway's separation; Secretary of State Elihu Root did so on October 30, 1905. And it was countersigned by an acting secretary on the day the Secretary of State died: John Hay "died on July 1st," as Roosevelt's proclamation of mourning announced two days later. Peirce, who signed for the department that day, would be named American minister to Norway on June 22, 1906, a coincidence of personnel rather than a sign the proclamation was meant as a gesture.

## Political impact
Both were technical instruments, and neither has been found in the newspapers of the day. A search of California newspapers for "Panama" with "tonnage" from mid-May to mid-June 1905, and for "copyright" with "Norway" from late June through August, turned up shipping news, magazine notes and serial fiction, but no notice of either proclamation. The places a notice of the copyright proclamation would most likely appear, the Norwegian-language American press (such as *Decorah-Posten*) and the book-trade press (such as *Publishers' Weekly*), have not been searched.

The one period reprint found is in the Bern copyright bureau's journal, *Le Droit d'Auteur* (September 15, 1905). It reprinted both Norway's decree and Roosevelt's proclamation, filed the exchange as an arrangement involving a member of the Berne copyright union, and noted that the American proclamation was identical in text to those issued for other countries. It treated the matter as a routine bilateral arrangement and reported no campaign by Norwegian authors behind it.

## Legacy
Both proclamations were routine pieces of the period's widening web of trade and copyright reciprocity, and neither has a legacy beyond the bilateral relationship it regularized. Norway was later folded into collective copyright proclamations, including the multi-country proclamation of April 9, 1910 (36 Stat. 2685).

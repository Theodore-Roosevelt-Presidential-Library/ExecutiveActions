## At a glance
On November 2, 1901, Theodore Roosevelt signed his first Thanksgiving proclamation, setting aside Thursday, November 28. It came just forty-nine days after William McKinley's death made him president. In a few short paragraphs he mourned McKinley, steadied a shaken country, and asked Americans to prove their gratitude "in deeds."

## Background
A Thanksgiving proclamation has no legal force. It commands no agency, withdraws no land, changes no rule. The president simply recommends that the country set aside a Thursday for gratitude, and its power is moral and customary.

What makes this one worth a closer look is its timing. It was the second proclamation Roosevelt signed as president. The first, Proclamation 465, came on September 14, 1901, the day McKinley died and Roosevelt took the oath. It appointed September 19, the day set for McKinley's burial, "as a day of mourning and prayer throughout the United States" ([Announcing the Death of William McKinley](story:32)). The two print one after the other in the *Statutes at Large*, with nothing between them. Roosevelt's first act in the proclamation form was to mourn a murdered president. His second was to ask the country to give thanks anyway.

He used the ritual's short frame to do delicate public work: name the nation's grief, honor the dead president, and reassure people that the constitutional order had held. "This Thanksgiving finds the people still bowed with sorrow for the death of a great and good President," he wrote. The manner of McKinley's death, he said, "should awaken in the breasts of our people a keen anxiety for the country, and at the same time a resolute purpose not to be driven by any calamity from the path of strong, orderly, popular liberty which as a nation we have thus far safely trod."

The rest follows the usual form: a year "of peace and plenty," a reminder that "as much as has been given us, much will be expected from us," and a call to show thankfulness by the way "each of us does his duty to his fellow men." The proclamation's opening line uses the word "especial," which looks like a typo but is the word as issued.

## Historical context
Roosevelt called the custom "time-hallowed," but as an unbroken yearly habit it was fairly young. George Washington proclaimed a national thanksgiving in 1789, but for decades the day came and went at the whim of presidents and governors. On October 3, 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national Thanksgiving for "the last Thursday of November next." Every president after him issued one every year, without a gap.

The date was looser than the habit. Andrew Johnson set the 1865 Thanksgiving for the first Thursday of December, and Ulysses S. Grant named November 18 in 1869, a week before the last Thursday. Until 1941, nothing but custom fixed the day.

What set 1901 apart was the shadow over it. McKinley had been shot at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo on September 6 and died on September 14. Roosevelt, at forty-two, became the youngest man ever to hold the office, at a moment of public fear about anarchism and the stability of the government. McKinley was the third president killed in thirty-six years, after Lincoln and Garfield. A Thanksgiving proclamation that ignored this would have rung false; one that dwelt only on the loss would have deepened the alarm. Roosevelt threaded the needle.

## Political impact
The proclamation was a wire story the day it was signed. The Associated Press moved it on Saturday, November 2, 1901, and over the next three days it ran across the country, often on page one and often in full. The *Evening Times-Republican* of Marshalltown, Iowa printed the complete text on its front page that same day, under "THANKSGIVING PROCLAMATION. President Selects November 28." The *San Jose Mercury-News* had it on page one November 3, and the *San Francisco Call* and Santa Barbara's *Morning Press* ran it the same morning. The *Madison Daily Leader* in South Dakota followed on the 4th; Ohio's *Stark County Democrat* and Indiana's *Berne Witness* on the 5th; the *Santa Fe New Mexican* and Iowa's *Decorah Public Opinion* on the 6th. Its closing line, "cease from their wonted occupations," turns up on 106 digitized pages in Chronicling America alone, and the McKinley phrase "keen anxiety for the country" on 90, in a collection that holds only a fraction of the roughly 2,200 daily papers then publishing. The text was also cabled to Manila, where it set off a second round of coverage in mid-November.

Headline writers went straight for the passage on McKinley: "Speaks in Touching Terms of the Memory of the Late President" (*Morning Press*, November 3); "Refers to Nation's Sad Loss and the Keen Anxiety of the People" (*San Francisco Call*, the same day); "President Roosevelt Mentions the Late William McKinley" (San Luis Obispo's *Morning Tribune*, November 6).

The comment did not stay in the pulpits. Secular editorial pages took it up, and approved. "President Roosevelt's Thanksgiving proclamation is characteristic of the man," the *San Jose Mercury-News* wrote on November 3. "He says something and does not merely string together a lot of platitudes and conventionalities." The *Call*'s editorial page came back to the "duty to his fellow men" line on November 13, and on the 28th the *Healdsburg Tribune* built a whole essay on the history of Thanksgiving proclamations around Roosevelt's. The *Mercury-News* even printed a correction on November 11 after botching the closing paragraph, a sign people were reading closely. The *Call* did remark on November 13 that the proclamation landed in election week, when Americans "were so busy with politics that they had little attention to give to anything else."

The only jokes aimed elsewhere. "There is something to be thankful for beyond the scope of the president's Thanksgiving proclamation," the *Los Angeles Herald* quipped on November 6. "It will be a full year before election time comes again." The *Marysville Daily Appeal* offered on November 17: "It is possible that Aguinaldo regards the cabling of the President's Thanksgiving proclamation to the Philippines as an unnecessary expense." No contemporary objection to the proclamation itself has been found in Chronicling America or the California Digital Newspaper Collection, though the big Eastern dailies are thinly represented in both.

Roosevelt would sign one every autumn of his presidency, eight in all: [Thanksgiving Day, 1902](story:41), [Thanksgiving Day, 1903](story:47), [Thanksgiving Day, 1904](story:54), [The Senate Call and Thanksgiving Proclamation of 1905](story:62), and on through 1908.

## Legacy
The yearly presidential Thanksgiving proclamation has continued without a break from Lincoln's in 1863 to the present, through every change of president. In 1963, John F. Kennedy signed the year's proclamation on November 4, eighteen days before his assassination, and Lyndon Johnson issued no replacement. Two cautions keep that claim honest. The unbroken chain starts with Lincoln, not Washington: from 1815 to 1863, no president issued a Thanksgiving proclamation at all. And "every year" is not "every November," since Andrew Johnson's 1865 Thanksgiving fell in December. From 1901 the date rested on custom alone for another thirty-eight years, until another Roosevelt moved it and Congress settled the question by law.

In 1939 November had five Thursdays, so Lincoln's "last Thursday" pointed to the 30th. Franklin Roosevelt's Proclamation 2373 of October 31, 1939 set Thanksgiving a week early, on Thursday, November 23. He did the same for three years running: Proclamation 2441 moved 1940 to November 21, and Proclamation 2522 moved 1941 to November 20. The shopping reason came from a press conference, not a proclamation. On August 14, 1939 he told reporters he had heard "complaints that Thanksgiving Day came too close to Christmas," that "stores and people who work, retail people, etc., are very anxious to have it set forward," and "I am going to step it up one week."

The country split. In 1939 twenty-five states kept the president's date, twenty-six kept Lincoln's, and Colorado, Mississippi and Texas observed both. Early polling ran about six to four against the change. At a press conference on May 20, 1941, Roosevelt gave it up: the experiment "did not work," and "next year it will return to the last Thursday of November, as heretofore."

Congress then fixed the day. Representative Earl Michener of Michigan introduced House Joint Resolution 41 on January 3, 1941, and the House passed it on October 6 naming the last Thursday of November. The Senate Judiciary Committee struck "last" and put in "fourth." Senator John Danaher explained on the floor that the fourth Thursday "is the historical date," is also the last Thursday five years in seven, and "we make it the fourth rather than the last Thursday simply to have a fixed date so that everyone will know from now on what day is to be Thanksgiving Day." The Senate agreed on December 9, two days after Pearl Harbor, and the House accepted the change on December 19. Roosevelt signed it on December 26, 1941 (ch. 631, 55 Stat. 862). By its own terms it applied "after the year 1941," so Thanksgiving 1942 was the first held under law.

Theodore Roosevelt's eight Thanksgiving proclamations sit inside that long chain. The first of them remains a small, well-made example of how a president can use a purely ceremonial document to steady a country: grief acknowledged, continuity affirmed, and nothing asked of anyone but a day's reflection.

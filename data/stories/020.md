## At a glance
On November 2, 1901, Theodore Roosevelt signed his first Thanksgiving proclamation, setting aside Thursday, November 28. It came just forty-nine days after William McKinley's death made him president. In a few short paragraphs he mourned McKinley, steadied a shaken country, and asked Americans to prove their gratitude "in deeds."

## Background
A Thanksgiving proclamation has no legal force. It commands no agency, withdraws no land, changes no rule. The president simply recommends that the country set aside a Thursday for gratitude, and its power is moral and customary.

What makes this one worth a closer look is its timing. It was the second proclamation Roosevelt signed as president. The first, Proclamation 465, came on September 14, 1901, the day McKinley died and Roosevelt took the oath. It appointed September 19, the day set for McKinley's burial, "as a day of mourning and prayer throughout the United States" ([Announcing the Death of William McKinley](story:32)). The two print one after the other in the *Statutes at Large*, with nothing between them. Roosevelt's first act in the proclamation form was to mourn a murdered president. His second was to ask the country to give thanks anyway.

He used the ritual's short frame to do delicate public work: name the nation's grief, honor the dead president, and reassure people that the constitutional order had held. "This Thanksgiving finds the people still bowed with sorrow for the death of a great and good President," he wrote. The manner of McKinley's death, he said, "should awaken in the breasts of our people a keen anxiety for the country, and at the same time a resolute purpose not to be driven by any calamity from the path of strong, orderly, popular liberty which as a nation we have thus far safely trod."

The rest follows the usual form: a year "of peace and plenty," a reminder that "as much as has been given us, much will be expected from us," and a call to show thankfulness by the way "each of us does his duty to his fellow men." The proclamation's opening line uses the word "especial," which looks like a typo but is the word as issued.

## Historical context
Roosevelt called the custom "time-hallowed," but as an unbroken yearly habit it was fairly young. George Washington proclaimed a national thanksgiving in 1789, but for decades the day came and went at the whim of presidents and governors. On October 3, 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national Thanksgiving for "the last Thursday of November next." Every president after him issued one every year, without a gap.

The date was looser than the habit. Andrew Johnson set the 1865 Thanksgiving for the first Thursday of December, and Ulysses S. Grant named November 18 in 1869, a week before the last Thursday. Until 1941, nothing but custom fixed the day.

What set 1901 apart was the shadow over it. McKinley had been shot at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo on September 6 and died on September 14. Roosevelt, at forty-two, became the youngest man ever to hold the office, at a moment of public fear about anarchism and the stability of the government. McKinley was the third president killed in thirty-six years, after Lincoln and Garfield. A Thanksgiving proclamation that ignored this would have rung false; one that dwelt only on the loss would have deepened the alarm. Roosevelt threaded the needle.

## Political impact
The proclamation was a wire story the day it was signed. The Associated Press moved it on Saturday, November 2, and over the next three days it ran across the country, often on page one and often in full. The *Evening Times-Republican* of Marshalltown, Iowa printed the full text on its front page that same day. The *San Jose Mercury-News*, the *San Francisco Call*, and Santa Barbara's *Morning Press* had it on November 3, and papers from South Dakota to Ohio, Indiana, New Mexico, and Iowa followed within the week. The text was even cabled to Manila.

Headline writers went straight for the passage on McKinley: "Speaks in Touching Terms of the Memory of the Late President" (*Morning Press*, November 3); "Refers to Nation's Sad Loss" (*San Francisco Call*, the same day).

Editorial pages took notice, and approved. "President Roosevelt's Thanksgiving proclamation is characteristic of the man," the *San Jose Mercury-News* wrote on November 3. "He says something and does not merely string together a lot of platitudes and conventionalities." The *Call* came back to the "duty to his fellow men" line on November 13, and the *Healdsburg Tribune* built a whole essay on the history of Thanksgiving proclamations around Roosevelt's.

The only jokes aimed elsewhere. "There is something to be thankful for beyond the scope of the president's Thanksgiving proclamation," the *Los Angeles Herald* quipped on November 6. "It will be a full year before election time comes again." No contemporary objection to the proclamation itself has been found.

Roosevelt would sign one every autumn of his presidency, eight in all.

## Legacy
The yearly presidential Thanksgiving proclamation has continued without a break from Lincoln's in 1863 to the present. (From 1815 to 1863, no president issued one at all.) For decades the date still rested on custom, until another Roosevelt moved it.

In 1939 November had five Thursdays. Franklin Roosevelt set Thanksgiving a week early, on November 23, and did the same in 1940 and 1941. He told reporters in August 1939 that he'd heard "complaints that Thanksgiving Day came too close to Christmas" and that retailers were "very anxious to have it set forward." The country split: twenty-five states kept the president's date, twenty-six kept Lincoln's, and three observed both. In May 1941, FDR conceded the experiment "did not work."

Congress settled it. The House passed a joint resolution naming the last Thursday of November; the Senate changed "last" to "fourth," so that, in Senator John Danaher's words, "everyone will know from now on what day is to be Thanksgiving Day." The House agreed on December 19, 1941, and Roosevelt signed it on December 26. Thanksgiving 1942 was the first held under law.

Theodore Roosevelt's eight Thanksgiving proclamations sit inside that long chain. The first of them remains a small, well-made example of how a president can use a purely ceremonial document to steady a country: grief acknowledged, continuity affirmed, and nothing asked of anyone but a day's reflection.

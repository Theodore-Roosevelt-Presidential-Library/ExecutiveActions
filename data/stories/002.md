## At a glance
On September 24, 1906, Theodore Roosevelt set aside 1,152.91 acres around Devils Tower in northeastern Wyoming as the first national monument in the United States. He used a law that was barely fifteen weeks old, the Antiquities Act, and by choosing a natural wonder instead of an ancient ruin he showed from the very first use that he would read that law broadly.

## Background
The proclamation protected the sheer column of igneous rock that, in the National Park Service's words, "rises 1,267 feet above the Belle Fourche River." The text itself names no acreage. The figure comes from the General Land Office map attached to the proclamation and signed by Commissioner William A. Richards, which reads "Containing 1152.91 acres."

Roosevelt acted under authority Congress had granted just 108 days earlier. The Antiquities Act of June 8, 1906, let a president protect "objects of historic or scientific interest" on federal land by proclamation, on his own, without waiting for a bill. Devils Tower was the first time anyone used it.

The proclamation calls the tower "such an extraordinary example of the effect of erosion in the higher mountains as to be a natural wonder and an object of historic and great scientific interest." It also issues a warning to "all unauthorized persons not to appropriate injure or destroy any feature of the natural tower," or to settle on the reserved land.

## Historical context
To read this as 1906 read it, look at the law it used and what that law was for. The Antiquities Act was fought for by archaeologists alarmed at "pot-hunters" stripping ancestral Puebloan ruins in the Southwest and selling the relics. Its language was drafted largely by the archaeologist Edgar Lee Hewett, endorsed by the American Anthropological Association and the Archaeological Institute of America, and carried in the House by Representative John F. Lacey of Iowa.

To get it through a wary Congress, its backers promised a small, careful tool. On June 5, 1906, Representative John H. Stephens of Texas asked Lacey whether the bill would "be anything like the forest-reserve bill, by which seventy or eighty million acres of land in the United States have been tied up." Lacey answered: "Certainly not. The object is entirely different. It is to preserve these old objects of special interest and the Indian remains in the pueblos in the Southwest, whilst the other reserves the forests and the water courses." Asked how much land would go, he said, "Not very much." The Act itself required that a reserved parcel be "confined to the smallest area compatible with the proper care and management of the object to be protected."

Roosevelt's very first use of the law stretched that intent. Devils Tower is not a ruin; it is a geological formation. The Act covered "objects of historic or scientific interest," and Roosevelt claimed both grounds at once. By making a natural wonder monument number one, he signaled that the antiquities law would reach scenery and science, not just ruins. That reading would soon carry [Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3).

The law and the moment left something out. To the Lakota and more than twenty other tribes, the formation was a sacred place: *Mato Tipila*, Bear Lodge, to the Lakota; *Na Kovehe*, Bear Lodge, to the Cheyenne; *Tso-aa*, a tree rock, to the Kiowa. The Antiquities Act made no mention of any Native interest. Its protection of the American past ran alongside the active dispossession of the living peoples whose past it was.

## Political impact
Locally, the designation drew little fight; no one was contesting the land. But it landed in the middle of a hot Wyoming argument over conservation. The state's lone congressman, Frank Mondell, was a forceful champion of opening public land to timber, grazing, and mining. He fought the Yellowstone game preserve in 1905 and opposed repeal of the Desert Land Act and the Timber and Stone Act, a stance that, he later wrote, "brought me into sharp opposition with the views of the President." On federal irrigation, though, he stood with Roosevelt.

Ten days before the Devils Tower proclamation, Mondell was already protesting a different withdrawal. In letters to the Secretary of the Interior, printed in the *Laramie Republican* on September 14, 1906, he objected to the late-July removal of "109 townships containing over two and a half millions of acres of lands, in Wyoming," from entry as coal land. "Against this suspension," he wrote, "I protest in the name of the people of the State on the ground that it is not warranted by law or justified by existing conditions." Nationally, the coal withdrawals of 1906 ran to roughly sixty-four million acres.

The election season sharpened the quarrel. Cheyenne's Democratic paper, the *Cheyenne Daily Leader*, charged on October 20, 1906, that "Mondell not only fought President Roosevelt to the bitter end, during the last session of congress on every important measure upon which the mind and heart of the President was centered, but since congress adjourned he has not let up in his antagonism to the nation's chief and the policy of the administration in putting a check to the domination and graft of the big corporations in this country, and particularly in Wyoming." The *Wyoming Tribune*, which backed Mondell, answered four days later.

To Western developers, every reservation, even a lone rock tower, was one more piece of the public domain closed to them by a president acting on his own say-so. As Roosevelt kept using the Antiquities Act, the worry its authors had tried to rule out came back: that "smallest area compatible" was being stretched past anything Congress had meant.

## Legacy
Devils Tower remains the founding unit of the national monument system. The power Roosevelt first used here has been used by presidents of both parties ever since, and contested ever since, on the same question raised in 1906: how much land, by one signature? The monument now covers 1,347 acres.

The site is still sacred to many tribes; the National Park Service names the Arapaho, Crow, Cheyenne, Shoshone, and Lakota among more than twenty. Under the 1995 Climbing Management Plan, worked out by a group that included both climbers and American Indian representatives, the Park Service asks visitors "throughout June" to "voluntarily refrain from climbing on the Tower or scrambling inside of the Tower Trail loop." June, in the Park Service's words, "is a culturally significant time when many (but not all) Indian ceremonies occur." A proposed June ban on commercial guiding was blocked in court in 1996 and withdrawn. Climbers challenged the voluntary closure, and in *Bear Lodge Multiple Use Association v. Babbitt* (1999) the federal appeals court held that they lacked standing, because the closure was voluntary and they had kept climbing. The courts left the voluntary June closure in place, and with it the unresolved tension the 1906 proclamation passed over.

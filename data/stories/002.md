## At a glance
On September 24, 1906, Theodore Roosevelt set aside 1,152.91 acres around Devils Tower in northeastern Wyoming as the first national monument in the United States. He used a law that was barely fifteen weeks old, the Antiquities Act, and by choosing a natural wonder instead of an ancient ruin he showed from the very first use that he would read that law broadly.

## Background
The proclamation protected the sheer column of igneous rock that, in the National Park Service's words, "rises 1,267 feet above the Belle Fourche River." The text itself names no acreage. The figure comes from the General Land Office map attached to the proclamation and signed by Commissioner William A. Richards, which reads "Containing 1152.91 acres."

Roosevelt acted under authority Congress had granted just 108 days earlier. The Antiquities Act of June 8, 1906, let a president protect "objects of historic or scientific interest" on federal land by proclamation, on his own, without waiting for a bill. Devils Tower was the first time anyone used it.

The proclamation calls the tower "such an extraordinary example of the effect of erosion in the higher mountains as to be a natural wonder and an object of historic and great scientific interest." It also issues a warning to "all unauthorized persons not to appropriate injure or destroy any feature of the natural tower," or to settle on the reserved land.

## Historical context
To read this as 1906 read it, look at the law it used and what that law was for. The Antiquities Act was fought for by archaeologists alarmed at "pot-hunters" stripping ancestral Puebloan ruins in the Southwest and selling the relics. Its language was drafted largely by the archaeologist Edgar Lee Hewett, endorsed by the American Anthropological Association and the Archaeological Institute of America, and carried in the House by Representative John F. Lacey of Iowa.

To get it through a wary Congress, its backers promised a small, careful tool. On June 5, 1906, Representative John H. Stephens of Texas asked Lacey whether the bill would "be anything like the forest-reserve bill, by which seventy or eighty million acres of land in the United States have been tied up." Lacey answered: "Certainly not. The object is entirely different. It is to preserve these old objects of special interest and the Indian remains in the pueblos in the Southwest, whilst the other reserves the forests and the water courses." Asked how much land would go, he said, "Not very much." The Act itself required that a reserved parcel be "confined to the smallest area compatible with the proper care and management of the object to be protected."

Roosevelt's very first use of the law stretched that intent. Devils Tower is not a ruin; it is a geological formation. The Act covered "objects of historic or scientific interest," and Roosevelt claimed both grounds at once. By making a natural wonder monument number one, he signaled that the antiquities law would reach scenery and science, not just ruins. That reading would soon carry [Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3).

The law and the moment left something out. To the Lakota and more than twenty other tribes, the formation was a sacred place: *Mato Tipila*, Bear Lodge, to the Lakota; *Na Kovehe*, Bear Lodge, to the Cheyenne; *Tso-aa*, a tree rock, to the Kiowa. The Antiquities Act made no mention of any Native interest. Its protection of the American past ran alongside the active dispossession of the living peoples whose past it was.

## Political impact
Locally, the designation drew little fight; no one was contesting the land. But it landed in the middle of a hot Wyoming argument over conservation. The state's lone congressman, Frank Mondell, was a forceful champion of opening public land to timber, grazing, and mining. He fought the Yellowstone game preserve in 1905 and opposed repeal of the Desert Land Act and the Timber and Stone Act, a stance that, he later wrote in his memoir, "brought me into sharp opposition with the views of the President." On federal irrigation, though, his enthusiasm put him on Roosevelt's side; the Reclamation Act of 1902 was one of the President's signature first-term achievements.

Mondell was already protesting a different withdrawal before the Devils Tower proclamation was signed. By mid-September 1906 he had sent two letters to the Secretary of the Interior about an order of late July that closed 109 Wyoming townships, by his count more than two and a half million acres, to every kind of entry as coal land. The first letter laid out the withdrawal; the second entered a formal protest against what he called a wholesale suspension of the land laws and asked that the lands be reopened. The *Laramie Republican* printed his summary of the letters on September 14, 1906, at least ten days before the Devils Tower proclamation, and the *Sheridan Post* carried them a week later. Nationally, the coal withdrawals of 1906 ran to roughly sixty-four million acres (see [The Coal-Land Withdrawals, 1906–1909](story:27)).

The election season sharpened the quarrel. On October 20, 1906, two and a half weeks before the election, the *Cheyenne Daily Leader*, the city's Democratic paper, ran an editorial headed "Mondell vs. Roosevelt." It charged that the Republican congressman had opposed the President on every major measure of the last session and had kept up his opposition since, especially to the administration's effort to check the power of big corporations in Wyoming. It also quoted his coal-land protest, then on file at the Interior Department. The *Wyoming Tribune*, which backed Mondell, reprinted the *Leader*'s paragraphs and answered them four days later under the head "Mr. Mondell and the President."

To Western developers, every reservation, even a lone rock tower, was one more piece of the public domain closed to them by a president acting on his own say-so. As Roosevelt kept using the Antiquities Act, the worry its authors had tried to rule out came back: that "smallest area compatible" was being stretched past anything Congress had meant.

## Legacy
### The first of many
Devils Tower remains the founding unit of the national monument system. The power Roosevelt first used here has been used by presidents of both parties ever since, and contested ever since, on the same question raised in 1906: how much land, by one signature? Roosevelt would soon apply the same broad reading to [Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3). The monument today covers 1,347 acres, about 194 more than the 1,152.91 Roosevelt reserved.

### A sacred place and a climbing rock
The tower is still sacred to many tribes. The National Park Service says "over 20" on one page and "over two dozen" on another, and names the Arapaho, Crow, Cheyenne, Shoshone, and Lakota among them.

The Park Service's Climbing Management Plan of February 1995 was worked out by a group that included both climbers and American Indian representatives. Its central piece is a voluntary June closure: "Throughout June, the park asks visitors to voluntarily refrain from climbing on the Tower or scrambling inside of the Tower Trail loop." June, in the Park Service's words, "is a culturally significant time when many (but not all) Indian ceremonies occur." The request goes to all visitors, not climbers alone, and the Park Service reports that, though voluntary, it has sharply reduced June climbing. A 2006 update to the plan kept it.

The plan first included a June ban on commercial guiding as well. A court blocked that ban with a preliminary injunction in June 1996, and the Interior Secretary withdrew it that December. Climbers also challenged the voluntary closure. The federal district court in Wyoming upheld the plan on its merits in 1998. The Tenth Circuit affirmed on April 26, 1999, in *Bear Lodge Multiple Use Association v. Babbitt*, but on narrower ground: because the closure was voluntary and the climbers had kept climbing, they had "alleged no injury and, therefore, lacked standing to sue." The Supreme Court declined to hear the case in 2000. The courts left the voluntary June closure in place, and with it the unresolved tension the 1906 proclamation passed over.

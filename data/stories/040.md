## At a glance
On September 4, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt restored to the public domain a forty-acre tract near Lawton, Oklahoma Territory, that had been held for the Fort Sill Indian sub-agency. The release let the young town buy the land for a cemetery. Today it forms the western part of Lawton's Highland Cemetery.

## Background
This is narrow land bookkeeping: a parcel walled off for one federal use is formally "restored to the public domain" so it can be put to a new one. Its closest sibling is Proclamation 478 of June 23, 1902, which freed a 38.60-acre Kiowa and Comanche agency tract beside Anadarko for a town park — the same two statutes, the same agency country, ten weeks earlier ([The Public-Domain Restorations of 1902](story:37)).

When the Kiowa, Comanche, Apache and Wichita lands were opened to settlers in 1901, a forty-acre tract — the southwest quarter of the northwest quarter of section 19, township 2 north, range 11 west — was held back for the Fort Sill Indian sub-agency. By 1902 the agency no longer needed it. The town of Lawton, laid out as a county seat for the 1901 opening, wanted it for a cemetery. The proclamation notes that the land lay "within one and a half miles" of the city, and that mattered: the law that let a town buy land for a cemetery allowed it only "within three miles."

The proclamation names two statutes, and they do different jobs.

- **The act of September 30, 1890** is a two-sentence law "to authorize entry of the public lands by incorporated cities and towns for cemetery and park purpose[s]." It let an incorporated town *purchase* up to a quarter section of public land "not reserved for public use" within three miles of its limits. That phrase is why this proclamation had to exist. As long as the forty acres stood reserved for the agency, Lawton could not buy them at any price. And Lawton was to pay for the ground, not receive it as a gift.
- **Section six of the act of June 6, 1900** ratified the Kiowa, Comanche and Apache cession — the "Jerome Agreement" of October 1892. The only presidential power in it is the direction that the ceded lands "be opened to settlement by proclamation of the President." It says nothing about reserving agency tracts or restoring them. The agency land had been kept out of the 1901 opening by McKinley's blanket exception of "lands now used, occupied, or set apart for military, agency, school, school farm, religious, Indian cemetery, wood reserve, forest reserve, or other public uses." So Proclamation 492 read a power to restore land into the power to open it — an inference the Interior Department evidently treated as settled, since Proclamation 478 had made the same one in June.

One small oddity sits in the dating clause. The proclamation is "Done at the City of Washington" on September 4, but Roosevelt was not in Washington that day. On September 3 his carriage was struck by a trolley at Pittsfield, Massachusetts, and he cut short his New England tour and went home to Sagamore Hill. On September 4 he was at Oyster Bay, entertaining Grand Duke Boris of Russia at luncheon. He reached Washington about 6:45 p.m. on September 5 and stayed "exactly one hour and a half" before leaving by train for Chattanooga. The Washington dateline was simply the standing formula of these documents.

## Historical context
Lawton was one of the instant towns of the 1901 opening. Two reservations were opened at once: the lands of the Wichita and affiliated bands, ceded under an act of 1895, and the Kiowa, Comanche and Apache lands ceded under the 1900 act. Every member of the tribes had already received an individual allotment before the opening, and 480,000 acres of tribal grazing land were held out of it entirely. Registration ran July 10–26, 1901, at El Reno and at a provisional land office "in the immediate vicinity of Fort Sill," "until suitable quarters can be provided at Lawton," as McKinley's proclamation put it. The drawing at El Reno, beginning July 29, decided only the order in which registered homesteaders could file on the remaining land. A brand-new town then had to assemble the ordinary furniture of a settled community — streets, schools, a burial ground — on land only just opened.

The cession beneath that land was still in court on the day Roosevelt signed. Article 12 of the Medicine Lodge treaty of 1867 provided that no cession of the Kiowa-Comanche reservation would be valid "unless executed and signed by at least three-fourths of all the adult male Indians occupying the same." In June 1901, before the opening, the Kiowa leader Lone Wolf and others had sued the Secretary of the Interior, arguing that the Jerome Agreement never met that bar. In September 1902, *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* was on appeal to the Supreme Court. It was argued on October 23, 1902, and decided on January 5, 1903, when the Court held that Congress could dispose of tribal lands without the consent the treaty required. When this proclamation issued, then, the title to every 1901 homestead and townsite in the cession — and to the forty acres it handed to Lawton — still rested on an agreement the tribes were actively contesting.

Who asked for the release? The proclamation answers on its face: "the city authorities of said city desire to make entry thereof," and the land was "no longer required" by the sub-agency. The city's petition and the Indian Office's finding have not been found. The annual reports of the Commissioner of Indian Affairs for 1902 and 1903 and of the General Land Office for 1903 say nothing about them.

## Political impact
As a presidential document, this kind of action was rare. Volume 32 of the *Statutes at Large* prints forty-four proclamations from March 1901 to March 1903, and only three of Roosevelt's restore a single tract to the public domain for a new owner: Proclamation 476 (a section at Capitan Grande, California), Proclamation 478 (Anadarko's park), and this one. The General Land Office handled many restorations of withdrawn land by departmental order. A proclamation was needed here because the tract had been reserved under an act that left openings, and their exceptions, to the President.

Whether anyone outside Lawton noticed is not known. A search of California newspapers for September–December 1902 found no wire pickup, which is what a forty-acre local release would predict, but the Oklahoma Territory papers, where any reaction would surface, have not yet been searched. No contemporary objection has been found. If there was opposition, it would most plausibly have come not from settlers but from the tribes, whose suit against the cession was then pending.

## Legacy
The forty acres became, and remain, the western part of Lawton's Highland Cemetery. In today's federal survey, the tract the proclamation describes is Government Lot 2 of section 19 — a west-edge section, where the westernmost quarter-quarters are numbered as lots — recorded at 38.06 acres rather than a nominal forty. The Bureau of Land Management's survey layer places Highland Cemetery's grounds across Lot 2 and the quarter-quarter just east of it, and the cemetery's 1936 WPA entrance arch stands at the west edge of Lot 2 on NW Fort Sill Boulevard. Lawton's municipal cemetery, with more than 20,000 burials, stands in part on land released by this proclamation.

The proclamation is also one more example of the machinery that steadily moved reserved and tribal land into settler and town use across Oklahoma Territory — the same machinery seen at Fort Hall (Proclamation 472) and in the restorations of 1902. And it shows how quickly the *Lone Wolf* question was overtaken on the ground: by the time the Supreme Court ruled, the forty acres had already been released to Lawton. In 1904, Proclamation 520 released a 120-acre Fort Sill Indian boarding-school tract next to Lawton to the city for a park, under the same two statutes ([Town Parks in Oklahoma Territory](story:52)).

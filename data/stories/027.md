## At a glance
In the summer of 1906 Theodore Roosevelt had the government stop giving away its coal. On his direction the Interior Department withdrew millions of acres of public land believed to hold coal from entry under the cheap agricultural land laws, while geologists worked out which tracts held workable coal and what it was worth. Roosevelt signed no executive order to do it, and no statute expressly gave him the power. The fight over that power, and over how the coal should finally be handed out, ran long past his presidency.

## Background
Roosevelt set the withdrawals in motion with a letter to the Secretary of the Interior on June 29, 1906. The first withdrawal order followed on July 26, 1906. A chain of orders came after it that fall and winter:

- **July 26, 1906** — the first withdrawal, closing the land to all forms of entry
- **October 10, 11, 13 and 15** — further withdrawals, closing land to coal entry only
- **November 7** — orders brought into line with the July 26 form
- **November 12** — Alaska
- **December 17** — all the orders narrowed to coal entry
- **January 15, 1907** — protection for rights acquired in good faith before the withdrawals

Classification, restoration and further withdrawals continued until Roosevelt left office in March 1909.

**Not an executive order.** Every one of these withdrawals was a departmental order: a letter from the Secretary or Acting Secretary of the Interior to the Commissioner of the General Land Office, who carried it out by wiring the local land offices. Acting Secretary Thomas Ryan signed the first one, because Secretary Ethan Allen Hitchcock was away. When Roosevelt's secretary, William Loeb, asked Interior to draft "such an order for the President's signature," Hitchcock explained in a December 17, 1906 report to the President why none was needed: "This is the usual method of procedure in such matters and therefore it was not necessary to prepare and forward a formal Executive order for your signature." The Supreme Court later described the first one the same way, as "a departmental order dated July 26, 1906." Roosevelt himself wrote loosely in his *Autobiography* that "by executive order the coal lands were withdrawn," but coal withdrawals did not become formal executive orders until after the Pickett Act of 1910.

**How much land.** The numbers moved constantly, because land came off the market and went back on as the ground was examined. The General Land Office reported that by June 30, 1907, 66,716,240 acres had been withdrawn in all. Of that, 8,524,800 acres had already been restored to entry and 31,568,400 were still waiting to be classified, leaving 58,191,440 acres withdrawn on that date. By the end of the administration the gross total was higher. The National Conservation Commission reported in February 1909 that Roosevelt "withdrew over 68,000,000 acres," and his *Autobiography* gives "about sixty-eight million acres of coal land in the United States, including Alaska." The total also shrank as the work went on: the U.S. Geological Survey later reported that of roughly 68 million acres withdrawn in the first two years, about 36 million were restored once geologists walked the ground and found no workable coal.

**The geologists.** The Survey's job was to map which tracts actually held workable coal and appraise what it was worth. Charles D. Walcott led the Survey when the withdrawals began and stayed until April 30, 1907, after his election as Secretary of the Smithsonian. His successor, George Otis Smith, appointed April 5, 1907, oversaw most of the coal classification and held the job until December 1930, longer than anyone else in the Survey's history.

**A question frozen, not settled.** The withdrawal did not decide what would become of the coal. It stopped the clock. Roosevelt's preferred answer was that the government keep title to the coal and lease the right to mine it under regulation, collecting royalties and keeping control. Failing that, he said, the coal should at least be sold apart from the surface, so a farmer could still get his field while the nation kept the mineral beneath it. That "split-estate" idea, worked up under Secretary of the Interior James R. Garfield, is the compromise that eventually became law.

## Historical context
Coal ran the age, and the government was giving away land that held it at a fraction of its value. Under the land laws a person could acquire public land as if it were farm ground, for a dollar or two an acre. If coal lay beneath it, the buyer got a fortune for the price of a homestead. Speculators, and behind them the railroads and coal combinations, had learned to work this seam through dummy entrymen and outright fraud, assembling coal empires out of land meant for settlers.

The men around Roosevelt — Gifford Pinchot in the Forest Service, Garfield at Interior, the geologists at the Survey — did not make a sentimental argument. They made the one that ran through all of the era's conservation: a finite public resource was being wasted and monopolized, and the next generation was owed fair value and fair access, not a fire sale.

Roosevelt put the case to Congress in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907. The conservation section opens: "The conservation of our natural resources and their proper use constitute the fundamental problem which underlies almost every other problem of our National life." A few paragraphs on, he warned:

> "We are prone to speak of the resources of this country as inexhaustible; this is not so. The mineral wealth of the country, the coal, iron, oil, gas, and the like, does not reproduce itself, and therefore is certain to be exhausted ultimately; and wastefulness in dealing with it to-day means that our descendants will feel the exhaustion a generation or two before they otherwise would."

Two paragraphs after that, he named who was getting the coal and what he wanted done about it: "In the eastern United States the mineral fuels have already passed into the hands of large private owners, and those of the West are rapidly following. It is obvious that these fuels should be conserved and not wasted, and it would be well to protect the people against unjust and extortionate prices, so far as that can still be done." In the same paragraph he laid out his plan:

> "In my judgment the Government should have the right to keep the fee of the coal, oil, and gas fields in its own possession and to lease the rights to develop them under proper regulations; or else, if the Congress will not adopt this method, the coal deposits should be sold under limitations, to conserve them as public utilities, the right to mine coal being separated from the title to the soil."

The withdrawal was the tool that stopped the bleeding while Congress argued. No more patents could issue on the withdrawn ground until the government decided, on purpose, how to part with the coal, if at all.

## Political impact
The withdrawals drew the same objections the forest reserves drew, only sharper, because coal was money in the ground and the legal footing was openly disputed. Western settlers and their representatives protested that homesteaders acting in good faith were being shut out of land they were entitled to claim. Coal and railroad interests protested the loss of cheap acquisition. And constitutional critics protested loudest of all: Congress alone controlled the disposal of the public domain, they said, and a president who could withdraw millions of acres Congress had opened was legislating by decree. The charge that Roosevelt had usurped legislative power followed his whole conservation program.

Their objection deserves a fair hearing. The land laws were Congress's to write, the settler's claim was a real and long-honored expectation, and nobody could yet say where a president's authority over the public domain stopped. No statute granted the sweeping withdrawal power Roosevelt was using. He never pretended otherwise. In his *Autobiography* he recalled that "there was a great clamor that I was usurping legislative power," and answered: "Under this interpretation of executive power I did and caused to be done many things not previously done by the President and the heads of the departments. I did not usurp power, but I did greatly broaden the use of executive power."

What made the coal withdrawals unusual is easy to overstate. Executive withdrawal of public land was old and common. Presidents had been withdrawing public land for some eighty years, by the hundreds of orders, for Indian reservations, military posts and bird reserves. Nor was coal the biggest land action of Roosevelt's own presidency. The forest reserves already stood at about 107 million acres in 1906, and he added more than 148 million acres of them over his two terms, more than double the coal figure. The difference lay in the legal footing. The forest reserves rested on an express grant from Congress, in the Creative Act of 1891 and the Organic Act of 1897. The coal withdrawals rested on nothing but the President's judgment of the public interest.

Inside the government, the work went on through the ordinary machinery. Interior issued the orders and narrowed them as it went: by December 17, 1906 every withdrawal had been confined to coal entry, and an order of January 15, 1907 protected rights acquired in good faith before the land was closed. The Geological Survey's classifiers sorted the ground tract by tract, and by June 30, 1907 the General Land Office had already restored 8,524,800 acres to entry.

On the substance, Roosevelt largely carried the day; selling coal fields at farmland prices was hard to defend once the fraud was out in the open. The real fight was over how to hand the coal back out. Roosevelt wanted the government to keep the land and lease it. Much of Congress wanted the land disposed of, with the coal at most reserved, and Congress began on its own terms. The Coal Lands Act of March 3, 1909, passed the day before Roosevelt left office, let a good-faith farm entryman take the surface of land later found to hold coal, while the coal itself was reserved to the United States. In June 1910 Congress reopened the withdrawn lands on that basis, and the same month the Pickett Act of June 25, 1910 finally gave the President explicit authority to withdraw public lands, an admission that the power had been in doubt. That deadlock over leasing, not the withdrawal itself, outlasted Roosevelt's presidency.

## Legacy
The withdrawals set off a chain of law that took more than a decade to finish, and it finished close to where Roosevelt had aimed.

**The courts.** In *Plested v. Abbey* (1913) the Supreme Court described the first withdrawal for what it was, "a departmental order dated July 26, 1906." Two years later, in *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.* (1915), the Court upheld even the withdrawals made before the Pickett Act. The case concerned an oil withdrawal President Taft made on September 27, 1909, after Roosevelt left office, but the Court folded the coal withdrawals into the same long practice, noting that "public land containing coal was withdrawn 'pending the enactment of new legislation'" and that "there was no repudiation of the order or of the practice" by Congress. The Court found that presidents had issued "a multitude of Executive Orders which operated to withdraw public land," that "scores and hundreds of these orders have been made," and that the land involved "has aggregated millions of acres." It counted at least 252 executive orders reserving public land for purposes no statute named: 99 for Indian reservations, 109 for military reservations and 44 for bird reserves. A long practice, known to Congress and accepted by it, the Court reasoned, implied Congress's consent. The vote was 5 to 3. Justice Joseph Rucker Lamar wrote for the Court; Justice Day dissented, joined by Justices McKenna and Van Devanter; Justice McReynolds took no part.

**Leasing at last.** Roosevelt's own preferred answer, keep the land and lease the coal, became general law with the Mineral Leasing Act of February 25, 1920. It set up the federal leasing system for coal, oil, gas and other minerals that still governs the mineral estate of the public lands.

**The withdrawal power.** The withdrawals did not break the coal monopolies on their own. What they established was a plain principle: the minerals under the public domain were a public asset, to be managed for value rather than surrendered for the price of a farm. They also left behind, in the withdrawal power itself, one of the most durable and most argued-over tools a president has for acting on the land without waiting for Congress. That the tool is still contested more than a century later is part of the record, not a footnote to a settled victory.

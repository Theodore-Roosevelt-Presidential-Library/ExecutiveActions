## At a glance
Between February 3 and February 27, 1909, with weeks left in his presidency, Roosevelt signed a run of executive orders that added more federal bird refuges than any comparable stretch of his time in office. One order locked away the whole Northwestern Hawaiian chain, where feather hunters had been slaughtering seabirds for the hat trade. Another turned seventeen Western irrigation reservoirs into bird preserves at a stroke. Seven more, signed in a single day, reached up the Alaskan coast and out to Puerto Rico and the Farallones.

## Background
Each order used the same simple form: the land was reserved "for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds," and it became "unlawful for any person to hunt, trap, capture, wilfully disturb, or kill any bird of any kind whatever, or take the eggs of such birds" inside it.

**Hawaii.** Executive Order 1019 (February 3) reserved a dozen named islets and reefs "at and near the extreme western extension of the Hawaiian Archipelago": Cure Island, Pearl and Hermes Reef, Lysianski or Pell Island, Laysan Island, Mary Reef, Dowsetts Reef, Gardiner Island, Two Brothers Reef, French Frigate Shoal, Necker Island, Frost Shoal, and Bird Island. It was to be "known as the Hawaiian Islands Reservation." It covered the whole Northwestern Hawaiian chain except Midway Atoll, which the Navy already held.

**The reservoirs.** Executive Order 1032 (February 25) took a different approach. Instead of one place, it named seventeen federal reclamation reservoir sites in ten Western states:

> Salt River, Arizona; East Park, California; Deer Flat and Minidoka, Idaho; Willow Creek, Montana; Carlsbad and Rio Grande, New Mexico; Cold Springs, Oregon; Belle Fourche, South Dakota; Strawberry Valley, Utah; Keechelus Lake, Kachess Lake, Clealum Lake, Bumping Lake and Conconully, Washington, and Shoshone and Pathfinder, Wyoming

The lands along their flow lines became bird preserves "subject to Reclamation Service uses under the provisions of the act approved June 17, 1902." The water projects were making new open water and marsh in dry country; the order laid a bird sanctuary over them while leaving the Reclamation Service's use first in line (see [The Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21)).

**February 27.** A cluster of single-site orders followed, several narrower than their popular names suggest:
- **Bering Sea** (Executive Order 1037): Saint Matthew Island, Hall Island, and Pinnacle Islet.
- **Fire Island**, Cook Inlet (Executive Order 1038): a preserve for **Alaska moose**, not birds.
- **Tuxedni** (Executive Order 1039): Chisik and Egg Islands.
- **Saint Lazaria** (Executive Order 1040) and the **Yukon Delta** (Executive Order 1041), Alaska.
- **Culebra** (Executive Order 1042, *Establishment of a Bird Reservation on the Island of Culebra, Porto Rico*): the islands of the Culebra group, **excepting Culebra Island itself**.
- **Farallon** (Executive Order 1043): only "the Middle and North Farallon islands and other rocks to the Northwest of same," not the whole group.
- **Pribilof** (Executive Order 1044): Walrus Island and Otter Island, not the big fur-seal islands of Saint Paul and Saint George.

The rest of the month's orders were ordinary business: a bank-examiner exception, customs and Treasury reinstatements, lighthouse land in Minnesota and on Michigan Island in Wisconsin, more time for the Spanish Treaty Claims Commission, an agricultural experiment station near Rampart, Alaska, a larger naval reservation at Subic Bay in the Philippines, a new internal-revenue district in California, and a wireless-telegraph station at Nome.

## Historical context
The bird orders answered the same plume-and-feather crisis that had driven the refuge program since Pelican Island in 1903 ([Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1)). The Hawaiian order answered a specific outrage. The Northwestern islands, above all Laysan, with its millions of nesting albatrosses and terns and its own Laysan duck, had become a killing ground for feather hunters supplying the millinery trade. Raids organized out of Honolulu slaughtered seabirds by the hundreds of thousands for wings and plumage; the operation of Max Schlemmer, who held the guano lease on Laysan, is the case usually named.

The push for the order came through a short chain, and not where one might expect. Press reports of Schlemmer's plumage dealings reached William Dutcher, president of the National Association of Audubon Societies, who sent a clipping to the naturalist William Alanson Bryan, then in Washington. Bryan took the matter to the State Department in person. On February 3, 1909, the Interior Department sent over a memorandum recommending that the President reserve the islands for native birds, and Roosevelt signed Executive Order 1019 that same day. The Audubon Association's own earlier Hawaiian work had been diplomatic: in 1903 it had moved the State Department to press Japan to forbid its subjects to poach on the islets.

Roosevelt, who by 1909 counted the bird reservations among the surest goods of his conservation program, used the one tool that needed no act of Congress: reserving unclaimed public land by executive order. The reservoir order reflected a second insight of the reclamation era. The dams the government was building in the arid West made ready-made waterfowl habitat, and an order could protect the birds without touching the water right.

## Political impact
Like the rest of the reservation program, these orders passed with little national notice: no floor fight, no lawsuit, no headline. The lands themselves explain much of that. Remote reefs, Alaskan islets, and reservoir margins in empty country took nothing from any established interest. Where the orders did touch a going concern, the reclamation reservoirs, the wording put the bird preserve second to the Reclamation Service. Congress's broader unease with Roosevelt's free hand over the public domain, which had produced the 1907 rider stripping his power to create forest reserves in six states, never settled on the bird reservations. Small and scattered, they made a poor target.

The bird-protection movement claimed the results as its own. The National Association of Audubon Societies' annual report for 1909 took broad credit for "the fifty-three National Bird Reservations now in existence." That was a blanket sentence, though. The Hawaiian reservation appears in the report's tables as a single line, and none of the Association's forty-four paid wardens worked in Hawaii.

**The test on Laysan.** The Hawaiian reservation was soon put to work. On the morning of February 2, 1910, the revenue cutter *Thetis*, under Captain W. V. E. Jacobs, came into Honolulu harbor with twenty-three poachers seized on Laysan and Lisianski, fifteen of them taken on Laysan, and their baled feathers in her hold. The Honolulu papers of February 3 and 4 reported "[t]wo hundred and fifty-nine thousand bird wings, cut from stunned goonies, cured and baled for shipment, together with two and a quarter tons of loose feathers," valued at $112,000. The men were turned over to the customs authorities. The bird killers had been living in the guano company's buildings, and the *Pacific Commercial Advertiser* of February 3 described Max Schlemmer as "the 'King of Laysan Island,' whose lease on the guano deposits of Laysan was recently terminated by the United States government." It quoted his admission that as to the Lisianski men "he was responsible for their acts, as he was 'the head of the company' operating there."

Even so, Schlemmer was never charged over the birds. The federal grand jury's indictments of March 21, 1910 "have nothing to do with birds or feathers," the Honolulu press reported. They dealt only with his bringing Japanese workers into the United States without passing the immigration officer, and he was prosecuted under the immigration laws.

Later federal accounts told the raid in rounder numbers. Henry W. Henshaw of the Biological Survey wrote in 1912 of "the plumage of more than a quarter of a million birds" and placed the cutter's return in January 1910, and Hornaday's *Our Vanishing Wild Life* (1913) went higher still, to "about three hundred thousand birds." The Honolulu papers of the day are the better guide to both the date and the count. Henshaw's 1911 account of the reservation names no one as the advocate behind it.

## Legacy
### Hawaii
The Hawaiian Islands Reservation proved the most consequential of the group. It became the Hawaiian Islands National Wildlife Refuge and, nearly a century later, part of Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument, established by Proclamation 8031 on June 15, 2006, under the Antiquities Act, the same law Roosevelt had used for Devils Tower and the Grand Canyon ([Devils Tower National Monument, Wyoming](story:2)). It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as a mixed natural and cultural site on July 30, 2010, and is among the largest protected areas on earth. Laysan's birds, once nearly stripped for hats, are its emblem.

### Alaska
Proclamation 2416 of July 25, 1940 renamed the Bering Sea, Tuxedni and Saint Lazaria reservations as national wildlife refuges. The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of December 2, 1980 then "redesignated as subunits" eleven existing refuges and added about 460,000 acres to form the Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge. Bering Sea and the Pribilof islets (named in the act as "Pribilof (Walrus and Otter Islands)") went into its Bering Sea Unit. Saint Lazaria and Tuxedni went into its Gulf of Alaska Unit. The subunits no longer carry separate acreages.

The Yukon Delta reservation did not last. Executive Order 3642 revoked it on February 27, 1922, thirteen years to the day after Roosevelt signed it. The Agriculture Department's press release of March 9, 1922, headed "Yukon Delta Bird Reservation Canceled," said the reservation "comprised approximately 8,000,000 acres," that "with the limited funds at the disposal of the Department it was impossible adequately to administer it," and that the country was "of too marshy a character to be desirable for settlement," which "protects the birds to a great extent." Today's Yukon Delta National Wildlife Refuge has a different family tree. The 1980 Alaska lands act built it from the Clarence Rhode National Wildlife Range and the Hazen Bay and Nunivak refuges, plus about 13.4 million new acres. The 1909 order is not part of that line.

The Fire Island moose preserve had an on-and-off life. Executive Order 3421 revoked it on March 26, 1921, Executive Order 4131 restored it on January 22, 1925, and Public Land Order 639 revoked it for good on April 26, 1950. It ran from 1909 to 1950, with a four-year gap. Some accounts point instead to Public Land Order 1152 of 1955, but that order revoked a New York refuge and never mentions Fire Island. Cook Inlet Region, Inc. now owns most of the island, about 3,600 of its 4,000 acres by one 2012 newspaper count. The company says it acquired the land as surplus federal property in 1982 through the Cook Inlet Land Exchange. The island now hosts Anchorage's Fire Island wind farm.

### Culebra and the Farallones
Culebra survives as the Caribbean's first federal wildlife reserve. The Farallon reservation grew. Roosevelt's order covered only the North and Middle Farallones and Noonday Rock; Public Land Order 4671 added the main South Farallon island in 1969, and Public Law 93-550 of December 26, 1974 made the 141-acre Farallon Wilderness, every island of the group except Southeast Farallon.

### The reservoirs
The seventeen reservoir reservations had a mixed afterlife, and most were revoked as the reclamation projects and their uses changed.
- **1921–1922.** Executive Order 3468 of May 20, 1921 revoked East Park and four of the five Washington lake sites, on the Biological Survey's advice that they "lack plant food and other requirements for useful bird reservations." The same order dropped the Loch Katrine reservation in Wyoming ([The Executive Orders of Late 1908](story:103)). Executive Order 3725 of August 18, 1922 revoked Shoshone and Pathfinder, the Survey reporting them "of too little value to be retained."
- **The New Deal swaps.** Executive Order 6086 of March 28, 1933 revoked the Rio Grande reservation and reserved the same ground "as a refuge and breeding ground for birds and wild animals," to be known as the Rio Grande Wild Life Refuge. That refuge was itself revoked by Public Land Order 378 on July 1, 1947. Executive Order 7417 of July 17, 1936 did the same for Minidoka, taking in two later enlargements of 1912 and 1930. As printed, that 1936 order misdates Roosevelt's order as February 5, 1909; the correct date is February 25.
- **The public land orders.** Carlsbad, renamed Carlsbad National Wildlife Refuge by Proclamation 2416 in 1940, was released by Public Land Order 392 on August 11, 1947, which put it at about 18,679.66 acres. Strawberry Valley in Utah followed on March 16, 1961, and Salt River in Arizona on March 21, 1961. Belle Fourche in South Dakota, about 13,680 acres, was released on July 13, 1964, and Willow Creek in Montana on May 29, 1968. Conconully, the last of the Washington sites, lasted until Public Land Order 2072 of March 24, 1960.
- **Still refuges.** Two of the seventeen were never revoked. **Deer Flat** in Idaho is now Deer Flat National Wildlife Refuge, which the Fish and Wildlife Service dates to the Deer Flat Bird Reservation of February 25, 1909. **Cold Springs** in Oregon is now Cold Springs National Wildlife Refuge, which the Service says was "established as the 'Cold Springs Reservation'" by Executive Order 1032.

### The count
Across his presidency Roosevelt established fifty-one federal bird reservations: twenty-six created by individual orders, the seventeen created at once by Executive Order 1032, and eight signed on February 27 and March 3, 1909 (the last of them at Bogoslof; see [The Executive Orders of March 1909](story:106)). Published totals differ because of how they count. *Bird-Lore* claimed fifty-three in 1909, and every circulating total counts the seventeen reservoir sites one by one; counting Executive Order 1032 as a single reservation, the most Roosevelt's total can be is thirty-five. The moose preserve on Fire Island is not among the fifty-one, and the national game preserves, such as the Wichita and the National Bison Range ([The Wichita Forest and Game Preserve](story:18); [The National Bison Range](story:19)), are a separate category. That is why the Fish and Wildlife Service's combined figure of fifty-five is not a count of bird reservations. These fifty-one are the seed of today's National Wildlife Refuge System.

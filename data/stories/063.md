## At a glance
In 1906 Theodore Roosevelt signed seventy-one forest-reserve proclamations, more than one a week, from Utah's Uintah reserve in January to New Mexico's Taos and Arizona's Tumacacori in November. Forty-two created reserves on land never reserved before. The rest renamed, enlarged, trimmed or merged reserves already on the map. Twenty-eight came in a single three-day burst in early November, just as Roosevelt was leaving for Panama.

## Background
Every one of the seventy-one is short. Rather than tracing boundaries line by line, each one reserves the lands "shown ... on the diagram forming a part hereof," and the *Statutes at Large* print a township map with each, captioned "Forest Service, U.S. Dept. of Agriculture, 1906. Compiled from G. L. O. Plats." That map-based form had first appeared with the Yuba reserve in late 1905; by 1906 it was standard.

The year's work breaks down this way:

- **Forty-two new reserves:** Beaver, La Sal, Fruita, North Platte, Helena, Vernon, Ruby Mountains, Fillmore, San Luis Obispo, Monterey, Pinnacles, Heppner, Crazy Mountains, Kootenai, Wasatch, Goose Lake, Fremont, Lolo, Long Pine, Siskiyou, Mount Taylor, Sierra Madre, Gallinas, Magdalena, Ekalaka, Snowy Mountains, Raft River, Lemhi, Peloncillo, San Mateo, Salmon River, Baboquivari, Independence, Charleston, Big Hole, Huachuca, Coeur d'Alene, Manzano, Pryor Mountains, Missoula, Taos and Tumacacori.
- **Two new names over old reserves:** Blue Mountains, in Oregon (March 15), took in "the greater portion of The Baker City Forest Reserve," declared that name "abolished," and returned the rest of Baker City to the public domain. Bear River (May 28) absorbed the whole Logan reserve of 1903 and retired its name.
- **Twenty-three enlargements of twenty-one reserves:** Uintah (twice), Sevier, Manti, Fish Lake, Gallatin, Ashland, Weiser, Wichita, Hell Gate (twice), Lincoln, Black Mesa, Stanislaus, Shasta, Santa Barbara, Diamond Mountain, Chiricahua, Kootenai, Priest River, Lolo, Sawtooth and Jemez. Kootenai and Lolo were each enlarged within three months of being created.
- **Three boundary changes:** the May 29 Uintah proclamation only cut land out, "released and excluded" and returned to the public domain. Grand Canyon (August 8) and Little Belt (November 6) both added and cut land.
- **One merger:** on September 17 the Tahoe and Yuba reserves were "superseded" and a single, larger Tahoe Forest Reserve "established in place thereof." Yuba had existed for less than a year.

The documents also record a spelling change. In January the Utah reserve is the "Uintah"; by May the proclamations call it "the Uinta Forest Reserve ... established ... under the name of the Uintah Forest Reserve."

The legal footing split by type. New reserves rested on section 24 of the 1891 law entitled "An act to repeal timber-culture laws, and for other purposes," which let the President "set apart and reserve" timbered public land. That section gave no power to change a reserve once made, so every enlargement and boundary change invoked the Sundry Civil Act of June 4, 1897, which authorized the President "at any time to modify any Executive order that has been or may hereafter be made establishing any forest reserve." The Tahoe–Yuba merger cited both laws.

The fine print shifted during the year, too. Through July, the proclamations protected settlers whose entries or filings were already on the books, so long as they kept complying with the law. From the Grand Canyon proclamation in August, the texts instead exempted any land already withdrawn for a non-forest purpose or covered by "any prior valid claim, so long as the withdrawal, reservation, or claim exists." And from the second Hell Gate enlargement on September 14, the *Statutes* print the seal clause as "caused the seal of the United States to be affixt," ten days after the Government Printing Office began applying the reformed spellings of [The Simplified-Spelling Order, 1906](story:15) to executive documents.

Two of the year's reserves link to other stories. Proclamation 628 enlarged the Wichita reserve in Oklahoma Territory, which had been made a game preserve the year before ([The Wichita Forest and Game Preserve](story:18)). Proclamation 638 set apart the Pinnacles reserve in California, later the core of [Pinnacles National Monument, California](story:26).

The year ended with a rush. From November 5 through 7 Roosevelt signed twenty-eight forest proclamations: sixteen on the 5th, nine on the 6th, three on the 7th. Twenty-one created new reserves; seven enlarged or redrew old ones. Election day fell on November 6. A Washington dispatch printed in the *Morning Oregonian* on November 9 linked the timing to something else: it reported that Roosevelt had signed the proclamation creating four new Idaho reserves and enlarging two others before leaving for Panama. He sailed for the Isthmus that week.

## Historical context
By 1906 the forest reserves were no longer a General Land Office matter. The Transfer Act of February 1, 1905, had moved them to the Department of Agriculture under Gifford Pinchot, whose bureau became the Forest Service on July 1, 1905. Every 1906 map printed with these proclamations carries the new name. The reserves were still "forest reserves"; the name "national forests" was still months away.

The thinking behind them was practical. Reserves protected the watersheds that farms and towns drank from, held a working timber supply against a feared "timber famine," and kept public timber, range and minerals out of the hands of monopolists and speculators. In his Sixth Annual Message (December 3, 1906), urging that public coal lands be withdrawn as well, Roosevelt drew the comparison himself: "The withdrawal of these coal lands would constitute a policy analogous to that which has been followed in withdrawing the forest lands from ordinary settlement. The coal, like the forests, should be treated as the property of the public and its disposal should be under conditions which would inure to the benefit of the public as a whole."

Some reserves carried the weight of the land-fraud scandals. The Blue Mountains reserve had been temporarily withdrawn on July 28, 1902, but was not proclaimed until March 1906, after the "lieu-land" provision of the 1897 act had been repealed. Its creation had been the target of a conspiracy for which Franklin Pierce Mays, Willard N. Jones and George Sorenson stood trial in federal court in Portland that August. The government charged that they had bought up about 200,000 acres of state school land inside the proposed boundaries, planning to trade it for federal lieu scrip, which the *Sunday Oregonian* of August 26 valued at about $6 an acre. The reserve Roosevelt finally proclaimed was large; the same page put it at 3,600,000 acres.

Some reserves had plain local roots. A Washington dispatch in the *Deseret Evening News* (May 1, 1906) reported that the original Ashland reserve in Oregon had been made at the request of the city's council and board of trade, and that the enlargement aimed to protect more fully the watershed that supplied the town. The same dispatch said the new Vernon reserve in Utah's Tooele County guarded streams that settlers in the Rush valleys depended on for irrigation, where an examination had blamed overgrazing for their decline. The *Salt Lake Tribune* (February 10) reported that the January Utah proclamations "were issued on the recommendation of the Department of Agriculture."

## Political impact
These were not quiet filings everywhere. When the November Idaho reserves came down, the *Morning Oregonian* gave them a stacked headline on page 2, putting the four new reserves at 7,406,556 acres and calling them a great slice out of Heyburn's home counties. Its Washington bureau reported that Roosevelt had created every reserve the Forest Service recommended, including the Shoshone reserve in northern Idaho that Senator Heyburn had fought so hard, and read the result as the administration brushing his protest aside. Senator Weldon B. Heyburn of Idaho had been fighting withdrawals in his state since 1905 (see [The Forest Reserves of 1905](story:55)). The same dispatch carried the Forest Service's answer to the charge that land was being locked up: the reserves' resources would remain open to use and development, with cutting limited to mature timber.

Stockmen pushed back against the new management as much as against the boundaries. In June, according to the *Lexington Wheatfield* of Lexington, Oregon (June 21), Pinchot overruled the Umatilla County Woolgrowers' association, which had protested paying a grazing fee on the Blue Mountains reserve range that season. The growers pointed to California and Washington court decisions holding that the Secretary of Agriculture had no authority to tax the use of forest-reserve range; the Forest Service replied that such decisions would not change its rules for the reserves. Meanwhile the Mays land-fraud trial kept the Blue Mountains reserve in the news, drawing attention, the *Sunday Oregonian* said, from all over the country.

Elsewhere the reception was calmer. California papers ran the 1906 actions as local news. The *San Francisco Call* (July 19) reported the Pinnacles reserve "created by proclamation of the President today," adding that "The Forestry Bureau officials hope to have this reserve made a national park in the near future." (The *Call* misplaced it in Santa Cruz County; the *Santa Cruz Weekly Sentinel* headed its item "NEW FOREST RESERVE FOR SAN BENITO CO.") The *Sacramento Daily Union* noted that Governor Pardee had received a copy of the Tahoe consolidation, and the *Amador Ledger-Dispatch* listed, township by township, the county land added to the Stanislaus. The one sign of resistance in California concerned a reserve that was never made: the *Truckee Republican* (June 30) credited Congressman J. N. Gillett with getting land in El Dorado, Placer and Nevada counties left out of "a proposed forest reserve" after "Many protests." Utah papers, too, reported the proclamations as news from Washington or the land office ("Fish Lake Forest Reserve Greatly Enlarged," ran a *Deseret Evening News* headline on January 24). The protests they printed concerned other things, such as a quarrel between Sanpete and Nephi sheepmen over grazing allotments inside the Manti reserve.

Taken together, though, the year's proclamations fed a grievance that Western stockmen, timbermen and their senators voiced more and more loudly: that the federal government was "locking up" land, at the expense of states' rights and local economies. Their case was more than obstruction. They argued that a large share of a state's land was being put beyond the reach of its settlers and its legislature by executive act, and that grazing fees were a tax Congress had never voted. Supporters saw the same reserves as insurance against waste, fraud and monopoly, and the Forest Service presented them as open for use.

## Legacy
The grievance came to a head in February 1907, when Congress attached a rider to the agricultural appropriation bill: "hereafter no forest reserve shall be created, nor shall any additions be made to one heretofore created within the limits of the States of Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado, or Wyoming, except by Act of Congress." The same act renamed the reserves: "forest reserves, which shall be known hereafter as national forests." What Roosevelt did before signing it is the story of [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9).

The 1906 reserves became the backbone of many national forests that exist today, among them the Malheur, Umatilla and Wallowa-Whitman in Oregon (from the Blue Mountains); the Helena–Lewis and Clark, Lolo, Kootenai and Custer Gallatin in Montana; the Idaho Panhandle and Salmon-Challis country in Idaho; the Fremont-Winema and Rogue River–Siskiyou in Oregon; the Humboldt-Toiyabe in Nevada; the Nebraska National Forest; the Manti-La Sal, Fishlake and Uinta-Wasatch-Cache in Utah; and the Coronado, Cibola, Carson and Santa Fe in Arizona and New Mexico. The Monterey, San Luis Obispo and Pinnacles reserves passed into what became the Los Padres. A wave of mergers in July 1908 folded many of the smaller 1906 reserves into larger ones: Ekalaka and Long Pine into the Sioux, North Platte into the Nebraska, Ruby Mountains and Independence into the Humboldt, Goose Lake into the Fremont, Peloncillo into the Chiricahua. Boundaries have shifted and forests have merged many times since, so no single 1906 proclamation maps neatly onto one modern forest.

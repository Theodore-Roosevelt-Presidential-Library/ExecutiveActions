## At a glance
On June 17, 1902, Roosevelt signed the Reclamation Act, which put the federal government into the business of watering the dry West. Money from public-land sales in sixteen western states and territories went into a dedicated "reclamation fund." That money paid for dams and canals, and settlers on the newly irrigated land repaid the cost so the fund could build the next project. Within a year, five projects were under way, including the great dam on Arizona's Salt River that would carry Roosevelt's name.

## Background
For a century the public domain had been handed out on a simple theory: give a family 160 acres and a plow, and they will make a farm. West of about the hundredth meridian, that theory stopped working. Rain thinned out, and the best land could not be farmed at all unless someone brought water to it. Storing a river's flood water and spreading it across a valley took reservoirs and main canals on a scale no homesteader could build.

The Reclamation Act of June 17, 1902 (ch. 1093, 32 Stat. 388) answered with a fund designed to keep itself going. Its ten sections run across three pages of the *Statutes at Large*. The key provisions:

- **The fund (section 1).** Receipts from "the sale and disposal of public lands in Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, New Mexico, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Dakota, Utah, Washington, and Wyoming" were set aside "as a special fund in the Treasury to be known as the 'reclamation fund.'" The fund also took in surplus land-office fees. It left out the five percent of land-sale money already promised to those states for schools and other purposes.
- **Entry limits and withdrawals (section 3).** Land to be watered by a project could be taken up only under the homestead laws, "in tracts of not less than forty nor more than one hundred and sixty acres." The Secretary of the Interior was directed to withdraw from entry the land needed for the works. He could also withdraw land "believed to be susceptible of irrigation from said works," so speculators could not grab it the moment a reservoir site was picked.
- **Repayment (section 4).** Settlers would pay construction charges in no more than ten annual installments, set "with a view of returning to the reclamation fund the estimated cost of construction of the project."
- **The 160-acre water right (section 5).** No water right for private land could be sold "for a tract exceeding one hundred and sixty acres to any one landowner," and only to an "actual bona fide resident on such land, or occupant thereof residing in the neighborhood."

The 160-acre limit therefore appears twice in two forms: as a cap on how much public land a settler could enter, and as a cap on how much private land could receive project water. Together with the residence rule, the idea was plain. The water was meant for small family farms, not land companies.

The land withdrawals under section 3 were orders signed by the Secretary of the Interior, not presidential executive orders. Surviving examples include secretarial orders of 1906, 1907 and 1909 setting aside Montana land for the Lower Yellowstone and Huntley projects. No total for reclamation withdrawals across Roosevelt's presidency has been found. One dated figure does survive. Attorney General George W. Wickersham's 1910 report on the Ballinger charges stated that between December 4, 1908, and February 17, 1909, land in Wyoming, Utah and Montana was withdrawn on the Reclamation Service's recommendation, and that the land withdrawn came to approximately 8,252,060 acres. The same report notes those withdrawals were later said to have been made to keep power sites out of private hands. That puts the figure in the final-months fight over water-power sites, not in the program's ordinary work.

Texas had no federal public land to sell, so it was not among the original sixteen. An act of June 12, 1906 extended the law to Texas.

Engineers would run the program. On July 8, 1902, Secretary of the Interior Ethan Allen Hitchcock created the United States Reclamation Service. It was not a separate bureau but a part of the U.S. Geological Survey, inside the Survey's Division of Hydrography. Survey director Charles D. Walcott was its first head. Frederick H. Newell, who had joined the Survey as an assistant hydraulic engineer in 1889 and ran the Division of Hydrography, became Chief Engineer. On March 14, 1903, Hitchcock accepted Walcott's recommendation and authorized the first five projects.

## Historical context
Roosevelt did not invent federal reclamation. He broke a logjam that had held it back for twenty years. Since John Wesley Powell's surveys of the 1870s, westerners had argued that only the national government had the reach and the money to store the West's flood water. Private and state irrigation companies had tried and mostly failed. They were short of capital, tangled in water-rights fights, and unable to finance works on the scale the arid country demanded.

A National Irrigation Congress had met from time to time since its first session at Salt Lake City in September 1891. A determined group had a bill ready: Nevada congressman Francis G. Newlands; George H. Maxwell, executive chairman of the National Irrigation Association; and the Geological Survey's Frederick H. Newell. In his *Autobiography* (1913), Roosevelt wrote that Newlands "was ably assisted by Mr. G. H. Maxwell, a Californian, who had taken a deep interest in irrigation matters." Maxwell attended both White House strategy meetings on the bill, on April 9 and May 7, 1902, and the final law's stand against land monopoly owes something to him.

What the reclamation advocates had lacked was a president willing to spend political capital. In his first annual message to Congress, on December 3, 1901, Roosevelt put reclamation front and center. His case was about homes and national strength, not scenery. "Great storage works are necessary to equalize the flow of streams and to save the flood waters," he told Congress. "Their construction has been conclusively shown to be an undertaking too vast for private effort." He also made a case from fairness: "It is as right for the National Government to make the streams and rivers of the arid region useful by engineering works for water storage as to make useful the rivers and harbors of the humid region by engineering works of another kind."

The country was also thinking about its own limits. The 1890 census had reported that "the unsettled area has been so broken into by isolated bodies of settlement that there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." (That was Superintendent Robert P. Porter's wording. The idea of a "closed frontier" came later, from Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 reading of it.) Reclamation offered a way to keep the homestead ideal alive: if the continent had run short of open farmland, the government could make more by bringing water to the desert.

## Political impact
The fight in Congress did not pit West against East so much as it ran along the line between them, and it split both parties. Western members of both parties badly wanted federal water. Opposition came from easterners who saw no reason to tax the whole country to build up a rival farm region, and from strict constructionists and states'-rights men who thought federal dam-building was outside the government's proper business and a threat to state control of water. House Appropriations chairman "Uncle Joe" Cannon and his circle were prominent among them. Cannon was not yet Speaker (that was David B. Henderson of Iowa until Cannon took the chair in November 1903), but his hold on Appropriations gave him real power over the bill.

There was regional jealousy, too: why should the arid states get a permanent, self-renewing fund of their own when older states got nothing like it? Roosevelt had little patience for that argument. In his *Autobiography* (1913) he recalled: "While I had lived in the West I had come to realize the vital need of irrigation to the country, and I had been both amused and irritated by the attitude of Eastern men who obtained from Congress grants of National money to develop harbors and yet fought the use of the Nation's power to develop the irrigation work of the West."

The dedicated fund was itself a political strategy. Because land-sale money went straight into the reclamation fund instead of through a yearly appropriation for each project, the bill's authors hoped to keep the program out of a hostile Congress's reach.

The bill's path, according to the *Congressional Record*:

- **January 21, 1902:** Companion bills were introduced: H.R. 9676 from Representative Francis G. Newlands of Nevada (then a House member; he entered the Senate in 1903) and S. 3057 from Senator Henry C. Hansbrough of North Dakota. The Senate bill became the law.
- **March 1, 1902:** After debate, the Senate passed S. 3057 by voice vote.
- **April 9 and May 7, 1902:** Supporters, George H. Maxwell among them, met with Roosevelt at the White House to plan strategy.
- **June 12–13, 1902:** The House adopted a special order to take up the bill. On June 13 Roosevelt wrote to Cannon asking him to stand aside. Cannon went along and left the House early that day, and that afternoon the House passed the bill, 146 to 55.
- **June 16–17, 1902:** The Senate agreed to the House amendments on June 16, and Roosevelt signed the bill on June 17.

Within weeks the Interior Department put the law to work, creating the Reclamation Service on July 8, 1902 and authorizing five projects at once on March 14, 1903. The section 3 withdrawal power came into use as well, and in the last months of the administration, reclamation withdrawals in Wyoming, Utah and Montana became tangled in the fight over water-power sites described above.

## Legacy
The five projects of March 1903 were Sweetwater (later North Platte) in Wyoming and Nebraska, Milk River in Montana, Truckee (later Newlands) in Nevada, Gunnison (later Uncompahgre) in Colorado, and Salt River in Arizona Territory. None can claim to be "the first" by authorization, since all five were approved on the same day. Truckee was the first to start construction: its Truckee River Diversion Dam, better known as Derby Dam, was the subject of Reclamation's first construction specification and was finished in 1905. In March 1919, after Newlands's death, Secretary of the Interior Lane announced that the Truckee-Carson project would be known from then on as the Newlands Project, in memory of the man the *San Francisco Call*'s Washington dispatch called the father of the reclamation law. Interior's public notices carried the new name by April 1919.

### Theodore Roosevelt Dam
The most famous of the five was the great masonry dam on Arizona's Salt River, and it carried Roosevelt's name years before it was finished. In mid-1903 the trade press still called it the "Tonto dam." By late 1904 the Reclamation Service's *Third Annual Report* headed its Salt River section "ROOSEVELT DAM" and mentioned a camp and town at Roosevelt and a Phoenix-Roosevelt road. In January 1905 the *San Francisco Call* wrote of bids for fuel oil "for the Roosevelt dam," and in March reported that the dam "will be known as the Roosevelt dam." Who chose the name, and exactly when, is not known. The first stone was laid on September 20, 1906. Roosevelt, by then a private citizen, dedicated Theodore Roosevelt Dam on March 18, 1911. "You could not have done anything which would have pleased and touched me more than to name this great dam, this reservoir site, after me," he told the crowd. The reservoir behind it still bears his name. See also [Tonto National Monument, Arizona](story:25).

### The Bureau of Reclamation
The Reclamation Service became independent of the Geological Survey on March 9, 1907, with Frederick H. Newell as Director, and became the Bureau of Reclamation on June 20, 1923. Over the following decades it built Hoover, Grand Coulee and the network of dams that made large-scale settlement, irrigated farming and hydroelectric cities possible across the West. Texas, left out of the original law because it had no federal public land, had been brought in by an act of June 12, 1906. Roosevelt counted reclamation among the achievements he was proudest of.

### A mixed record
The program watered millions of acres and seeded lasting farm communities. But the self-funding cycle never worked as cleanly as planned. Settlers struggled to repay construction costs on schedule, and Congress repeatedly stretched and softened the terms. The 160-acre limit meant to keep the water with small family farmers was widely evaded, and much of the benefit flowed to larger landholders and agribusiness.

The dams also remade whole rivers. Derby Dam, among Reclamation's oldest facilities, sent so much of the Truckee River into the Truckee Canal that it set off the better part of a century of litigation over water owed downstream to Pyramid Lake and the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe. The United States filed the Orr Ditch suit in 1913, and the decree came in 1944. The Tribe won a round in *Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe of Indians v. Morton* (1973), then lost the larger question in *Nevada v. United States* (1983), where the Supreme Court held the 1944 decree binding against further reserved-rights claims. By the mid-1960s, Reclamation's own fact sheet records, the Lahontan cutthroat trout had been eliminated from Pyramid Lake. Today the program's achievements are weighed against fisheries, wetlands and tribal water rights that the 1902 law never considered.

Read from inside 1902, the Act was a bold claim that a modern national government could and should build what a frontier economy could not. Read forward, it was also the start of a century-long argument over who the water was really for.

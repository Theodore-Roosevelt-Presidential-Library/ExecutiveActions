## At a glance
In the last full autumn of his presidency, Roosevelt signed fifty-three executive orders, most of them routine: appointments, land-office mergers, lighthouse and military reservations, small civil-service changes. A few reached further. One pulled the Marines off Navy ships, and Congress soon pushed them back aboard. One redrew part of the Navajo Reservation. And one, signed December 28, 1908, returned to China more than ten million dollars of the indemnity it owed the United States after the Boxer uprising, money that went on to send generations of Chinese students to American universities.

## Background
After the Boxer uprising of 1900, the foreign powers whose citizens and legations had been attacked imposed a collective indemnity on China under the Boxer Protocol of September 7, 1901. The American share came to about $24.4 million, secured by a Chinese bond dated December 15, 1906. As the American claims were added up (property losses, military costs, payments to citizens), it became plain that the sum far exceeded what the United States had actually spent.

Congress, by joint resolution of May 25, 1908, authorized the President to scale the debt down, holding back $2,000,000 against private claims still pending in the Court of Claims. Executive Order 999 put that authority to work. It capped China's total obligation at $13,655,492.69 plus 4 percent interest, and remitted the rest of the bond, $10,785,286.12, "as an act of friendship," to be returned in installments beginning January 1, 1909. The order reads, in part:

> I hereby consent to a modification of the bond for $24,440,778.81, dated December 15, 1906, received from China pursuant to the protocol of September 7, 1901, for indemnity against losses and expenses incurred by reason of the so-called Boxer disturbances in China during the year 1900, so that the total payment to be made by China under the said bond shall be limited to the sum of $13,655,492.69 and interest at the stipulated rate of 4 per cent per annum, and that the remainder of the indemnity to which the United States is entitled under the said protocol and bond be remitted as an act of friendship …

Three long schedules of yearly figures running to 1940 were annexed, signed by Secretary of State Elihu Root. One small oddity is in the document itself: the order credits a January 1909 installment of $85,223.04, while its own Schedule A gives that installment as $85,223.64, the arithmetically correct figure.

The rest of the season's orders, briefly:
- **The Marine Corps.** Executive Order 969 (November 12) defined the Corps' duties in five paragraphs, none of them aboard ship, and so took Marines off Navy vessels.
- **The Navajo lands.** Executive Order 1000 (December 30) redrew part of the Navajo Reservation in northwestern New Mexico, the "checkerboard" country. It restored to the public domain the unallotted lands that Executive Orders 709 (November 9, 1907) and 744 (January 28, 1908) had added, while keeping out the tracts that held 110 unapproved Navajo allotments.
- **The Canal Zone courts.** Executive Orders 996 and 997 (December 23) named H. A. Gudger Chief Justice and Wesley M. Owen an Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the Canal Zone, each effective January 4, 1909.
- **Civil service.** Executive Orders 982 and 983 (November 30) brought fourth-class postmasters in fourteen northeastern and midwestern states into the classified service. Executive Order 977 (November 24) barred most notaries in federal service from charging fees for notarial acts, on pain of "immediate dismissal from the service," exempting fourth-class postmasters and rural carriers. Executive Order 984 (December 1), a single sentence, opened civil-service examinations to deaf-mutes.
- **Birds.** Two more bird reservations: Island Bay, Florida (Executive Order 958, October 23), and Loch Katrine (Executive Order 961, October 26). Loch Katrine is in the Oregon Basin along the Shoshone River canal, which places it in **Wyoming**, not Oregon.
- **Land offices.** Dardanelle, Arkansas, was folded into Little Rock (Executive Order 974, November 21) and Colby, Kansas, into Topeka (Executive Order 988, December 7). The Colby merger shows how mechanical this work was: the law required consolidation once a district's unclaimed land fell below 100,000 acres, and Colby was down to about 42,000. Dardanelle closed after January 30, 1909; Colby on February 1.

The numbering here is not strictly in date order. Executive Orders 946 and 947 are dated October 3, a day after 948, and 990 (December 10) follows 991 (December 9). Both quirks go back to Clifford L. Lord's 1944 numbered index.

## Historical context
American standing in China was low in 1908. Exclusion policy, and the 1905 push to renegotiate the exclusion treaty, had set off a broad boycott of American goods that ran through 1905 and into 1906, spreading from Shanghai to Guangzhou, Fuzhou, Xiamen, Tianjin, Beijing, and Chinese communities overseas. Chinese officials and reformers openly resented the indemnity. The boycott cut both ways: it hardened Chinese feeling, but it also gave Congress a reason to stall. Roosevelt wrote to W. W. Rockhill on August 22, 1905, that "[t]he chance of my getting favorable action by Congress will be greatly interfered with by the failure of the Chinese to do justice themselves on such important matters as the boycott and the Hankow [railway] concession."

Rockhill, the American minister to China, was the steady hand behind the remission; the missionary Arthur H. Smith was its most effective public advocate. Educators pressed a parallel case. In early 1906 Edmund J. James, president of the University of Illinois, sent Roosevelt a memorandum arguing that "[t]he nation which succeeds in educating the young Chinese of the present generation will be the nation which for a given expenditure of effort will reap the largest possible returns in moral, intellectual, and commercial influence." James never mentioned the indemnity, and the argument was not sentimental. His memorandum closes: "Trade follows moral and spiritual domination far more inevitably than it follows the flag."

Roosevelt made the case himself in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907: "I ask for authority to re-form the agreement with China under which the indemnity of 1900 was fixed, by remitting and cancelling the obligation of China for the payment of all that part of the stipulated indemnity which is in excess of the sum of eleven million, six hundred and fifty-five thousand, four hundred and ninety-two dollars and sixty-nine cents, and interest at four per cent." Under a separate heading in the same message he added: "This Nation should help in every practicable way in the education of the Chinese people, so that the vast and populous Empire of China may gradually adapt itself to modern conditions. One way of doing this is by promoting the coming of Chinese students to this country and making it attractive to them to take courses at our universities and higher educational institutions."

Congress made the gesture smaller than he asked. Roosevelt proposed a cap of $11,655,492.69; the joint resolution set it at $13,655,492.69, exactly two million dollars higher, matching the sum held back for private claims. He asked to remit $12,785,286.12 and was allowed $10,785,286.12. At China's request the returned money went to education, run from the Chinese side by an office under the Foreign and Education ministries that would send 100 students a year for the first four years and at least 50 a year after that. Contemporaries called it the educational mission; "Boxer Indemnity Scholarship" is a later label.

The Navajo order belonged to a different machine: allotment, the era's policy of surveying tribal land, assigning family parcels, and returning the "surplus" to the public domain. Section 25 of the Act of May 29, 1908, directed that surplus lands be restored once the Indians in an area had been allotted. Executive Order 1000 did that for the eastern third of the 1907–1908 addition, east of the New Mexico First Guide Meridian.

## Political impact
The remission drew no serious opposition at home. Congress had already authorized it, and had trimmed it. It was welcomed by missionary and educational groups and by a Chinese government eager for a gesture of respect. On December 31, 1908, Secretary of State Elihu Root sent the order and its three schedules to the Chinese minister in Washington and telegraphed Rockhill, the American minister in China, and the State Department printed the whole package in its published *Foreign Relations* volume for 1908, accounting and all.

The order that started a fight was Executive Order 969. Taking Marines off Navy ships ran against a tradition older than the Republic and against the Corps' sense of itself. A subcommittee of the House Committee on Naval Affairs held hearings on the status of the Marine Corps from January 7 to 15, 1909, and printed the record in a 365-page volume. Within four months of the order, Congress reversed it, not by commanding Marines aboard but by attaching a condition to their pay. The naval appropriations act of March 3, 1909, provided:

> *Provided*, That no part of the appropriations herein made for the Marine Corps shall be expended for the purposes for which said appropriations are made unless officers and enlisted men shall serve as heretofore on board all battle ships and armored cruisers, and also upon such other vessels of the navy as the President may direct, in detachments of not less than eight per centum of the strength of the enlisted men of the navy on said vessels.

The other orders drew no recorded national notice. The Navajo boundary change was worked out between the Indian Office and the General Land Office. The land-office mergers simply closed two offices: Dardanelle after January 30, 1909, and Colby on February 1. The personnel exceptions were the quiet routine of federal housekeeping.

## Legacy

### The Boxer indemnity and Tsinghua
The returned indemnity paid for the education of generations of Chinese students in the United States. The first sailed before there was a school to prepare them: 47 students in October 1909 and 70 more in August 1910, among the latter Hu Shih and Zhao Yuanren. In the spring of 1911 a preparatory school opened in Beijing as Tsing Hua Imperial College, an eight-year school in two departments for as many as 500 students bound for American universities. It became Tsing Hua College in 1912, added a university section in 1925, and took the name National Tsing Hua University in 1928. Tsinghua is now one of China's foremost universities.

The sum Executive Order 999 remitted was $10,785,286.12. A further $1,175,835.64, left over from the $2,000,000 claims reserve, went back to China in installments in 1915–1918 under the same 1908 resolution, for a combined $11,961,121.76. A second remission resolution, of May 21, 1924, cites that larger figure as though Roosevelt had remitted all of it on December 28, 1908. The gesture is still cited in both countries as an early, lasting act of American cultural diplomacy, though historians also note that it advanced American influence as much as Chinese welfare.

### The Navajo lands
Executive Order 1000 was the first step in ending the 1907–1908 Navajo addition in New Mexico, not a one-off adjustment. Executive Order 1284 (January 16, 1911) restored all the remaining unallotted land, and the reservation went on being adjusted by later executive orders in 1911–1912. In 1990 a federal appeals court held that the two restoring orders together diminished or ended the New Mexico part of the addition (*Pittsburg & Midway Coal Mining Co. v. Yazzie*). The same court found that the area had "remained predominantly Navajo in character throughout the entire century," with about 55 percent of the land in Navajo fee ownership or held in trust for the Tribe or individual Navajos, and another 21 percent Bureau of Land Management land leased to the Tribe. How much of the land this order restored passed to non-Navajo settlers is not known. The checkerboard of allotments and restorations from this era is still part of the land map of the eastern Navajo country.

### The housekeeping orders
Two of the routine orders lasted. Executive Order 983, letting the Post Office fill fourth-class postmasterships by interim appointment until the Civil Service Commission could examine, stood for thirty-nine years before it was revoked in 1947. Executive Order 984's single sentence admitting deaf-mutes to examination was amended, not repealed, four months later. The rest carry no independent legacy.

## At a glance
These orders close Roosevelt's 1905. Most are routine, but three strands stand out. On October 10 he created three island bird reservations at once, in Florida and on Lake Superior. In November he pushed merit examinations across nearly the whole consular service and, for the first time, to diplomatic secretaries. And in October and November he rewrote the rules for removing federal employees, a move that drew the sharpest reaction of the season.

## Background
**Three bird reservations in a day.** On October 10, 1905, Roosevelt signed three short, nearly identical orders. The one for Passage Key, Florida, reads in full:

> It is hereby ordered that Passage Key, an island near the mouth of Tampa Bay, Florida, as shown on the General Land Office map of the State of Florida of date 1893, and situated in section 6, township 34 south, range 16 east, as the same appears upon the official plat of survey of said township approved March 17, 1877, be, and it is hereby reserved and set apart for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds. This reservation to be known as the Passage Key Reservation.

The other two did the same for "the unsurveyed islands of the Huron islands group, lying near the south shore of Lake Superior" (357-D) and for "the unsurveyed islands of the Siskiwit or Menagerie group of islands, lying near the mouth of Siskiwit bay, on the south side of Isle Royal in Lake Superior, Michigan" (357-C). This was the tool Roosevelt had used at [Pelican Island](story:1), [Breton Island](story:22), and Stump Lake, North Dakota ([The Executive Orders of Early 1905](story:85)): a bare executive order setting island land apart, under the Department of Agriculture, for nesting birds. In the Biological Survey's numbered list they are the fourth, fifth and sixth national bird reservations: Huron Islands, Siskiwit, Passage Key. Only Passage Key had been surveyed, at 36.37 acres.

**The consular and diplomatic orders.** On November 10, EO 367 amended Grover Cleveland's order of September 20, 1895, which had required examination or prior service for consulates paying $1,000 to $2,500. Roosevelt's order reached "all consulates general, consulates, commercial agencies and consular agencies, the annual compensation of which is not less than $1,000." With the old $2,500 ceiling gone, the better-paid posts were now covered too. Vacancies were to be filled by transfer or promotion within the State Department, by reappointing someone who had served it well, or by a candidate "selected by the President for examination" and found qualified by "a Board of three persons designated by the Secretary of State." EO 368 applied a parallel rule to secretaries of embassy and legation: transfer or promotion "from some branch of the foreign service," or examination.

**Removal and discipline.** A third group tightened the rules for disciplining and dismissing employees:

- **EO 351** (September 8) set a procedure for removing officers and employees on the Isthmus of Panama.
- **EO 359** (October 13) forbade any officer or employee to "instruct or be concerned in any manner in the instruction of any person … with a view to their special preparation for the examination of the United States Civil Service Commission," on pain of removal.
- **EO 362** (October 17) provided that when the President or a department head was satisfied an employee was "inefficient or incapable, and that the public service will be materially improved by his removal, such removal will be made without hearing, but the cause of removal shall be stated in writing and filed."
- **EO 371** (November 17) rewrote Civil Service Rule XII on removals in the same spirit. Department heads could, at their discretion, give an employee a written statement of reasons and time to answer. The Civil Service Commission could step in only where the required procedure had not been followed or "the removal was made for political or religious reasons."

**And the rest.** Around these ran the usual individual appointments, many of them women clerks, such as Marjorie H. Young, Marie L. Eastwood, Edna M. Peltz and Emily L. Genella. There were merit-system amendments; land-district mergers (Kingfisher into Guthrie, Oklahoma, 357-A; Grand Forks into Devils Lake, North Dakota, 365-A; Bent into the Valley district at Pueblo, Colorado, 372-A; Sidney into North Platte, Nebraska, 376-A); and lighthouse and military reservations from Gravina and False Islands, Alaska (351-A) to Point Sal, California (366-A). EO 352-A set regulations for presenting the Congressional Medal of Honor, and EO 377½ appointed a committee to plan an inquiry into sanitary conditions in government offices, to prevent tuberculosis. EO 349, appointing Thomas Harper a clerk in the Quartermaster's Department, is numbered out of order and dated July 31.

## Historical context
The bird orders belong to the most distinctive conservation idea of the Roosevelt years. Plume hunters and egg collectors had devastated seabird and wading-bird colonies through the late nineteenth century, because the millinery trade paid extravagant prices for feathers. The Biological Survey recorded what these islands sheltered: at Passage Key "herons of several species," and "laughing gulls and terns of various species also nest," while "the two Michigan reservations contain the largest known breeding colonies of the herring gull on inland waters." With no general wildlife-refuge law, Roosevelt used his authority over the public domain to set the islands aside one order at a time.

The National Association of Audubon Societies counted these reservations as its own work. Its 1905 annual report spoke of "four new reservations made by Executive Order of President Theodore Roosevelt, to whom this Association is deeply indebted for his never-failing interest in and ready help to our work." It had already hired two wardens in Michigan that season to guard herring gull colonies, "one on what is now the Huron Island Reservation." A warden was on the Huron Islands before the order was signed.

The consular orders answered a different pressure. Business groups and reformers had campaigned for years to pull the consular service out of the spoils system. Cleveland had opened the door in 1895 for the middle range of posts. Roosevelt and Secretary of State Elihu Root widened the rule in November 1905, a few weeks before the new Congress met and took up a consular reorganization bill.

The removal orders show the other face of civil-service reform: Roosevelt's insistence that protecting employees from patronage firings should not mean protecting the incompetent. That ran straight into the fear that removal "without hearing" could become cover for the very political firings the merit system was meant to stop. EO 371 anticipated the objection by leaving the Commission one opening to investigate: the charge that a removal was political or religious.

## Political impact
The bird reservations reached the papers through the Audubon association rather than as a Washington story. At its annual meeting in New York, president William Dutcher reported four new reservations, the most important at Tampa Bay and the others on Lake Superior (*Los Angeles Herald*, November 13, 1905). They needed no act of Congress and drew no congressional fight.

The consular orders drew a short wire item and at least one quip: "Consular appointees must now know at least two languages and have a good head for business" (*Petaluma Daily Morning Courier*, November 25, 1905). The National Civil Service Reform League's consular committee, chaired by George McAneny, called the November 10 orders "important" and the administration's direction "in the highest degree encouraging."

The removal order drew the sharpest response. The AP dispatch announcing EO 362 said a Cabinet officer now had "the power to remove summarily and without hearing any civil Service employee in his department." It traced the immediate occasion to the chauffeur of a government automobile who had driven into the path of the President's carriage and seemed to enjoy cutting him off. A week later the *Alameda Daily Encinal* ran an editorial under a mock death notice, "The Funeral of Civil Service," predicting the order would be "received with terror by Federal officeholders," while conceding that a loafer had no better claim to a job for life from Uncle Sam than from a private employer.

The organized reformers held back judgment, with misgivings. The League's journal, *Good Government*, said the order "has attracted more attention than almost any order heretofore issued by him on the subject of the civil service," that in the press "expressions of approval are far more numerous than those of disapproval," and that "among the Federal employees at Washington the order has created a good deal of apprehension." At the League's December meeting in Milwaukee, its committee on removals, chaired by Samuel H. Ordway, said it was "unconvinced as yet" that the old requirement to tell an employee the reasons and let him answer was undesirable. Still, it held that "the new rule should be given a full trial before a judgment upon its merits or demerits should be pronounced," and was "confident that the rule will not be abused under the present administration."

## Legacy
Roosevelt went on to create fifty-one federal bird reservations by the end of his term, the seed of the national wildlife refuge system. (The figure of fifty-five found in some accounts counts four national game preserves as well.) Two of the October 1905 islands remain refuges more than a century later. The Huron Islands took a roundabout route: in 1938 Franklin Roosevelt revoked the 1905 order and re-reserved the islands as the Huron Migratory Bird Refuge, renamed Huron National Wildlife Refuge in 1940. Today it is eight islands totaling 147 acres, all within the Huron Islands Wilderness, closed to the public except Lighthouse Island. Passage Key, renamed a national wildlife refuge by the same 1940 proclamation, lost its freshwater lake and most of its vegetation in the hurricane of 1921 and has since shrunk to a small sandbar, closed to all public use since the 2005 hurricanes.

Siskiwit went another way. It was still a refuge in 1940, when Isle Royale National Park was established. Then the Act of March 6, 1942 declared: "The Siskiwit Islands Bird Reservation is hereby abolished and shall hereafter be a part of the Isle Royale National Park." Of the three October 1905 reservations, it is the one that did not survive as a refuge.

The consular orders were the executive partner of the Act of April 5, 1906, which reorganized the consular service. It graded consuls-general and consuls by salary, from $12,000 at London and Paris down to $2,000; created five inspectors of consulates; sent all fees into the Treasury so that salaries were an officer's "sole and only compensation"; and barred consular officers paid over $1,000 from trading or practicing law. It contained no examination clause at all. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge's bill had included a board of examiners, but the Senate committee struck it to meet Democratic objections that it would let Republican officeholders stay on indefinitely. Selection by examination kept resting on Roosevelt's executive orders, which is why the orders of November 1905 matter. The act was one step on the long road to the professional Foreign Service created by the Rogers Act of 1924.

The removal orders fed into the decades-long working-out of federal job protections, later shaped by the Lloyd–La Follette Act of 1912 (see [The Civil-Service Orders of Early 1902 and the "Gag Order"](story:78)).

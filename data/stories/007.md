## At a glance
On March 2, 1909, two days before his presidency ended, Theodore Roosevelt carved Mount Olympus National Monument out of the Olympic National Forest in Washington. The proclamation named glaciers and, above all, the Olympic elk, a species named for Roosevelt himself and "rapidly decreasing in numbers." Six years later, a new administration gave back roughly half of it. The land became the heart of Olympic National Park.

## Background
Proclamation 869 reserved a vast block of the Olympic Mountains, in the counties of "Jefferson, Clallam, Mason and Chehalis" (Chehalis County was renamed Grays Harbor County in 1915). It gave three grounds: "numerous glaciers," other "objects of unusual scientific interest," and the region that "from time immemorial has formed the summer range and breeding grounds of the Olympic Elk (*Cervus roosevelti*), a species peculiar to these mountains and rapidly decreasing in numbers."

The proclamation never counts its acres. It fixes the boundary by township and range and by an attached Forest Service diagram, and the diagram gives no area either. The government's own figure came in 1917, when the Interior Department reported that the monument "as originally set aside by presidential proclamation of March 2, 1909, contained approximately 608,640 acres of land in the Olympic Mountains in northwestern Washington."

One paragraph of the proclamation carried more weight than any other. The monument was laid on top of the national forest, and the two reservations would "both be effective on the land withdrawn, but the National Monument hereby established shall be the dominant reservation and any use of the land which interferes with its preservation or protection as a National Monument is hereby forbidden." The same sentence, though, kept the land open for "forest purposes."

## Historical context
The elk at the center of the proclamation carried Roosevelt's name. The naturalist C. Hart Merriam described it in December 1897 as *Cervus roosevelti*, when Roosevelt was Assistant Secretary of the Navy, four years from the presidency. Merriam explained: "I deem it a privilege to name this splendid animal Roosevelt's Wapiti. It is fitting that the noblest deer of America should perpetuate the name of one who, in the midst of a busy public career, has found time to study our larger mammals in their native haunts and has written the best accounts we have ever had of their habits and chase." Merriam credited Roosevelt with noticing the difference in this elk at the Oregon exhibit of the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition.

The proclamation does not say why the herds were shrinking. People at the time pointed to hide and meat hunting, lost habitat, and a fashion as peculiar to its era as the feather trade that threatened the birds of [Pelican Island](story:1). Elk were shot for their two ivory upper canine teeth, the "ivories" or "tusks," which had become stylish watch fobs, rings, cuff links and hat pins among members of the Benevolent and Protective Order of Elks. The men who took them were called "tuskers." One club member recalled buying teeth in Idaho for 50 cents, "whereas now a pair of fine teeth cannot be had for $5." A *Seattle Mail and Herald* editorial of the period described the result: "These animals have apparently been shot down for the mere love of killing, and in a great many instances not a pound of meat has been taken; nothing in fact except the horns and teeth; the carcasses have been left to rot." Washington closed the season on Olympic elk in 1905, when the herd was estimated at about 2,000.

Congress had already been asked to protect the peninsula and had said no. In January 1904 Representative Francis W. Cushman of Tacoma introduced a bill for "The Elk National Park," protecting "the elk, game, fish, birds, animals, timber, and curiosities therein." It died against a rival bill from Representative William E. Humphrey of Seattle, a game preserve that left the timber open to logging. Humphrey tried again in 1908 and failed too.

Then the story takes a turn. On March 2, 1909, it was Humphrey, the congressman whose bill had sunk the protective one, who came to the White House with Chief Forester Gifford Pinchot and asked Roosevelt to act by proclamation. Roosevelt had not been personally involved in shaping the proposal and deferred to Humphrey. Humphrey asked Pinchot to write the "forest purposes" language into the draft so logging could continue, and the reservation was trimmed from the roughly 750,000 acres he first floated. Unlike the [Grand Canyon](story:3), this was no unilateral conservation stroke. It was a deal worked out by a pro-logging congressman and the Chief Forester, signed by a president on his way out the door.

## Political impact
The "dominant reservation" language made the monument a target, and the objections were about money. The Olympic Peninsula Development League and the Seattle Commercial Club wanted the mountains open to prospectors. The *Seattle Daily Times* ran an editorial on July 28, 1910 headlined "Conservation, and Olympic Copper." At the American Mining Congress in 1912, F. H. Stanard attacked "Pretorius Pinchot at the tennis court of Theodore the First" for making "a Siberian wilderness of the West."

Critics did not, at the time, argue that the monument broke the Antiquities Act's "smallest area compatible" limit. Mount Olympus was one of only two original proclamations that never mentioned that standard at all. The size argument came later, and when it reached the Supreme Court over the Grand Canyon in *Cameron v. United States* (1920), the Court held that size alone did not disqualify a monument.

The historian Hal Rothman later called Mount Olympus "Roosevelt's going-away present to himself," arguing that with Congress out of session and little time left, a congressionally approved designation "was out of the question."

The cuts came quickly. Taft removed 160 acres in 1912 to relieve a homesteader. Then Woodrow Wilson's Proclamation 1293 of May 11, 1915 took out 309,110 acres, a little over half the monument, leaving 299,370. The proclamation gave no reason. The record points to timber and, secondarily, copper: the Interior Department said the lands went back to the forest to "permit their development," and Chief Forester Henry S. Graves recommended removing, with one exception, everything the timber-backed Seattle Chamber of Commerce had asked for, plus six more miles of the heaviest timber. Graves wrote in a memorandum of November 12, 1914: "It is certain that many prospectors will be disappointed in the mining values [and] I am deeply concerned at the prospect[] of . . . fake mining claims on our heaviest and most valuable bodies of timber." Coolidge trimmed another 640 acres in 1929.

The lesson was blunt. A proclamation could reserve enormous country in an afternoon, and a later president could give much of it back just as fast.

## Legacy
Because the monument sat inside a national forest, the Forest Service ran it from 1909 until 1933, when Executive Order 6166 moved the national monuments to the National Park Service. Chief Forester Ferdinand Silcox tried to undo the transfer; Interior Secretary Harold Ickes publicly refused in March 1934.

The Act of June 29, 1938 abolished the monument and created Olympic National Park in its place, about 624,000 acres by the Interior Department's estimate that year. It returned every former monument acre outside the new park to the national forest, preserved "the rights reserved by treaty to the Indians of any tribes," and allowed the President to enlarge the park to no more than 898,292 acres. The largest additions came later by proclamation: Franklin Roosevelt added 187,411 acres in 1940 and 20,600 acres in the Morse Creek country in 1943, and Harry Truman added 47,753 acres in 1953, bringing in the Coastal Strip, the Queets Corridor, and part of the Bogachiel Valley. The 1909 monument held no coastline at all.

Olympic National Park became an International Biosphere Reserve in 1976 and a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981, and covers 922,650 acres today. It is home, in the National Park Service's words, to "the largest fully wild herd of Roosevelt elk in the Pacific Northwest." The elk-tooth fashion that helped push those animals toward the edge is long forgotten; the land set aside to outlast it is still there.

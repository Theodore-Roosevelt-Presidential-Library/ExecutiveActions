## At a glance
Roosevelt's first executive orders of 1906, issued between January 3 and February 28, are mostly the everyday paperwork of the presidency. Four threads stand out. Indian Key, a ninety-acre island in Tampa Bay, became a refuge for nesting birds. The 1902 "gag order" on federal employees was widened to cover independent agencies. And the government issued its first rules against tuberculosis among its own workers.

## Background
On February 10, 1906, Roosevelt set aside Indian Key, an island in Tampa Bay, "as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." Executive Order 409 is a single sentence, the same simple tool he had first used at [Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1) in 1903. It placed the island under the Department of Agriculture and named it Indian Key Reservation. It was one more link in a Florida chain that ran from Pelican Island through Passage Key in 1905 ([The Executive Orders of Late 1905](story:86)) and on to Mosquito Inlet, Pine Island and Island Bay in 1908.

**The gag order, widened.** Executive Order 163 of January 31, 1902, had forbidden federal employees, "either directly or indirectly, individually or through associations, to solicit an increase of pay or to influence or attempt to influence in their own interest any other legislation whatever, either before Congress or its committees, or in any way save through the heads of the Departments in or under which they serve, on penalty of dismissal from the Government service" (see [The Civil-Service Orders of Early 1902 and the "Gag Order"](story:78)). Executive Order 402, signed January 25, 1906, stretched that rule in a single sentence: "The Executive order of January 31, 1902, is hereby amended by adding 'or independent Government establishments' after the words 'departments' in the third and ninth lines."

**Fighting tuberculosis.** Executive Order 421 of February 28 put into effect the plan of a committee Roosevelt had appointed the previous December (Executive Order 377½). Its opening line says so: "In accordance with the report and recommendations of the committee appointed by Executive order of December 7, 1905, to prepare a plan for the prevention of tuberculosis in the Government offices and workshops, I hereby promulgate the following order…" The rules went well beyond a poster on the wall:

- Departments had to print and post the rules.
- Each department was to identify, by name, employees who had tuberculosis and hand them the rules.
- Failure to follow the rules was "a just cause for separation from the service."
- Where it was unclear whether an employee had the disease, a laboratory examination could be required.
- The Surgeons-General of the Army, the Navy, and the Public Health and Marine-Hospital Service were to inspect public buildings and workshops.

**Other notable orders in the run:**

- **EO 389½ (January 5)** created the Jamestown Ter-Centennial Commission under the Act of March 3, 1905: three cabinet officers, Treasury Secretary Leslie M. Shaw, War Secretary William H. Taft and Navy Secretary Charles J. Bonaparte. This federal commission was separate from the exposition company. It was the government's machinery behind the fair at Norfolk, Virginia, which Roosevelt had proclaimed in 1905 ([Celebration of the 300th Anniversary of the Founding of Jamestown, Virginia](story:58)). He opened the fair in person on April 26, 1907, and it closed on November 30.
- **EO 394-A** reserved lands in Minnesota for the reservoir system at the headwaters of the Mississippi River.
- **EO 395-A** reserved lands at Diamond Head, Kupikipikio Point and Punchbowl Hill in Hawaii for military purposes.
- **EO 405-B** reserved lands and waters around Yes Lake, Yes Bay and Back Bay, Alaska, for a salmon hatchery.
- **EO 407-A** set aside a tract at Randlett, Utah, for the Protestant Episcopal Church, for the use and benefit of the Ute Indians.
- **EO 399 (January 23)** gave the Board on Geographic Names added duties.
- **EO 389-A (January 3)** abolished the Stockton, California, land district and moved its business to Sacramento.

Around these sit dozens of named appointments and reinstatements made "without regard to the civil-service rules," small changes to the merit rules, land-district boundary shifts, and the usual Isthmian Canal Commission orders on appointments, pay and excepted positions (EOs 391, 393, 394, 412 and 418).

## Historical context
No general wildlife-refuge law existed in 1906. Roosevelt used his authority over the public domain to set island bird colonies apart one order at a time. The target was the plume and egg trade, which had stripped Florida's rookeries through the late 1800s, when feathers for women's hats brought extravagant prices.

The gag-order amendment shows a running tension in Roosevelt's civil-service record. He was determined to keep federal workers from lobbying Congress behind their superiors' backs. Postal and other employee groups saw the rule as a muzzle on their right to petition.

The tuberculosis order reflects the public-health worries of the day. The "white plague" was a leading cause of death, and the movement against it was at its height. The compulsory examinations and the dismissal rule are the striking part: an early case of the federal government acting as a health-conscious employer, and of how far that idea reached in 1906.

The land withdrawals show the everyday machinery of a conservation-minded administration, reserving headwaters, harbors and hatchery sites for public use before private claims could close them off.

## Political impact
Almost none of this drew national notice. The Indian Key order was signed without ceremony. Its effect was felt mainly by the local egg hunters and fishermen it shut out.

The tuberculosis order did reach the wire. An Associated Press dispatch, printed in the *San Jose Mercury-News* on March 9, 1906, under the head "Crusade Against Consumption," reported that "a crusade against the spread of tuberculosis among the employees of the Government service in Washington was today directed by President Roosevelt, who issued an order to the heads of all departments giving them explicit instructions as to their duties in combating the disease." The notice was brief and neutral, and no protest over the naming and dismissal rules has turned up.

The gag-order amendment widened a rule postal unions and other employee groups already resented. No newspaper coverage of it has been found in the California papers searched for early 1906. It belonged to a longer fight over federal workers' right to petition Congress that would not be settled until the Lloyd–La Follette Act of 1912. The rest, the appointments, land-district changes and Canal Commission business, passed without remark.

## Legacy
Indian Key had a long federal afterlife. President Harding enlarged the reservation by Executive Order 3502 of June 25, 1921, which covered "Indian Key Island located in secs. 10 and 15, and all mangrove islands located in secs. 20, 27, 28, 33 and 34." (The island names Cow, Calf and Bush come from local accounts, not from that order.) Proclamation 2416 of July 25, 1940, renamed it the Indian Key National Wildlife Refuge. On June 26, 1950, Secretary of the Interior Oscar L. Chapman signed Public Land Order 650, revoking both the 1906 and 1921 orders, covering about 191 acres.

Today Indian Key lies within Pinellas National Wildlife Refuge, east of Tierra Verde. Established in 1951, the refuge takes in Indian, Tarpon, Mule and Jackass Keys, is closed to the public year-round, and is managed within the Crystal River refuge complex. Indian Key is one of the fifty-one bird reservations Roosevelt created, the seed of the national wildlife refuge system. (The figure of fifty-five, often seen, counts his four national game preserves as well.)

The Board on Geographic Names, given new duties in January, was renamed the United States Geographic Board that August ([The Executive Orders of Summer 1906](story:89)). That board was abolished in 1934, its work was carried on by a departmental U.S. Board on Geographic Names from 1935, and Congress put the board on a statutory footing in 1947. The U.S. Board on Geographic Names still exists, staffed by the Geological Survey.

The gag-order amendment fed the long working-out of federal employees' rights to speak and petition, reshaped by the Lloyd–La Follette Act of 1912 and later law. The tuberculosis order is an early marker in the federal government's history as an occupational-health authority.

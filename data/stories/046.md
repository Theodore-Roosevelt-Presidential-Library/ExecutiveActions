## At a glance
When the government opened Indian lands to settlers in the 1890s, it held back a few tracts for public uses: an agency headquarters, a mission school, a county seat. By 1903 two of those uses were gone. Roosevelt's Proclamation 497 released the old Cheyenne River Agency grounds in South Dakota, and Proclamation 504 released a half-section in Oklahoma Territory set aside for a county seat that had moved away.

## Background
Both proclamations do the same small thing: they let go of a parcel once the reason for holding it had disappeared.

**Proclamation 497 (February 7, 1903)** concerns the old Cheyenne River Agency grounds on the west bank of the Missouri. The Sioux Act of 1889 broke up the Great Sioux Reservation and drew the new Cheyenne River Reservation's southern line along the Cheyenne River. This tract lay south of that river, in the ceded land thrown open in 1890. It ran from a point on the Missouri opposite Deep Creek, "about three miles south of the Cheyenne River," five and a half miles west, then north to the Cheyenne and back down to the Missouri. President Harrison's proclamation of February 10, 1890 had reserved it because the agency, its school and other government buildings stood there.

In 1891 the agency moved upriver to a new site north of the Cheyenne, opposite Forest City, the same year nearby Fort Bennett was abandoned. The empty ground stayed in reserve for twelve more years. Roosevelt's proclamation recited that "the government, agency and school buildings have been removed" and declared the land "subject to disposal" under the 1889 act, except "160 acres of land to which the St. Johns Mission School has obtained title under the Sioux Act."

The 1889 act let a religious society keep up to 160 acres it was using for missionary or educational work. St. John's was the Episcopal Church's boarding school for Lakota girls near old Fort Bennett, run by the Missionary District of Niobrara under Bishop William Hobart Hare. It was still enrolling about fifty girls in the 1901–02 school year. By mid-1903 the Indian Office no longer listed it, and the Rosebud agency physician that year called it "St. John's (Episcopal Mission) School of Cheyenne River Agency, which is now discontinued on account of the removal of the agency," remembering it as "an ideal school for girls." So the 160-acre exception protected a title the mission still held, most likely for a school that had already closed.

**Proclamation 504 (August 12, 1903)** concerns the south half of section 15, township 17 north, range 22 west, nominally 320 acres, in western Oklahoma Territory. When the Cheyenne and Arapaho ceded lands were opened in April 1892, this tract was reserved as the seat of county "E." The seat went to **Ioland**, north of the Canadian River, and the county took the name Day, for Captain Charles Day, one of the men who built the first courthouse there. The proclamation recites that the seat "was removed from said land to the town of Grand in said county in 1893." The courthouse at Ioland burned on the night of November 12, 1893, and the next day the three county commissioners moved the seat to Grand, sixteen miles west on the Canadian, where the water was good. No one had filed an entry on the old site in the ten years since, so Roosevelt "opened to settlement and restored to the public domain" the land.

## Historical context
These were cleanup acts left over from the great land openings of the late 1880s and early 1890s. The Sioux Act of 1889 opened the land between six new Sioux reservations to settlers. The 1891 act ratifying the Cheyenne and Arapaho agreement, and Harrison's 1892 proclamation, opened the Cheyenne-Arapaho country to the land run of April 19, 1892. Each opening held back tracts for public functions, and when those functions moved or failed, a later proclamation returned the ground to the pool open to homesteaders. Both 1903 proclamations recite facts ("it appears that…") of the kind the Indian Office and General Land Office supplied, but who asked for them is not known.

The Day County parcel is a window on the churn of early Oklahoma Territory. Settlers lived mostly along the small streams feeding the Canadian, and Ioland's water, like most nearby, was full of gypsum, "gippy" in the local word. The 1893 move was contested from the start. C. A. Squire, a homesteader near Grand from 1905 who read the county's records "from first to last," wrote that the fire brought charges of complicity against some county officers, though no one was convicted.

The question of which town was the lawful seat reached the territorial supreme court in 1907. Day County tried to escape its own bonds on the ground that the court authorizing them in 1900 had sat at Grand, not Ioland. The court accepted as "a fact beyond controversy" that "Ioland was the originally established county seat," and that Grand "was the place where all the public business of Day county was and is transacted." But it did not decide which town was the lawful seat. "If Ioland was the county seat de jure, Grand was the county seat de facto," it reasoned, and it held that any dispute over the seat "can be settled only in a direct proceeding for that purpose." Roosevelt's 1903 proclamation, in reciting that the seat "was removed" to Grand, had already treated Grand as the working seat for federal land purposes.

## Political impact
Neither proclamation opened enough land to draw a rush: one South Dakota tract of unstated size and one half-section in Oklahoma. No controversy, protest or opposition has been found in the sources consulted, and the General Land Office's annual report for fiscal 1903 mentions neither one. Whether newspapers noticed them is not yet known. The *Day County Tribune* at Grand, which had every reason to note the release of its rival's old townsite, has not yet been searched, and neither have the South Dakota papers of early 1903.

In Day County the August proclamation landed on a live quarrel. By reciting that the seat "was removed from said land to the town of Grand in said county in 1893," the federal government treated Grand as the working seat for land purposes. Which town was the lawful seat stayed unsettled: when the question reached the territorial supreme court in 1907, the court upheld what had been done at Grand but declined to decide it.

## Legacy
These proclamations carry little independent legacy beyond opening their two parcels to settlement.

### Cheyenne River
The 160-acre exception in Proclamation 497 did not last long. On March 30, 1904, Roosevelt's Proclamation 521 declared that tract, too, subject to disposal, after the Domestic and Foreign Missionary Society of the Protestant Episcopal Church said it no longer wanted it (see [The Sioux Land Openings of 1904](story:51)). The agency site north of the Cheyenne that had replaced the old grounds was itself abandoned decades later, when the Oahe Dam's reservoir flooded the Missouri bottoms, and the agency moved to Eagle Butte.

### Day County
Day County did not survive statehood. When Oklahoma entered the Union on November 16, 1907, the county was abolished; land north of the Canadian went to Ellis County and land south of it to Roger Mills County. Grand served as Ellis County's temporary seat until a nine-month county-seat fight gave the honor to Arnett. When the county property finished moving in the spring of 1909, Grand "ceased to exist" as a town of any importance, C. A. Squire wrote. By 1950 he found "nothing left to show where the town was."

### The larger pattern
Both acts belong to the vast, granular record of the allotment-and-opening era, when reservation land across the Plains was steadily converted into homesteads and townsites, a policy with far larger consequences for the Sioux, Cheyenne and Arapaho nations than these two small parcels suggest.

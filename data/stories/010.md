## At a glance
On March 1, 1907, Roosevelt set aside the timbered public lands of the Bear Lodge Mountains, in Crook County in Wyoming's northeastern corner, as a forest reserve "for the use and benefit of the people." It was a new reserve of 136,784 acres, created in the very state and the very hours a new law was about to put off-limits. Locally it was big news, and for two weeks Crook County believed it had been cancelled.

## Background
The Bear Lodge proclamation, Proclamation 716, printed at 34 Stat. 3287, was one of thirty-three forest proclamations Roosevelt signed on March 1 and 2, 1907 (see [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)). The document itself is headed simply "A Proclamation." The *Statutes at Large* lists it as "Establishing the Bear Lodge Forest Reserve, Wyoming."

This was a brand-new reserve, not an enlargement. The proclamation calls on section 24 of the Forest Reserve Act of 1891, the law that let a president reserve forested public land. It reserves "all the tracts of land, in the State of Wyoming, shown as the Bear Lodge Forest Reserve on the diagram forming a part hereof." It also protects existing legal claims and settlements, keeps coal-land withdrawals in force, and closes with a warning "to all persons not to make settlement upon the lands reserved by this proclamation." Secretary of State Elihu Root countersigned it.

The map bound into the *Statutes* shows the reserve spanning Townships 49 to 56 North, Ranges 60 to 64 West, with the Black Hills Forest Reserve next door to the east. It is a Forest Service township diagram, and it gives no acreage. The 136,784-acre figure, which includes private and state land inside the boundary, comes from Forest Service records compiled later.

## Historical context
The name has a history of its own. "Bear Lodge" translates the Lakota name for the great rock a few miles to the west, *Matȟó Thípila*, also recorded as *Mato Tipi* and *Mato Tipi Paha*, and rendered "Bear Lodge," "Bear's Tipi," or "Bear-Dwelling." Chief Luther Standing Bear, Oglala, wrote that *Mato Tipila* "means dwelling place of the bears," and that *Tipila* "does not, strictly speaking, mean a lodge but rather a living place, or place where bears should live."

The name belonged first to the rock. Warren's 1857 map labels the tower "Bears Lodge," and Engle's sketch of the same year calls it "Mato Tipi or Bear's Lodge." The mountains, and later this reserve, took their name from it. That rock is the one Roosevelt had made the country's first national monument in September 1906 ([Devils Tower National Monument, Wyoming](story:2)). "Bear Lodge" was its first published English name, nearly a quarter century before Colonel Dodge coined "Devil's Tower" in 1875.

The tower has many names. The Lakota alone have several, including *He Hota Paha* (Grey Horn Butte) and *Wanaghipaha* (Ghost Mountain). Cheyenne (*Nakoeve*, Bear Peak), Arapaho (*Woox-niii-non*, Bear's Tipi), Crow (*Dabicha Asow*, Bear's House) and Kiowa (*Tso-aa*, Tree Rock) peoples have their own. The National Park Service counts more than twenty tribes with cultural ties to the site.

The tower sits near the reserve, but it was never inside it. The 1906 monument lies one to two ranges west of the reserve's western edge, roughly six to twelve miles away.

## Political impact
Bear Lodge became a running story in Crook County, covered for at least three months under its own headlines in the *Crook County Monitor* of Sundance.

On March 15, 1907, the *Monitor* printed a letter of March 7 from Representative Frank W. Mondell, describing the rush from the receiving end. The reserve, he wrote, "like many others, was created hurriedly just before the president signed the sundry civil bill, which prohibits the creation of any further reserves in Wyoming and other western states, except by an act of congress." (The bill was in fact the Agricultural Appropriation Act.) The Forestry Bureau had meant to return two townships to settlement, Mondell said, but "evidently they forgot to do this in their rush to have the reserve established before the law prohibiting further reserves went into effect."

On March 22 the paper ran "NO BEAR LODGE RESERVE," reporting that the Interior Secretary had revoked a January 4, 1906 withdrawal and restored the land to settlement. Its editorial page was frank: "the majority of our people will rejoice at the decision of the department." The story ran again on March 29.

On April 19 came the correction, under "BEAR LODGE FOREST RESERVE / Official Correspondence From Washington Explains Situation." It was the Forester's own reply to Mondell: "The Bear Lodge National Forest has not been abandoned and no steps have as yet been taken looking toward that end… I must confess, as things now stand, I am not in favor of the abandonment of the northern part of the Bear Lodge National Forest." A week later the *Monitor* reported "disappointment" among people in from the Bear Lodge country and noted that "the idea of a forest reserve is not at all popular with some of the ranchers in the vicinity." G. C. Smith, put in charge of the reserve, had moved his family to the Paulson ranch. By May 10 the paper was printing the Forest Service grazing chief's word on free grazing for established users through the 1907 season.

Washington noticed too. On March 4, 1907, the *Evening Star* listed "Bear Lodge forest reserve, Wyoming" among "the forest reserves that have been created or added to in the last day or two."

## Legacy
Bear Lodge became a national forest three days after it was created. No proclamation was needed: the Act of March 4, 1907, declared that "forest reserves … shall be known hereafter as national forests," and by late March the Forester's office was writing "the Bear Lodge National Forest."

The name did not last long. Executive Order 850 of June 26, 1908, effective July 1, combined Bear Lodge with part of the Black Hills National Forest to form the Sundance National Forest, 183,224 acres. Sundance was dissolved in turn by Executive Order 2161 of April 6, 1915, effective July 1, and its land went to the Black Hills National Forest.

The name survives on the ground in the Bearlodge Ranger District of the Black Hills National Forest, headquartered at Sundance, Wyoming. The surrounding landscape remains sacred to Northern Plains tribes, tying this quiet 1907 proclamation to the same living questions of stewardship and meaning that surround Devils Tower.

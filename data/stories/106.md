## At a glance
Roosevelt signed his last executive orders on March 2 and 3, 1909, the two days before William Howard Taft took office. Most of the six closed out routine civil-service business, and one gave federal workers in Washington time off for the inauguration. The last conservation order of his presidency set aside the Bogoslof Islands, a cluster of young volcanic islets in the Bering Sea, as a refuge for nesting seabirds.

## Background
**Bogoslof.** Executive Order 1049, dated March 3, 1909, used the same one-paragraph form as Roosevelt's other bird reservations (see [The Bird Reservations of February 1909](story:105)). It ordered "that the volcanic islets, commonly known as the Bogoslof islands of the Aleutian archipelago, Alaska," be "reserved and set apart for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." It closed them to hunting, trapping and egging except under the Department's rules, and it ended: "This reserve to be known as the Bogoslof Reservation."

The islands lie about sixty miles northwest of Unalaska and Dutch Harbor in the eastern Aleutians. They are among the most active young volcanoes in the Aleutian arc. With each eruption they rise, change shape and partly wash away. Even so, they hold large seabird colonies: as many as 90,000 murres, kittiwakes, puffins and gulls nest there. The islands also hold a Steller sea-lion rookery and a northern fur-seal rookery.

**The other five.** None of the remaining orders touched conservation, and several say something more specific than their catalog titles:
- **Executive Order 1045** (March 2) named one man: "Mr. James Mullenbach may be appointed inspector in charge of information and distribution work, Immigration Service, Chicago, Ill., without examination under the civil-service rules, in view of his peculiar qualifications for the work involved."
- **Executive Order 1046** (March 2) rewrote the Department of Justice exception in the civil-service rules, first set by Executive Order 209 in 1903, to cover "[a]ny person employed as field deputy in the office of a United States marshal or whose chief duties are to serve process." Field deputies and process-servers stayed outside the competitive service. Office deputies in marshals' offices came inside it.
- **Executive Order 1047** (March 2) also named a man: "Mr. John M. Longan, of Massachusetts, may be appointed to a clerical position in the classified service without reference to the requirements of the civil-service rules."
- **Executive Order 1048** (March 2) gave federal employees in the District of Columbia time off on Inauguration Day, March 4. That description comes from a catalog label only, because no full text has been found. Whether the order covered the whole day, and whether it covered every employee, is not known.
- **Executive Order 1050** (March 3) reads in full: "The two employees under the appropriation 'Enforcement of antitrust laws' and the four under the appropriation 'Five Civilized Tribes' in the Department of Justice, who have been commended by the Attorney-General for retention in the public service, may be transferred to the competitive classified service after appropriate noncompetitive examination and approval by the Civil Service Commission." Two plus four makes the six of the catalog title. The transfer still depended on an examination and the Commission's approval, so the order made these employees eligible for transfer. It did not transfer them.

The executive orders were only part of Roosevelt's final paperwork. On March 2 he also signed fourteen proclamations, all of them conservation actions. Eleven enlarged or reorganized national forests. Others moved several Indian reservations into surrounding forests and created a new Zuni National Forest. Proclamation 869 created [Mount Olympus National Monument, Washington](story:7). Those proclamations are covered in [a separate story](story:108). Bogoslof counts as the last conservation act only because it is dated one day later.

## Historical context
Four days earlier, on February 27, Roosevelt had signed bird reservations for the Bering Sea, Tuxedni, Saint Lazaria, the Yukon Delta and the Pribilofs, carrying the refuge system up to the seabird colonies of the far northern coast. Bogoslof belonged to the same push. He kept making reservations into the last working days of his term for two reasons. He was convinced they were plainly in the public interest. He also knew that the power behind them, executive withdrawal of unclaimed public land, would soon pass to a successor whose enthusiasm for it was untested.

The civil-service orders moved a few more posts into the merit system, as Roosevelt had done throughout his presidency. The Inauguration Day order was a small piece of housekeeping for a peaceful handover of power.

## Political impact
None of the six drew notice. Bogoslof passed in silence, like the rest of the bird reservations. The appointment and civil-service orders were routine, and the inauguration excusal was an expected courtesy. Nobody opposed any of them.

Executive Order 1050 was the last numbered order Roosevelt signed. The series picked up again with Executive Order 1051 on March 16, 1909, under Taft. Taft took the oath on March 4 in the Senate chamber instead of on the East Front. A blizzard had dropped ten inches of snow on Washington and brought down trees and telephone poles across the city. Clearing fifty-eight thousand tons of snow and slush from the parade route took six thousand men and five hundred wagons. That morning Roosevelt and the president-elect left the White House together by carriage at 10:45. Roosevelt spent his last hour as president in the President's Room off the Senate chamber, signing bills as they came over from the floor.

## Legacy
Franklin Roosevelt's Proclamation 2416 of July 25, 1940 renamed the federal wildlife reservations, and the Bogoslof Reservation became the Bogoslof National Wildlife Refuge. Congress designated the Bogoslof Wilderness on October 23, 1970, at "three hundred and ninety acres." The Fish and Wildlife Service now gives the island as 175 acres. Both figures were right in their day: the 2016–17 eruption, in the Geological Survey's words, "greatly modified the topography," and an island that keeps building and wearing itself away never holds a fixed size. The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of December 2, 1980 merged eleven Alaska refuges into the Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge. Bogoslof and little Fire Island next to it are now part of its Aleutian Islands unit. The Steller sea-lion rookery on Bogoslof is listed as a major rookery in federal critical-habitat rules, and "the Bogoslof area" of the Bering Sea shelf is one of three special Steller sea-lion foraging areas in Alaska.

Roosevelt started the refuge system at Pelican Island in 1903 and made his last addition at Bogoslof in 1909. It grew into the National Wildlife Refuge System, which today counts more than 570 national wildlife refuges nationwide.

The five administrative orders left no legacy of their own. Their civil-service changes were absorbed into the growing merit system.

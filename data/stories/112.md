## At a glance
Clifford Lord's index lists twenty-five Roosevelt entries for 1903. Two are numbered documents listed a second time, which leaves twenty-three unnumbered orders. One is the most consequential unnumbered order of the presidency: Pelican Island, the first federal bird reservation. Most of the rest lay out the Army's ground in the Philippines and Puerto Rico. They also turned a Wyoming forest reserve into a rifle range, set aside the old Camp McDowell for the Yavapai people living there, and adjusted a run of posts and land offices from Alaska to Minnesota. *(For what the unnumbered orders are and how they're counted, see [The Unnumbered Executive Orders](story:110).)*

## Background
**Pelican Island.** Lord gives it one flat line: "Pelican Island Reservation, Volusia County, Fla., established as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds, and boundaries described." He also cross-references Executive Order 1014, which enlarged the reservation in 1909. That plain entry marks the founding of what became the National Wildlife Refuge System, the first time the federal government set land aside expressly to protect wildlife. The full story is told in [Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1). What Lord's catalog adds is this: the 1903 order was unnumbered, and the 1909 enlargement was numbered.

Two details in Lord's line are wrong or uncertain. The first is the county. The order itself names no county. Its whole operative text reads: "It is hereby ordered that Pelican Island in Indian River in section nine, township thirty-one south, range thirty-nine east, State of Florida, be, and it is hereby, reserved and set apart for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." In 1903 the island lay in Brevard County. St. Lucie County was carved out of southern Brevard in 1905, and Indian River County out of northern St. Lucie in 1925. Volusia County is far to the north.

The second is the date, and it can't be fully settled yet. Every contemporary federal source, from the Agriculture Department's own reports and compilations to T. S. Palmer's Biological Survey Circular 87 (1912), says March 14. The Surveyor-General of Florida reported: "On March 14, 1903, the President made an order reserving this island for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." The date March 13 comes from Roosevelt's own 1909 enlargement order, which refers back to the original by that date, and Lord follows the 1909 order. The 1903 Agriculture *Yearbook* gives a third version: "in April, 1903."

**The Philippine and Puerto Rican posts.** Most of 1903's orders built out the military map of the new possessions. A single order of September 1, 1903 set apart fifteen Philippine military reservations at once. Among them were Angeles (later Camp Stotsenberg), Batangas (Camp McGrath), Los Baños (Camp Eldridge), Tacloban (Camp Bumpus), and Ormoc (Camp Downes).

Other orders that year added Torrey Barracks at Malabang, Nonucan (later Camp Overton) on Mindanao, Camp Hartshorne on Samar, the Siassi pipe-line right-of-way in Moro Province, and the Fort San Pedro reservations. The Angeles tract of 1903 came to 7,668.8 acres. That's large, but it isn't yet the enormous Camp Stotsenberg reservation that later became Clark Field. That reservation grew out of an enlargement of April 30, 1908, and by 1910 it covered more than 151,000 acres.

Baguio was set apart in a separate order of October 10, 1903. The reservation covered 535.63 acres in Benguet Province, on Luzon. Roosevelt's own later order enlarging it, Executive Order 553 of January 23, 1907, refers to "the military reservation of Camp John Hay, at Baguio, … as reserved by Executive Order of October 10, 1903." The name came after Hay died on July 1, 1905; by March 1906 the *Evening Star* reported that General Leonard Wood had ordered the cantonment "be known as Camp John Hay."

In Puerto Rico, an order of June 30, 1903 set apart a group of military reservations: Aibonito, Aguadilla, Henry Barracks, Mayagüez, the San Juan posts, and the harbor islands. It was modified twice that November. (The other Puerto Rico reservations of that June are told in [The Puerto Rico Land Reservations of 1903](story:44).)

**The West and Alaska.** An order of October 9, 1903 created the Fort D. A. Russell Target and Maneuver Reservation in Albany County, Wyoming, in the Pole Mountain country about thirty miles west of Cheyenne. This wasn't the fort itself, which sat beside Cheyenne in Laramie County, but a separate tract, made by transferring the Crow Creek Forest Reserve to the War Department as a rifle range for infantry and artillery. Crow Creek was William McKinley's: he had created it by Proclamation 450 on October 10, 1900. The transfer took in the whole reserve, about 56,000 acres, and it was made "with the understanding that the use of the lands for such purposes shall not interfere with the objects for which the Forest Reserve was established." The land stayed in forest reservation until an order of April 19, 1910 ended it.

On September 15, the abandoned Camp McDowell reservation in Arizona was set aside for the people living on and around it. The order's own words are "for the use and occupancy of such Mojave-Apache Indians as are now living thereon or in the vicinity and such other Indians as the Secretary of the Interior may hereafter deem necessary to place thereon." "Mojave-Apache" was a period misnomer. The people were Yavapai, and the community there today, the Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation, traces its land to this order. Its 1999 constitution begins "formerly known as the Fort McDowell Mohave-Apache Community."

The Moapa River (Paiute) reservation in Nevada was enlarged by four lots totaling 103.28 acres. Land offices were reshuffled across Minnesota, at St. Cloud, Marshall, Cass Lake, Crookston, and Duluth.

In Alaska, the Skagway Military Reservation (466.12 acres) was established. At Valdez, the Army set up a Signal Supply Station for its Alaska military-telegraph line. It covered 24,012 square feet, about half an acre, more a town lot than a reservation. On December 31, 1903, Fort Liscum at Port Valdez was cut back from 1,600 acres to 659.89, and the 940.11 acres it gave up went to the Interior Department.

## Historical context
The United States was settling into the islands it had taken from Spain in 1898, and these orders drew the boundaries of the posts it meant to keep. In the American West, the land offices were still following settlement, and a forest reserve created only three years earlier could be turned to military use with a single signature. And on the Florida coast, the same presidential power found a new use: reserving a small island of federal land as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds.

## Political impact
The reservation orders were signed without announcement. No paper in the Library of Congress's digitized newspapers appears to have carried the Pelican Island order in March 1903. That collection has no 1903 issues of Brevard County's own paper, the Titusville *Florida Star*, and its Florida holdings for the year are thin, so the silence isn't proof that nothing ran.

Within weeks, the reservation itself became news. The *DeLand Weekly News* of May 8, 1903 told Florida readers that "Pelican Island in Indian river has been designated as a government reservation. No shooting will be allowed on the island or near it." A New York *Sun* wire story out of Washington, headlined "A GOVERNMENT AVIARY: The Agricultural Department Has Established One on Pelican Island, Florida," ran in the *Minneapolis Journal* on June 8, 1903. About three dozen papers from Walla Walla to Savannah reprinted it over the rest of the year. One reprint, in the *Chipley Banner* of August 6, explained the quiet: "The reservation was acquired by the government several weeks ago, but nothing was said about it as visitors were not wanted." Roosevelt enlarged the reservation in 1909 with a numbered order, Executive Order 1014.

For the rest of the year's orders, the effects show up in the government's own paperwork over the next few years:
- **Valdez.** The Signal Supply Station was trimmed from 24,012 to 22,946 square feet by an order of June 30, 1904.
- **Fort Liscum.** The 940.11 acres cut from the post on December 31, 1903 went to the Interior Department.
- **Baguio.** By March 1906 General Leonard Wood had ordered that the post be known as Camp John Hay, after the Secretary of State who had died the summer before. Roosevelt enlarged the reservation by Executive Order 553 on January 23, 1907.
- **Angeles.** The 7,668.8-acre tract of 1903 was enlarged on April 30, 1908, and by 1910 the Camp Stotsenberg reservation covered more than 151,000 acres.
- **Pole Mountain.** The Crow Creek Forest Reserve kept its forest status after the transfer to the Army. Taft's Executive Order 1080 of May 28, 1909 excluded 320 acres of it, and his Executive Order 1192 of April 19, 1910 finally provided that the lands "should no longer be regarded as in reservation for forest purposes."
- **Puerto Rico.** The June 30 order setting apart the island's military reservations was modified twice in November 1903.

## Legacy
Setting Pelican Island aside, the 1903 orders were administrative, but on a large scale. Through the President's own orders rather than by statute, they quietly laid out the American military presence in the Philippines and Puerto Rico. That habit of governing the new possessions by executive order is part of the larger story of how far executive power reached in these years (compare [Governing the Panama Canal Zone: the Executive Orders of 1904](story:14)).

### Pelican Island
The one-line order of 1903 marks the founding of the National Wildlife Refuge System, the first time the federal government set land aside expressly to protect wildlife. The full story is told in [Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1).

### The Philippine posts
Most of the place-names in these orders made up the American garrison map of the islands for the next forty years. The Angeles reservation, after its 1908 enlargement, grew into the Camp Stotsenberg reservation that later became Clark Field. Several of the Philippine posts went on to long and sometimes tragic histories in the Second World War.

### Pole Mountain
The Wyoming maneuver ground appears in the War Department's compilations from 1916 on as the Pole Mountain Reservation. The compilations never agreed on its size: the 1907 and 1910 editions give about 36,800 and 36,320 acres, figures that can't fit the boundary of the old forest reserve, while the 1916 edition gives about 56,320. The orders themselves show that the whole reserve, about 56,000 acres, was transferred.

### Fort McDowell
The land set apart at Camp McDowell is still home to the people it was reserved for. The Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation ties its territory to this order, and its 1999 constitution opens by noting that the community was "formerly known as the Fort McDowell Mohave-Apache Community," an echo of the period misnomer in the order itself.

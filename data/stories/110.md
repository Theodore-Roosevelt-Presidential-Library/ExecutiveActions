## At a glance
Executive orders weren't numbered at all until 1907, when the State Department put numbers on the orders it happened to have on file. Plenty of Roosevelt's orders weren't in those files, so they never got a number. The standard guide to these orders lists 126 Roosevelt entries, from September 20, 1901 to December 1, 1908. Once the numbered orders that it lists a second time are taken out, 105 distinct unnumbered orders remain. Most of them are routine paperwork. Two are landmarks: Pelican Island and Breton Island, the first two federal bird reservations.

## Background
Roosevelt is usually credited with 1,081 executive orders. That figure belongs to the American Presidency Project, which built it from Lyn Ragsdale's *Vital Statistics on the Presidency* and its own research, and it should always be credited to them. It isn't an official count. The National Archives publishes no total for Roosevelt at all, because its Executive Orders Disposition Tables don't reach back past Hoover. The 1,081 also isn't a count of numbered orders only. The Presidency Project's own footnote says its pre-1945 figures combine numbered and unnumbered orders. So the problem with the figure isn't that it leaves the unnumbered orders out. It's that it counts too few of them.

The numbering came late. The Department of State didn't number executive orders until 1907. In the words of the standard index, "all of the relatively few Orders then on file in that Department were arranged chronologically and given a number, as was every Order subsequently filed." The series started at 1 and reached back to Abraham Lincoln's order of October 20, 1862, which set up a provisional court in Louisiana. That's why Executive Order 1 belongs to Lincoln and not to Washington. Roosevelt's numbered orders fall between 141 and 1050. But the 1907 numbering only covered orders that were in State's files. A whole parallel body of Roosevelt orders never got numbers. Many dealt with Navy pay, land-office boundaries, and military reservations in the island possessions. These aren't lesser documents. They're simply the ones the numbering missed.

The standard guide to them is the *List and Index of Presidential Executive Orders, Unnumbered Series*, edited by Clifford L. Lord and published by the New Jersey Historical Records Survey of the Work Projects Administration in Newark. Lord is named on the title page as editor. The preface credits the compiling to Doty Hobart and Madelaine Caldecott, and the index was prepared under Frank Roth. No publication date is printed anywhere in the volume. The usual date, 1943, is written in pencil on the title page. The only printed dates are in the preface, signed July 1, 1942, and in Lord's index note, signed at Cooperstown, New York, on October 11, 1942. For each order, the guide gives a date, a one-line description, and where the text can be found. The most common sources are James D. Richardson's *Messages and Papers of the Presidents*, Charles Kappler's *Indian Affairs, Laws, and Treaties*, the annual reports of the General Land Office and the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, the Civil Service compilations, and the War Department's *Military Reservations, National Cemeteries and Military Parks*.

**Counting them.** Lord lists 126 Roosevelt entries. Roosevelt took office on September 14, 1901, and the first entry is dated September 20. The last is dated December 1, 1908. The first six give a sense of the range:

- **September 20, 1901:** the Fort Sill Military Reservation in Comanche County, Oklahoma Territory, enlarged by 893.07 acres to hold Apache prisoners of war
- **September 23:** land removed from a Colorado forest reserve (the Battlement Mesa reserve, printed "Black Mesa" in Richardson)
- **October 1:** T. S. Sharretts appointed special appraiser of duties on imports into China
- **October 15:** ratings and pay set for enlisted men of the Navy's Commissary Branch
- **October 30:** Harbor Island and three islets in Sitka Harbor reserved for the Revenue Cutter Service
- **November 9:** lands in Zambales and Bataan, on Luzon, reserved for naval purposes

Not every one of the 126 entries is a separate unnumbered order. Twenty-one turn out to be numbered documents that Lord lists a second time. One is the January 8, 1907 order requiring every Navy vessel to be officially designated by its name preceded by "United States Ship" or "U.S.S." Lord's description matches the text of Executive Order 549 of the same date almost word for word. Two others are Lord's July 1, 1908 Deschutes and Fremont forest entries. These are Executive Orders 816 and 817, filed under the date they took effect rather than the date they were approved. July 1, 1908 is an effective date. Those orders were signed in June. Take out all twenty-one and **105 distinct unnumbered orders** remain. The figure of "about 75" that's often repeated is just 1,081 credited orders minus 1,006 numbered ones. It falls well short of what Lord documents.

There are two cautions about counting at all. Lord warns that a group of same-day items "may have been effected by a group of individual Orders or, more probably, by a single Order." The three Oklahoma land-office entries of January 20, 1902 are exactly that case. His sequence also isn't reliably in date order. One page runs March 25, then March 18, then March 26, and several dates are bracketed as his own inferences or footnoted as conflicting with other sources.

**Where the texts are.** Presidential proclamations have been printed in the *Statutes at Large* since 1845. Executive orders, numbered or unnumbered, generally were not. Until the Federal Register Act of July 26, 1935 (the first issue appeared March 14, 1936), nothing required an executive order to be published anywhere. That's why the Library of Congress says it is "practically impossible to determine exactly how many Executive Orders were produced by each president before Truman." The signed originals of the numbered series went to the Department of State and are now in National Archives Record Group 11. The Library of Congress has digitized the *Theodore Roosevelt Papers, Series 5: Speeches and Executive Orders, 1899–1918*. For the unnumbered orders, Lord's citations are the map, and his key needs a little care. A citation without parentheses points to a source that prints the full text of the order. A citation inside parentheses points only to a later document that mentions it. Every "X Richardson" citation means volume X, and Richardson's coverage of executive orders is selective.

## Historical context
These orders are the everyday machinery of the executive branch, and they follow the shape of Roosevelt's presidency.

They reorganized the public-land offices of a West that was still filling up. On January 20, 1902, the Perry and Enid land offices in Oklahoma Territory were abolished and their business moved to Guthrie and Kingfisher. Two years later, on January 15, 1904, a separate order closed the Oklahoma City office. Offices were shuffled across Minnesota, Idaho, and the Dakotas. In Alaska, a single order of April 2, 1902 merged the Circle, Sitka, and Yukon districts, closed their offices at Rampart City, Sitka, and St. Michael, and replaced them with one Juneau Land District covering the whole Territory.

They set apart military and naval reservations at home and, in great numbers, across the possessions won in 1898: the Philippines, Puerto Rico, Alaska, and Cuba. On a single date, September 1, 1903, fifteen Philippine posts were set apart. Among them were Angeles (later Camp Stotsenberg, some 7,668 acres), Batangas, Bayambang, Bongao, Los Baños, Lucena, Nueva Cáceres, Ormoc, San Fernando, Tacloban, and Camp Jossman on Guimaras. Other orders fixed Navy enlisted pay and discharge rules for a fleet that was being rebuilt. Some made individual civil-service employees eligible for transfer or reinstatement.

Some lowered flags to half-mast for the deaths of public figures: for Lord Pauncefote, the British Ambassador, on May 27, 1902, and for former Agriculture Secretary J. Sterling Morton on April 29, 1902. Others established or adjusted Indian reservations, and there are more of these than one might expect. San Felipe Pueblo gained land on June 13, 1902 and Nambe Pueblo on September 4, 1902. The White Mountain and San Carlos reservation was diminished on December 22, 1902, Pine Ridge on January 25, 1904. Santa Clara Pueblo was enlarged on July 29, 1905.

Lord's own note puts it fairly: "no distinction can be made between the numbered and unnumbered Order on the basis of subject matter, general applicability, public interest, or legal effect. The sole difference is an arbitrary one based entirely on whether the given Order does or does not bear a number." His preface says it more plainly still: "Numbered and Unnumbered orders cover exactly the same fields. Both types are equally important. The only distinction that can be made between them is this arbitrary one, the state of being numbered, which depends entirely on whether or not the State Department received a copy for its files."

## Political impact
Almost none of these orders drew notice on its own. They were routine technical acts that mattered to the clerk, officer, or land district they touched and to hardly anyone else. They are worth knowing as a body, not one by one. Land offices opened and closed, posts laid out on the far side of the Pacific, sailors' pay adjusted: this was how a working presidency ran, one signature at a time.

## Legacy
Two of these orders stand far above the rest, and both are unnumbered.

[Pelican Island](story:1), in Florida's Indian River, was the first federal bird reservation. Two contemporary records give its date as March 14, 1903. William Dutcher printed the order in full in *The Auk* for January 1904 under the dateline "WHITE HOUSE, March 14, 1903." On the next page he reproduced the Agriculture Department's April 4, 1903 letter appointing Paul Kroegel as warden: "Under an order signed by the President, on March 14, Pelican Island has been reserved." T. S. Palmer's Biological Survey Circular No. 87 (1912) also lists Pelican Island as reservation No. 1, established March 14, 1903. Palmer was the Biological Survey official who carried the request to Roosevelt. The other date, March 13, comes from a single later source: Roosevelt's 1909 enlargement order, Executive Order 1014, which Lord's index follows. The signed 1903 original hasn't been examined, so the date isn't settled beyond question.

[Breton Island](story:22), off the Louisiana coast, was the second, on October 4, 1904. Lord's listing of Breton under that date settles an old conflict between 1904 and 1905.

Pelican Island became the seed of the National Wildlife Refuge System.

## At a glance
In 1906 Theodore Roosevelt signed four nearly identical proclamations lowering American duties on a short list of European goods: crude tartar, brandy and other spirits, still wines and vermouth, and paintings and statuary. Each answered a foreign government that had just given American products its lowest tariff rates. Three were quiet trade housekeeping. The fourth, with Germany, kept American goods from being left out of the lower rates Germany had just granted its treaty partners.

## Background
Section 3 of the Dingley Tariff Act of July 24, 1897, named a handful of imports — argols (crude tartar), brandies and other spirits, champagne and other sparkling wines, still wines and vermuth, and paintings and statuary — and authorized the President to bargain with the countries that shipped them. Whenever a foreign government granted American products concessions "which, in the judgment of the President, shall be reciprocal and equivalent," he could suspend the higher regular duties on those goods by proclamation and put lower rates fixed in section 3 in their place.

In 1906 four countries made such concessions, and Roosevelt answered each one:

- **Switzerland (Proclamation 607, January 1):** Switzerland lifted "all differential customs duties" from American goods and granted its conventional tariff rates. The American reductions took effect the day of signing.
- **Germany (Proclamation 615, February 27):** Germany extended its conventional tariff to American products from March 1, 1906, "until June 30, 1907, or until further notice." The American rates took effect March 1.
- **Spain (Proclamation 647, August 27):** a Spanish Royal Decree granted American goods the "second or minimum column" of the Spanish tariff, plus every cut Spain made by law or treaty with other nations, Portugal's special advantages alone excepted. The American rates took effect September 1.
- **Bulgaria (Proclamation 655, September 15):** the Principality of Bulgaria extended its conventional rates, "the same being the lowest rates applied by Bulgaria to the like products of any other country," from June 5. The American rates took effect September 30.

The American side of the bargain was the same in all four: argols at five percent, brandies at $1.75 per proof gallon, still wines at thirty-five cents a gallon in casks, and paintings, pastels, drawings and statuary at fifteen percent. Champagne was left off every time, though the law allowed a lower rate for it. One small difference in wording stands out: three of the proclamations find the foreign concessions "reciprocal and equivalent," while Germany's says only "reciprocal concessions."

Secretary of State Elihu Root countersigned the Swiss and German proclamations. The Spanish one went out under Alvey A. Adee and the Bulgarian under Robert Bacon, each as Acting Secretary of State, while Root was away on a tour of South America from July to September.

## Historical context
The mechanism came out of the reciprocity tradition Republicans had written into the protective tariffs of the 1890s. It let the President trade small, defined duty cuts for better access abroad without reopening the whole tariff in Congress. The section-3 agreements in force when Roosevelt took office were William McKinley's, among them a German agreement proclaimed on July 13, 1900, covering the same goods at the same rates. Roosevelt had not issued one of his own until New Year's Day 1906. His two earlier reciprocity proclamations, for Cuba in 1902 ([Suspending Duties on Cuban Vessels](story:38)) and Panama in 1905 ([The Reciprocity Proclamations of 1905](story:61)), rested on a different law, one that waived discriminating shipping duties.

Germany was the case with the most at stake. Its new tariff law took effect on March 1, 1906, along with commercial treaties Germany had signed in 1904 and 1905 with several European states. Those treaties set lower "conventional" rates, and the United States was not a party to them. Berlin gave notice that the 1900 agreement would end on March 1, 1906, and the State Department acknowledged that notice on December 2, 1905. Without a new arrangement, American goods would have paid Germany's higher general rates. The bridge came together in February. On February 18 the German ambassador wrote that a bill was going to the Reichstag to let the Bundesrath grant the United States the lower treaty duties until June 30, 1907, and that Germany expected the reduced section-3 duties in return after March 1. Proclamation 615 delivered the American half. It bought sixteen months to work out something lasting.

The Swiss, Spanish and Bulgarian proclamations were quieter versions of the same exchange. Each followed a tariff change abroad, and each simply matched it.

## Political impact
On paper these were technical customs orders, of most interest to importers, customs officers, and the wine, spirits and art trades. The German question behind Proclamation 615 was different: it was wire news. As early as May 4, 1905, a Nebraska weekly, the *Loup City Northwestern*, ran a Berlin dispatch under the heads "TREATY OF TRADE / GERMAN TARIFF AGREEMENT ENDS IN 1906 / FORMAL NOTICE FROM BERLIN," reporting that the 1900 agreement would end on March 1, 1906, and that Germany stood ready to negotiate a reciprocity treaty. A Utah paper carried the same news under "TO EXCLUDE AMERICAN PRODUCTS."

When the proclamation came, it ran as a straight Associated Press item. The *San Jose Mercury* of March 1, 1906, under "Reciprocity," opened: "WASHINGTON, Feb. 28—President Roosevelt today issued a proclamation imposing the rates of duties provided by Section [3] …" The *San Diego Union* explained that the action answered Germany's grant of its minimum rates to American goods and kept in force rates that would otherwise have ended that day. Papers in Sacramento, Santa Cruz and Santa Barbara ran it too, without editorial comment. The Spanish proclamation got the same brief treatment; the *Chico Record* of August 30 reported under "SPANISH RECIPROCITY TREATY" that "The State Department tonight made public the Presidential proclamation declaring and putting into effect on September 1st, the new agreement as to reciprocal tariff concessions between this Government and Spain."

The larger tariff fight of the Roosevelt years — standpat protectionists against reciprocity men — played out in Congress and on the campaign trail. It did not turn on these proclamations, which carried out authority Congress had already granted.

## Legacy
The German arrangement was a stopgap and was treated as one. A new U.S.–German commercial agreement replaced it in 1907, announced by Roosevelt on June 1 of that year ([The Reciprocity and Copyright Proclamations of 1907](story:69)). Later section-3 proclamations followed for France in 1908 ([The Residual 1908 Proclamations](story:107)) and Spain again in 1909 ([The Last Three Proclamations](story:109)).

The whole section-3 system ended with the Payne-Aldrich Tariff of August 5, 1909, which set up a maximum-and-minimum tariff and directed the President to give notice, within ten days, to every country holding an agreement under section 3 of the 1897 act. Once those agreements ended, imports from those countries paid the rates of the new law. The modern framework for reciprocal trade came later still, with the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934.

## At a glance
On August 8, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed that the Muskogee (Creek) Nation's council had ratified a supplemental agreement with the United States, making its terms law as of July 26, 1902. The agreement rewrote the rules for dividing the Creek Nation's commonly held land into individual allotments. It arrived amid violent resistance from Creek traditionalists, whose leaders were in federal prison the day it was proclaimed.

## Background
The proclamation does one procedural thing. It tells the country that the Creek council has ratified the agreement, so the agreement's provisions "became law according to the terms thereof upon the twenty-sixth day of July, nineteen hundred and two" — the date of the council's act, not the date of the proclamation. That chain of consent, Congress first (June 30, 1902) and then the Creek council (July 26), was exactly what the agreement's own paragraphs 21 and 22 required before its terms could "have the force and effect of law."

What it put into force was one of the instruments that ended the Creek Nation's communal ownership of its land. The plan came from the original Creek agreement, ratified by Congress on March 1, 1901. The Creek domain in Indian Territory, held in common by the tribe, was to be divided so that each enrolled citizen received 160 acres, with "one hundred and sixty acres of land, valued at six dollars and fifty cents per acre" fixed as "the standard value of an allotment." The tribal government was not to "continue longer than March fourth, nineteen hundred and six, subject to such further legislation as Congress may deem proper."

The 1902 supplemental agreement amended that plan in twenty-two numbered paragraphs. Among other things, it:

- capped appraisal at $6.50 an acre;
- gave the Commission to the Five Civilized Tribes exclusive jurisdiction over contests between citizens for the same tract, and let a citizen who had mistakenly selected land away from his own home start over;
- replaced inheritance "according to the laws of the Creek Nation" with chapter 49 of Mansfield's Digest of the Statutes of Arkansas, while providing that only Creek citizens and their Creek descendants could inherit Creek land;
- added to the rolls children born to citizens between July 1, 1900 and May 25, 1901, and children of the 1890 and 1895 rolls who had never been listed;
- laid out section-line roads, town sites, cemeteries and town parks;
- barred the sale of any allotment for five years without the Secretary of the Interior's consent, and made a forty-acre homestead "nontaxable, inalienable, and free from any incumbrance whatever for twenty-one years," declaring any contrary conveyance "absolutely void";
- limited leases to one year for grazing and five for farming without the Secretary's approval; and
- required federal permits for cattle brought into the Nation.

The American Presidency Project calls the document a "treaty," but that is loose. Congress had ended treaty-making with tribes in 1871, and by 1902 these were agreements ratified by statute.

## Historical context
Allotment was the federal Indian policy of the age, and by 1902 it had reached the one region long exempted from it. The Dawes Act of 1887 had begun breaking reservations across the West into individual parcels, on the theory — held sincerely by many reformers, and conveniently by railroads and settlers — that private farms would "civilize" Indians and free up "surplus" land. The Five Civilized Tribes of Indian Territory, the Creeks among them, had been left out. But the Dawes Commission, created in 1893, was sent to negotiate with them anyway, charged with obtaining either a cession or an allotment. The Curtis Act of 1898 supplied the pressure, abolishing the tribal courts and authorizing allotment with or without tribal consent. The Creek agreements of 1901 and 1902 were the tribe's negotiated terms under that pressure.

The supplemental agreement was negotiated by the Dawes Commission — Henry L. Dawes, Tams Bixby, Thomas B. Needles and Clifton R. Breckinridge — and five Creek commissioners: Principal Chief Pleasant Porter, Roley McIntosh, Thomas W. Perryman, Amos McIntosh and David M. Hodge. Porter's government had its own reasons to want the 1901 terms revised. Investment companies were leasing Creek land wholesale, and the agreement's tight leasing clause, and its declaration that any lease or sale in violation was void, answered that concern. Porter may have traveled to Washington in February 1902 partly to stop that leasing, though the record of the trip has not yet been checked against the original clipping.

Inside the Nation, the agreements were bitterly contested. A traditionalist faction known as the Snakes rejected allotment outright and set up a rival government at Hickory Ground, led by Lahtah Micco with Chitto Harjo ("Crazy Snake") as his *heneha*, or spokesman. They demanded that the United States honor the Treaty of 1832, whose Article XIV "solemnly guarantied" the Creek country west of the Mississippi and promised that the Creeks "shall be allowed to govern themselves." (The famous phrase about land held "as long as the grass grows or the water runs" is not in any Creek treaty; it comes from Andrew Jackson's letter to the Creeks of March 23, 1829.)

The confrontation came in two stages. In January 1901, after Snake bands began seizing property and whipping Creeks who supported allotment, Deputy Marshal Grant Johnson arrested Harjo at his home on January 27, with a troop of the Eighth Cavalry encamped near Henryetta. The Snakes pleaded before Judge John R. Thomas in March 1901 and were paroled on suspended sentences. When they resumed whipping Creeks who applied for allotments, they were arrested again in February 1902. Judge Charles W. Raymond revoked their parole, and Harjo and eleven others were sent to the federal penitentiary at Leavenworth, Kansas. They were still there on August 8, 1902, when the proclamation issued, and were released on November 4.

## Political impact
The Creek council's vote was a wire story, and what traveled with it was violence. On July 29, 1902, the *San Francisco Call* ("Indians Are Gathering at Old Hickory Ground"), the *Los Angeles Herald* ("Crazy Snake's Followers") and the *San Diego Union and Daily Bee* ("One Indian Killed by the Posse") carried a dispatch from Checotah, Indian Territory. It reported that Crazy Snake's followers had gathered at the old Hickory Ground to act against the council's passage of the supplemental agreement, and that a marshal's posse had ridden out from Okmulgee and come back with ten prisoners. One Indian had been shot and mortally wounded resisting the officers, and other prisoners were chained to trees in front of the Okmulgee courthouse. A member of the Creek Council was reported found dead near Hickory Ground, shot many times; his name is garbled in the surviving digitized text.

The next day the *Herald* ("Treaty With the Creeks Ratified") and the *Call* ran a Washington item: the Interior Department had been notified of the council's July 26 ratification, and the agreement now made long leases illegal without the Secretary's approval, more than a year for grazing and more than five years for farming or mining. Leasing was news in its own right. A Guthrie item reprinted in the *South San Francisco Enterprise* that September reported land companies said to control as much as 100,000 acres each of Creek land.

By contrast, a search of California papers from August 8 through the end of the month found no notice of the proclamation itself. The vote and the bloodshed around it were the story, not the President's formal announcement. How eastern and Indian Territory papers handled it has not yet been sampled.

Inside Indian Territory, the agreement was argued over in print all summer. The catalog of Chief Pleasant Porter's papers lists a June 7 editorial on the supplemental agreement, a June 13 interview with Porter on its approval, a June 28 editorial on his calling the council, and items on his signing Creek deeds in late August, including two editorials of August 23 that, as the catalog summarizes them, read the new deeds to mean the Creeks could sell their land except for the homestead. A September 12 item, in the catalog's words, reported the Secretary of the Interior as saying that "the intention of the Creek agreement was to prevent Indians losing their lands to speculator-types." That is the period's own account of the agreement's purpose, and it matches the text. As written in 1902, the limits on sale and leasing, the voidness language and the inheritance restriction all pointed against speculators.

Within the Nation, the agreement deepened the split between the tribal government that had negotiated it and the Snake traditionalists who never accepted it. Chitto Harjo and eleven other Snake leaders were in the Leavenworth penitentiary when it took effect, and were not released until November 4, 1902.

## Legacy
### Statehood and the long tail of allotment
The Creek allotment this agreement helped govern was one of the steps that cleared the legal ground for joining Indian Territory and Oklahoma Territory as the State of Oklahoma in 1907 ([Oklahoma Statehood — Proclamation 780](story:17)). But the proclamation completed nothing. Allotment of Creek lands dragged on well past statehood, until Congress prohibited further allotments by an act of March 2, 1917.

### The protections erode
The protections written into the 1902 text did not hold. Congress relaxed the restrictions on selling allotments by an act of May 27, 1908, and the grafting of Creek allotments, especially those of minors, freedmen and full-bloods, and of land over oil, became one of the notorious scandals of early Oklahoma. The stripping of Creek land came later than this agreement, and largely through later laws.

### The Snakes and the Cushing field
There was a lasting irony. Full-blood Snakes who refused to choose allotments were assigned parcels by the Dawes Commission, some in what was thought to be worthless country. Some of that land lay over the Cushing oil field, whose great production began around 1913. The historian Angie Debo, in *And Still the Waters Run* (1940), wrote that when the Cushing pool opened, "it was reported that seventy or eighty Snakes who had been arbitrarily allotted in that region refused for a time to claim their tremendous wealth," and that many "soon began to receive royalties of five hundred dollars a day." The best known was Jackson Barnett, who, Debo wrote, "had belonged to the Snake faction, and had been arbitrarily given an allotment by the Dawes Commission," and whose wells "proved to be the most spectacular producers in the Cushing field."

### The Nation endures
The Creek Nation's government, slated by the 1901 agreement to end in 1906, never did. The Five Civilized Tribes Act of April 26, 1906, expressly continued the Creek "tribal existence and present tribal government," and in 1936 Congress authorized the Nation to adopt a constitution. In *McGirt v. Oklahoma* (2020), the Supreme Court held that Congress had never disestablished the Muscogee (Creek) reservation — the 1901 agreement allotted the land but contains no language of cession — so that, for purposes of the Major Crimes Act, it remains "Indian country," an area the Court described as "a portion of Northeastern Oklahoma that includes most of the city of Tulsa."

## At a glance
Once the United States had its canal treaty with Panama, someone had to govern the ten-mile-wide Canal Zone. Roosevelt did not ask Congress to write a territorial government. He did it himself, by executive order. In March 1904 he appointed a seven-member Isthmian Canal Commission, and on May 9, 1904, he made it the Zone's lawmaking body, answerable to Secretary of War William Howard Taft. That arrangement shaped American rule on the Isthmus for the next seventy-five years.

## Background
Under the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty, signed November 18, 1903, and ratified and proclaimed February 26, 1904, Panama granted the United States the use, occupation and control of a zone "of the width of ten miles extending to the distance of five miles on each side of the center line." That came to about fifty miles long and roughly 436 square miles. Panama City and Colón were expressly left out. Article III gave the United States all the rights "which the United States would possess and exercise if it were the sovereign of the territory … to the entire exclusion of the exercise by the Republic of Panama of any such sovereign rights, power or authority." Panama spent seventy years contesting that second half.

Congress handed the job of governing to the President. The Spooner Act of June 28, 1902, had authorized buying the canal rights, building the canal and appointing a seven-member commission, but it set up no civil government. That came from the Act of April 28, 1904, "An act to provide for the temporary government of the canal zone at Panama." Section 2 provided that until the end of the Fifty-eighth Congress, "all the military, civil, and judicial powers as well as the power to make all rules and regulations necessary for the government of the Canal Zone … shall be vested in such person or persons and shall be exercised in such manner as the President shall direct."

Roosevelt acted in two steps. On March 8, 1904, he appointed the Commission. The members were Rear Admiral John G. Walker (retired), chairman; Major General George W. Davis (retired); William Barclay Parsons; Professor William H. Burr; Benjamin M. Harrod; Carl Ewald Grunsky; and Colonel Frank J. Hecker. This was the second Isthmian Canal Commission; the first, also chaired by Walker, dated from 1899. Roosevelt's letter to the members opened: "Sirs: I have appointed you as the Commission which is to undertake the most important and also the most formidable engineering feat that has hitherto been attempted." An order of March 26 set their pay.

On May 9, 1904, in a letter to Taft signed "Very respectfully, Theodore Roosevelt," he put the Commission to work. Its central directive reads:

> … I direct that all the work of the commission done by virtue of powers vested in me by the act of Congress approved June 28, 1902, in the digging, construction, and completion of the canal, and all the governmental power in and over said canal zone and its appurtenant territory, which by virtue of the act of Congress approved April 28, 1904, and these instructions, shall be vested in said Isthmian Canal Commission, shall be carried on or exercised under your supervision and direction as Secretary of War.

First among the Commission's seven listed powers was "to make all needful rules and regulations for the government of the zone and for the correct administration of the military, civil, and judicial affairs of its possessions until the close of the Fifty-eighth Congress." That Congress ended March 3, 1905, so the grant was temporary, though it was later extended.

Three features of the letter matter more than they usually get credit for:

- **The Secretary of War held a veto.** Every law the Commission made went to Taft, and "should your approval be withheld from any such law, rule, or regulation, then from that time the law, rule, or regulation shall thereafter have no force or effect."
- **The Commission did not take over the courts.** "The municipal laws of the canal zone are to be administered by the ordinary tribunals substantially as they were before the change," and sitting magistrates and justices of the peace stayed in office "if they are suitable persons." The laws in force on February 26, 1904, remained in effect "until altered or annulled by the said commission." That is how Panama's civil and penal codes stayed the working law of the Zone in the first years of American control.
- **The inhabitants got a bill of rights.** The letter promised them "security in their persons, property, and religion" and listed "certain great principles of government which have been made the basis of an existence as a nation which we deem essential to the rule of law and the maintenance of order." These included due process, just compensation, a speedy public trial, protection from double jeopardy and self-incrimination, and freedom of speech, press, assembly and religion.

The letter also made General Davis governor of the Isthmian Canal Zone, acting "in my name, as the chief executive in the canal zone," with the power to grant reprieves and pardons. It closed: "These instructions may be modified and supplemented as occasion shall arise." Davis sailed from New York on May 10, reached Colón on May 17 and, from Culebra on May 19, published his first proclamation as governor.

In practice, a small commission answerable to the Secretary of War, and through him to the President, now made the laws and ran the territory. The Commission legislated, but it did not sit in judgment. This was government created and directed almost entirely by the executive branch.

## Historical context
The Zone was America's to govern because of what had happened the autumn before. Roosevelt badly wanted a canal across Central America, and the Isthmus of Panama, then a province of Colombia, was the preferred route. When the Colombian senate rejected the Hay–Herrán Treaty in 1903, holding out for better terms, Roosevelt was furious.

A revolution for Panamanian independence broke out on November 3, 1903. The U.S. warship *Nashville* had reached Colón the evening before. At daylight on the 3rd, the Colombian gunboat *Cartagena* landed 474 troops, and the *Nashville*'s commander, John Hubbard, let them come ashore. He had no instructions, and Colombia was still in control of the province. His orders reached him two hours after the troops had landed, and no American force landed that day. What actually stranded the Colombian soldiers was the American-run Panama Railroad. Its superintendent, James Shaler, had moved the trains to the Pacific end, sent the two Colombian generals across by themselves, and then refused to carry the troops without a request from the governor of Panama. The generals were arrested in Panama City that evening. Only afterward did Hubbard close the railroad to troops of either side. The stranded battalion was eventually persuaded to sail home. "I beg to assure the Department," Hubbard reported, "that I had no part whatever in the negotiations."

Washington moved fast. On November 6, Hay told the American vice-consul-general to deal with any government "republican in form, and without substantial opposition from its own people," and on November 13 Roosevelt received the credentials of Philippe Bunau-Varilla, a French canal promoter acting as Panama's envoy. Fifteen days after the revolution, on November 18, Hay and Bunau-Varilla signed the treaty. It granted the Zone "in perpetuity" for $10,000,000 in gold when ratifications were exchanged, plus $250,000 a year starting nine years later, so the first annual payment came in 1913.

The speed of it all, and the *Nashville*'s presence, brought charges that Roosevelt had engineered a revolution, or at least taken advantage of one, to get his canal. He answered in a Special Message to Congress on January 4, 1904: "I confidently maintain that the recognition of the Republic of Panama was an act justified by the interests of collective civilization. If ever a government could be said to have received a mandate from civilization to effect an object the accomplishment of which was demanded in the interest of mankind, the United State[s] holds that position with regard to the interoceanic canal." The governing orders followed from that position. Having won the Zone, the administration had to run it, and Roosevelt's instinct was to run it directly and start digging rather than wait for Congress.

## Political impact
The controversy centered on how the Zone was acquired, not on how it was governed. Democrats, anti-imperialists and parts of the press charged that the United States had helped Panama break away from a friendly neighbor and had taken the Isthmus by something close to force. Later the *New York World* and the *Indianapolis News* asked who had really received the $40,000,000 the government paid the New Panama Canal Company, and named Charles P. Taft and Roosevelt's brother-in-law Douglas Robinson. Roosevelt answered in a Special Message of December 15, 1908, and then had the papers prosecuted for libel. He lost. The Supreme Court ended the case in *United States v. Press Publishing Co.* on January 3, 1911, on the ground that the federal law used did not reach the conduct. The Court never decided whether the articles were libelous.

Colombia protested the loss of its province and pressed its claims for nearly twenty years. Roosevelt never apologized. At the University of California's Charter Day on March 23, 1911, out of office, he said, in the stenographic text published that April, "Fortunately the crisis came at a period when I could act unhampered. Accordingly I took the Isthmus, started the canal, and then left Congress — not to debate the canal, but to debate me." The line captured both his pride and what his opponents feared most: a president who made a huge foreign commitment first and told the legislature afterward.

The May 9 order itself did not go unnoticed. It went out on the wire and ran nationally the next morning. The *San Diego Union and Daily Bee* printed it on page one under the headline "NO GAMBLING ON THE STRIP," with all seven instructions word for word, and papers from San Francisco to Denver carried the news that Davis would be governor and the Commission would make the laws. Still, the acquisition remained the controversy, and the machinery of government did not. What the order created was a way of ruling that would define the Zone for generations: efficient, centralized, and answerable to Washington rather than to the people who lived there.

## Legacy
The executive-branch government set up in 1904 became the lasting shape of the Canal Zone. Under the Secretary of War and a series of governors, the Commission drove the construction that opened the Panama Canal on August 15, 1914, when the SS *Ancon* made the first official transit. The formal opening was not proclaimed until July 12, 1920. The project's success depended as much on Colonel William C. Gorgas's campaign against yellow fever and malaria as on the digging.

The Zone stayed a U.S.-run enclave, governed from the executive branch, until the Torrijos–Carter Treaties, signed September 7, 1977, set a timetable for handing it over. The Canal Zone ceased to exist as a jurisdiction on October 1, 1979, when the treaties took effect. The canal itself passed to Panama twenty years later, on December 31, 1999.

The quarrel with Colombia took longer to settle. The Thomson–Urrutia Treaty was signed at Bogotá on April 6, 1914. The 1914 text opened with an American expression of "sincere regret that anything should have occurred to interrupt or to mar the relations of cordial friendship that had so long subsisted between the two nations." That article was dropped by agreement of the two governments in 1919. The Senate consented to the treaty on April 20, 1921, by 69 to 19, and it was proclaimed March 30, 1922. Colombia received $25,000,000, paid in installments through 1926. Colombia called the money an indemnity. The ratified treaty insisted that American title was held "without any encumbrances or indemnities whatever." In the end the United States dropped the apology and kept the payment.

The episode remains the textbook example, cited by admirers and critics alike, of how far presidential power could reach in foreign affairs. Roosevelt never doubted that the canal belonged among his greatest works. A fair record notes both the canal he built and the way the ground to build it was obtained.

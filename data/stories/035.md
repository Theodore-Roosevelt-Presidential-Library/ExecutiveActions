## At a glance
At noon on June 17, 1902, about 1,300 men and boys raced across the line into the ceded part of Idaho's Fort Hall Reservation, many of them on horseback and armed. Roosevelt's proclamation of May 7 had set that hour. It carried out a bargain the Shoshone and Bannock had made under heavy pressure in 1898: roughly 416,000 acres of their reservation, given up for $600,000, now opened to homesteaders, miners, and townsite builders, with the land around the railroad town of Pocatello held back for a separate auction.

## Background
The Fort Hall Reservation in southeastern Idaho was set aside by executive order in 1867 and confirmed by the Treaty of Fort Bridger in 1868. On February 5, 1898, at the agency at Ross Fork, commissioners Benjamin F. Barge, James H. McNeely, and Charles G. Hoyt concluded an agreement under which the Shoshone and Bannock ceded the southern and eastern part of the reservation. It took about seventeen months of negotiating, with several breaks, and 250 Shoshone and Bannock men signed, against a certified adult-male majority of 227. Congress ratified the agreement on June 6, 1900 (31 Stat. 672), in an act signed by President McKinley. Roosevelt's part, two years later, was to carry out a deal already made.

The price was $600,000: $75,000 for a school plant, $100,000 in cash shared out within three months, and the rest in yearly payments. The ratifying act describes the cession only by metes and bounds and states no acreage. The commissioners estimated the tract at "about 418,560 acres," including land already sold to Pocatello and the railroad, and the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, W. A. Jones, put it at "about 416,000 acres" without those pieces. In the commissioners' own words, "The price agreed was $525,000, or about $1.25 per acre." The other $75,000, the school money, was "added ... as a reparation for the right to hunt in Wyoming, which was given them by the Fort Bridger treaty and of which they were deprived by the decision of the United States Supreme Court May 25, 1896," the case of *Ward v. Race Horse*.

Before settlers could enter, the law required three steps. Each Indian already living on and farming land inside the ceded tract who wanted to stay received an allotment. The improvements on the ceded land were appraised for sale. And the land was classed as agricultural or grazing so it could be priced. The Indian agent, A. F. Caldwell, made the allotments: 90 people, 80 acres each, 7,177.17 acres in all. Twenty-three heads of families chose instead to move onto the reduced reservation, and their improvements were appraised at $5,851.50 all told. The proclamation admits on its face that one step was unfinished: the improvements had not yet been sold, "but as this is not considered a bar to the opening."

The proclamation, printed as "[No. 18.]" in volume 32 of the *Statutes at Large* (32 Stat. 1997–1999) and countersigned by Secretary of State John Hay, then set the terms:

- **The general opening.** At noon, Mountain time, on June 17, 1902, the "residue" of the ceded land opened to entry under the homestead, townsite, stone and timber, and mining laws.
- **What stayed out.** Indian allotments, the appraised improvement tracts, the school sections (16 and 36 in each township), the Lava Hot Springs tract, the land within five miles of Pocatello, and the 1888 Pocatello townsite.
- **Prices set by statute.** Ten dollars an acre for land under the Idaho Canal Company's canal, $2.50 for other farmland, $1.25 for grazing land, and no buyer allowed more than 160 acres. Union veterans kept their homestead rights except as to price.
- **The Pocatello ring.** Land within five miles of the town would go to public auction, at no less than ten dollars an acre, beginning at noon on July 17, 1902.

## Historical context
By 1902 the reservation system and the homestead system were pulling hard against each other, and federal policy had settled on the homesteader. The backdrop was the Dawes General Allotment Act of 1887, whose premise was that communal reservations should be broken into individual farms, that this would turn Indians into yeoman landowners, and that whatever was left over was "surplus" for the government to sell. The 1896 law that created the negotiating commission said so plainly: its charge was to treat with the Bannock and Shoshone "for the cession of part of their surplus lands."

Water and the railroad drove the details. The Interior Department had contracted with the Idaho Canal Company to bring Snake River water to "about 95,000 acres of land on said reserve," and had planned for the canal to cover more land than the tribes needed, so that the surplus could be sold and the proceeds used to finish and maintain the irrigation system. When Congress authorized the commission in 1896, the Department sent it to Fort Hall first. The plan did not survive the bargaining. The tribes ceded the land outright and would not let the money be spent on irrigation.

The settler prices came out of the same story. Commissioner Jones and General Land Office Commissioner Binger Hermann both urged that the canal lands be "so disposed of as to insure some return for their real value," and the ten-dollar rate was written into the bill by the House Committee on Indian Affairs in June 1898. Jones estimated the canal lands at "probably 25,000 acres," adding that "competent judges have expressed the opinion that the lands affected are worth at least $10 per acre."

Pocatello had grown up on Fort Hall land once before. The Act of September 1, 1888 (25 Stat. 452) took "1,840 acres, more or less," expressly "for the purposes of a town-site," and gave the Utah and Northern Railway a right of way through the reservation. Fourteen years later the town wanted the ground around it, and the five-mile ring the proclamation drew, auctioned at four times the price of ordinary farmland, shows what that ground was worth.

What the record holds of the tribes' own position is thin. The agreement shows councils were held with interpreters, and some of its articles are plainly the tribes' own terms: the right to cut timber, pasture stock, hunt, and fish on the ceded lands while they stayed public, water reserved for Indians already irrigating, and protection of the money from claims for "damages or depredations." The printed government papers record no objection to the cession itself. They do record the tribes holding firm on one point against the government's wishes: payment in cash, shared out person by person. "We regret that these Indians would not agree to have a portion of the original purchase price placed to their credit and expended by yourself for their benefit," the commissioners reported. Jones called the refusal "to be deplored," and judged that "While the agreement as a whole is not all that might be desired, it is undoubtedly the best that could be effected with these Indians at the present time."

## Political impact
Both dates held. The Commissioner of Indian Affairs recorded in his 1902 report that the lands "were opened to public settlement on June 17, 1902," and that the Pocatello lands "were offered for sale by the Commissioner of the General Land Office on July 17, 1902."

June 17 was a land run. The *New-York Tribune* put it on page one the next morning: "At 12 o'clock exactly to-day 1,300 men and boys rushed across the line of the ceded Fort Hall Reservation," most of them mounted and "a majority ... heavily armed," with "probably a thousand persons" following later with pack animals. Before two o'clock, the paper said, "Pocatello was practically deserted." Claims were filed at the land office in Blackfoot. The *San Francisco Call* headlined its Blackfoot report "LEAD IS READY FOR 'SOONERS' / Bloodshed Expected on Idaho Open Reservation," and described "a steady and persistent flow of applications ... many disappointed applicants and much murmuring," with contests already being filed.

The *Call* also caught a real injustice. Settlers "who have by sufferance of the Government resided upon reservation lands from fifteen to thirty years" were being filed on by newcomers, because "the bill opening the Fort Hall reservation gave no protection to these old settlers."

The season was tense. The Fort Hall agent, Caldwell, reported that "the greater part of the best land was at once entered at the local land office at Blackfoot." Two white men were found murdered near new mining claims on June 24. Reports of Indians firing on settlers circulated, which his investigation "entirely discredited." When some of the people of Pocatello asked the governor for troops, Caldwell answered "that there was no excitement among the Indians and that no troops were needed."

The Pocatello auction on July 17 was an anticlimax. Assistant Commissioner W. A. Richards of the General Land Office opened it at noon with a staff of clerks from Washington, and the wire report was blunt: "There was little interest manifested and the probability is that few tracts will be sold."

The land office figures show how fast the opening moved. In the last two weeks of June 1902, the Blackfoot office recorded 126 sales under the 1900 act, covering 17,539.91 acres. In the whole of the following year it recorded 106, on 6,668.56 acres.

The story traveled well beyond Idaho. "Fort Hall Reservation" appears on well over a hundred digitized newspaper pages between May and July 1902, from Utah and Montana to Ohio and Virginia, though few papers seem to have reprinted the proclamation's text. The local view is harder to recover, since no Pocatello paper from 1902 and almost nothing from Blackfoot survives in the digitized collections.

Of the forty-four proclamations in volume 32 of the *Statutes*, only two open ceded Indian land: McKinley's Wichita-Kiowa proclamation of July 4, 1901, and this one.

## Legacy
The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes remain at Fort Hall, on the reservation this proclamation reduced. The general allotment of what was left came in 1911, under very different terms: twenty acres of irrigable land and 160 of grazing land to each person. Because allotment came so late, Fort Hall is among the least fractionated of the allotted reservations, with most of the land still in tribal ownership or trust.

The cessions of 1888, 1898, and after were argued over for most of a century. Before the Indian Claims Commission, the tribes pleaded that the 1898 cession had been made for "an alleged unconscionable consideration of about $525,000.00." That claim never went to trial. In 1968 the government settled it together with six other Fort Hall claims in a single judgment of $15,700,000, with no part of the sum assigned to the 1898 cession.

The Lava Hot Springs tract that the proclamation held back from settlement went to Idaho by a separate act of June 13, 1902 (32 Stat. 330), "to be held by said State for public use." It is a state resort still, run by the Lava Hot Springs Foundation within the Idaho Department of Parks and Recreation.

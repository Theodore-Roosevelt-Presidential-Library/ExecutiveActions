## At a glance
Roosevelt did not set out to destroy big business. He set out to make it answer to the public. His weapons were disclosure, through a Bureau of Corporations that Congress created in February 1903, and selective prosecution, starting with the 1902 suit against the Northern Securities railroad holding company. In March 1904 the Supreme Court ordered that company dissolved by a vote of five to four.

## Background
The policy starts with Roosevelt's First Annual Message to Congress, December 3, 1901. He began by describing a public mood: "There is a widespread conviction in the minds of the American people that the great corporations known as trusts are in certain of their features and tendencies hurtful to the general welfare." That conviction, he went on, "is based upon sincere conviction that combination and concentration should be, not prohibited, but supervised and within reasonable limits controlled; and in my judgment this conviction is right." He was describing what Americans believed, and then signing his name to it.

His remedy was information. "The first essential in determining how to deal with the great industrial combinations is knowledge of the facts—publicity," the same message said. "Publicity is the only sure remedy which we can now invoke." He also proposed a new Cabinet department to do the work.

Congress created that department on February 14, 1903: the Department of Commerce and Labor, with a Bureau of Corporations inside it (32 Stat. 825; the Bureau is § 6, 32 Stat. 827). The law explains both how far the Bureau could reach and where it had to stop. Its Commissioner could make "diligent investigation into the organization, conduct, and management of the business of any corporation … engaged in commerce among the several States." Railroads and other common carriers were excluded because they already answered to the Interstate Commerce Commission. The Commissioner could subpoena witnesses and documents, and "the information so obtained or as much thereof as the President may direct shall be made public." Nothing came out automatically. Roosevelt decided what was released.

The first Commissioner was James R. Garfield, a son of the assassinated President Garfield. He had been in the presidential party at the Baltimore & Potomac depot in 1881 when his father was shot. Garfield was nominated February 17, 1903, confirmed February 21 and sworn in February 25. He served until 1907, when he became Secretary of the Interior, and Herbert Knox Smith followed him at the Bureau. During Roosevelt's presidency the Bureau published reports on:

- **beef** (March 3, 1905)
- **the transportation of petroleum** (May 2, 1906)
- **the petroleum industry** (1907, in two parts)
- **the cotton exchanges** (1908)
- **tobacco** (February 25, 1909, a week before Roosevelt left office)

The Bureau could not prosecute anyone or regulate anything. What it could do was publish facts and let them do the work. The 1906 petroleum-transportation report shows how. It documented the secret rates and rebates railroads gave Standard Oil, and it fed straight into Roosevelt's special message of May 4, 1906, which asked Congress to give the Interstate Commerce Commission more power.

The government also had a sharper tool, and it used it selectively. On February 19, 1902, Attorney General Philander Knox announced that the government would move against the Northern Securities Company. On March 10 the government filed a bill in equity in the U.S. Circuit Court for the District of Minnesota. Northern Securities was a New Jersey holding company, chartered at Trenton on November 13, 1901, with $400,000,000 in capital. J. P. Morgan and James J. Hill created it to hold the stock of two great northwestern railroads, the Great Northern and the Northern Pacific, and so end the costly fight between them. Those two roads had already bought the Chicago, Burlington & Quincy together in April 1901, so the Burlington sat beneath them rather than inside the holding company. E. H. Harriman, the defeated rival in the May 1901 fight for the Northern Pacific, was elected to the board on November 14, 1901, and bought out the next day.

The suit was brought under the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890. On March 14, 1904, in *Northern Securities Co. v. United States*, the Supreme Court affirmed a decree dissolving the company, five to four. There was no majority opinion. Justice Harlan announced the judgment for four justices: himself, Brown, McKenna and Day. Justice Brewer supplied the fifth vote in a separate concurrence that rested on reasoning the four had rejected. Justices White and Holmes each wrote a dissent, and all four dissenters joined both.

Together the two moves defined the administration's position. Companies that behaved would face steady oversight and disclosure. Companies the government judged to have crossed the line would face the full force of the antitrust law.

## Historical context
The years around 1900 brought a merger wave like nothing before it. The National Bureau of Economic Research counted 3,012 firms swallowed by mergers between 1895 and 1904, including 1,208 in 1899 alone. In 1901 Morgan put together U.S. Steel, capitalized at a little over $1,402,000,000 including bonds and usually described as the first billion-dollar corporation. The Bureau of Corporations later measured its reach: at its formation it controlled about two-thirds of the country's crude-steel production and roughly 60 percent of crude and finished steel together.

To many Americans it looked as if private powers were growing to rival the government itself, and the law seemed weakest where it counted. In *United States v. E. C. Knight Co.* (1895), the Supreme Court held that "commerce succeeds to manufacture, and is not a part of it." That ruling left the Sherman Act looking largely powerless against combinations that controlled manufacturing. The Court did apply the Act to interstate railroads and to a group of pipe manufacturers in the later 1890s, but *Knight* still stood.

Roosevelt's answer was deliberately moderate in tone. He argued that concentration was often the natural, even useful, result of modern industry, and that the job was to supervise it rather than smash it. On this view disclosure was the conservative, stabilizing course: it would expose wrongdoing and reassure honest business without the shock of tearing working companies apart. "Great corporations exist only because they are created and safeguarded by our institutions," he told Congress in 1901, "and it is therefore our right and our duty to see that they work in harmony with these institutions."

Getting even that much through Congress was a fight. In *An Autobiography* (1913, ch. XII), Roosevelt wrote that "the Standard Oil Company took the lead in opposing all this legislation," working "by direct telegrams and letters to Senators and Congressmen from various heads of the Standard Oil organization." Then, he wrote, he "got hold of one or two of these telegrams and letters … and promptly published them," after which "the men who were all-powerful as long as they could work in secret … became powerless as soon as they were forced into the open," and "the bill went through without further difficulty."

The real episode is a little different from the popular version. Roosevelt did not issue a signed statement. On the evening of Saturday, February 7, 1903, he gave the story to the Associated Press without attribution, and it ran in the Sunday papers: "No less than six United States senators have received telegrams signed 'John D. Rockefeller,' urging that no anti-trust legislation be enacted." Within five days it was reported that the leak had come from him. The lobbying campaign was run by John D. Archbold of Standard Oil, who wired Senator Quay on February 6. The telegrams over the Rockefeller name were mostly sent by John D. Rockefeller Jr. at Archbold's urging. The elder Rockefeller's own telegram was mild. The fight was over the Nelson amendment, which strengthened the Bureau and gave the President discretion over what to publish.

## Political impact
The Northern Securities suit landed like a thunderclap because of who was on the other side. Wall Street assumed that matters like this got settled quietly between powerful men. The best-known story of that assumption comes from Joseph Bucklin Bishop's *Theodore Roosevelt and His Time* (1920), which tells it in Bishop's own narration rather than as Roosevelt's account and gives no date for the meeting. Morgan came to the White House and proposed, "If we have done anything wrong, … send your man (meaning the Attorney General) to my man (naming one of his lawyers) and they can fix it up." Roosevelt answered, "That can't be done," and Knox added, "We don't want to fix it up, … we want to stop it." When Morgan asked whether the government meant to go after "my other interests, the Steel Trust and the others," Roosevelt replied, "Certainly not, unless we find out that in any case they have done something that we regard as wrong."

The Bureau itself had to be fought through Congress first, as the Standard Oil telegram episode shows. The measure at stake, the Nelson amendment, strengthened the Bureau and left publication to the President's discretion, and Roosevelt called its publicity clauses "the most important of all" (Morison, ed., *Letters*, vol. III, p. 417). Once in place, the Bureau's reports became a political tool in their own right: the 1906 report on petroleum transportation, with its evidence of secret railroad rates and rebates for Standard Oil, went straight into Roosevelt's May 4, 1906 message asking Congress to strengthen the Interstate Commerce Commission.

The 1904 decision established that the federal government could reach even the largest combination, and Roosevelt counted it among the great achievements of his presidency. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes's dissent became nearly as famous as the ruling. Its second paragraph opens: "Great cases like hard cases make bad law." The breakup had its own aftermath in court: in 1905 E. H. Harriman, who had been bought out of the company in 1901, sued to get his railroad shares back (*Harriman v. Northern Securities Co.*, 197 U.S. 244). Roosevelt was displeased. He wrote to Henry Cabot Lodge that Holmes "has been a bitter disappointment, not because of any one decision but because of his general attitude." Years later, in 1921, Holmes told Sir Frederick Pollock that Roosevelt "looked on my dissent to the *Northern Securities Case* as a political departure."

Roosevelt took fire from both sides, and that was how he liked to describe where he stood. He had put it this way before he reached the White House. In 1899 Senator Thomas C. Platt warned that business leaders wondered "how far the notions of Populism, as laid down in Kansas and Nebraska, have taken hold upon the Republican party of the State of New York." Roosevelt answered that "we Republicans hold the just balance and set ourselves as resolutely against improper corporate influence on the one hand as against demagogy and mob rule on the other." Both letters are printed in *An Autobiography*, ch. VIII.

The attacks did come from both directions. Conservatives argued that federal oversight of state-chartered corporations went beyond the Constitution, and the Old Guard of his own party saw his activism as a threat to business confidence. Other critics thought disclosure and a few selective suits were far too timid. The 1904 Democratic platform managed both complaints at once. It promised to rescue the country from "the headstrong, arbitrary and spasmodic methods which distract business by uncertainty," and in the same document demanded "a strict enforcement of existing civil and criminal statutes against all such trusts, combinations and monopolies" and "such further legislation as may be necessary effectually to suppress them."

Roosevelt stayed in the middle on purpose. In *An Autobiography* (ch. XII) he described a federal agency that "would have the power to discriminate sharply between corporations that are doing well and those that are doing ill." The offense he wanted punished was misconduct, not size.

## Legacy
The "trust-buster" label that later stuck to Roosevelt oversimplifies what he built. His administration did bring dozens of antitrust suits, and the Northern Securities victory made the Sherman Act a real weapon again. Still, his stated preference, repeated often, was for continuous supervision and disclosure. In *An Autobiography* he wrote: "Such a Commission would furnish a steady expert control, a control adapted to the problem; and dissolution is neither control nor regulation, but is purely negative; and negative remedies are of little permanent avail." He was not ruling out dissolution. Two sentences earlier in the same passage, he wants monopoly "uproot[ed] when discovered."

The Bureau of Corporations put that preference into an institution, and its line of descent is direct. The Federal Trade Commission Act of September 26, 1914, provided that once the new Commission was organized, the Bureau would "cease to exist," and its investigations, staff and records would pass to the FTC. The Commission organized on March 16, 1915, and that is the day the Bureau ended. The FTC got what the Bureau never had: the power to enforce.

The biggest antitrust breakups came after Roosevelt left office. *Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States* was decided May 15, 1911, and *United States v. American Tobacco Co.* followed on May 29, 1911. His successor, William Howard Taft, also brought more cases. By the Justice Department's count of proceedings filed, Roosevelt's administration brought forty-four over seven and a half years (eighteen bills in equity, twenty-five indictments and one forfeiture proceeding). Taft's brought ninety in four (forty-six bills in equity, forty-three indictments and one contempt proceeding), roughly four times Roosevelt's yearly pace. Those are cases started, not cases won. The Bureau's own big study of the steel industry also came under Taft: Part I of its report is dated July 1, 1911. It found that U.S. Steel's share of crude steel, about two-thirds in 1901, had fallen by roughly twelve points by 1910.

What Roosevelt left behind was less a tally of broken trusts than a principle that outlasted the arguments of his day. In his words, a corporation exists because it is "created and safeguarded by our institutions," so it can be required to answer to them.

## At a glance
On August 27, 1906, writing from his summer home at Oyster Bay, Roosevelt told the Public Printer to spell 300 words the reformed way in executive-department publications: "thru," "tho," "dropt" and the rest. The press laughed, the Chief Justice raised an eyebrow, and in December the House of Representatives voted against it. Roosevelt dropped the order within days but kept using the spellings in his own letters.

## Background
The instrument was a short letter to Public Printer Charles A. Stillings. Roosevelt sent along circulars from the Simplified Spelling Board and wrote: "Please hereafter direct that in all Government publications of the executive departments the three hundred words enumerated in Circular No. 5 shall be spelled as therein set forth."

No numbered executive order was ever issued. The numbered series jumps from Executive Order 499 (August 17, 1906) to Executive Order 500 (August 29) without a gap. Both the Printing Office and the House still called the letter an "Executive order," and the letter is the document that did the work.

The Printing Office passed the instruction to its compositors in a circular of September 4, 1906. The circular was itself printed in the new spelling ("the President's letter addrest to the Public Printer") and was bound into a booklet, *Simplified Spelling, First Edition, For the Use of Government Departments*, along with Roosevelt's letter and the Board's first six circulars. It also told the typesetters the job was smaller than it looked. Of the 300 words, the office already preferred 153 in the new form, and it set 49 more that way whenever an author asked for copy to be followed. Copy-cutters were issued rubber stamps reading "Nu spelng."

The new spelling showed up in department reports. Secretary of Commerce and Labor Metcalf's annual report "adhered assiduously to the rule of simplified spelling throughout." Roosevelt went further and sent Congress his own messages in it. The Associated Press reported that his Sixth Annual Message of December 4, 1906 used "thru," "past" for "passed," and "demagog." Congress, though, reprinted both that message and his Panama message of December 17 in conventional spelling. The order applied only to the executive branch. It covered neither Congress nor the courts, which print their own work, and it required nothing of private citizens. Roosevelt made that point, and his critics paid it no attention.

The GPO's printing of the letter has its own small joke. Because the Printing Office applied the reform to everything, its copy has Roosevelt saying "dropt." The Associated Press text, which went out from Oyster Bay, reads "dropped," and the *San Francisco Call* of September 3, 1906 noticed: "the President in his letter to the Public Printer fails to use the reforms in spelling which he advocates."

## Historical context
In 1906 spelling reform was a respectable Progressive cause with money and well-known names behind it. The Simplified Spelling Board was announced in New York on March 11, 1906, five months before Roosevelt's letter. Andrew Carnegie funded it with a substantial subsidy, believing a simpler, more phonetic English would spread literacy and might one day serve as a world language. The Board's members included Yale's Thomas R. Lounsbury (the "Professor Lounsbury" of Roosevelt's letter), Columbia's Brander Matthews (the Board's chairman and a personal friend of the president), Mark Twain, and Melvil Dewey.

The argument rested on efficiency, a value the era prized above most others. English spelling was a tangle of silent letters and historical accidents that cost every schoolchild and every typesetter time. Roosevelt's letter made that case and claimed nothing larger:

> "There is not the slightest intention to do anything revolutionary or initiate any far-reaching policy. … It is merely an attempt to cast what slight weight can properly be cast on the side of the popular forces which are endeavoring to make our spelling a little less foolish and fantastic."

He presented the change as the next step in an "unconscious movement" that had already made farmers "write 'plow' instead of 'plough'" and most Americans "write 'honor' without the somewhat absurd, superfluous 'u.'" The idea suited him. He liked action and efficiency, disliked waste, and had a reformer's confidence that a sensible improvement, once people saw it, would win on its own.

## Political impact
It didn't win. The response was quick, loud and mostly mocking, and it was one of the few times a Roosevelt initiative made him look a little ridiculous rather than formidable. Newspapers in the United States and Britain enjoyed themselves. The London press treated the order as proof of American barbarism, and American editors ran gleeful columns misspelling the president's name.

### A raised eyebrow at the Supreme Court
The Supreme Court answered with a dry remark rather than a joke. On October 30, 1906, during argument in *Dalcour v. United States*, Solicitor General Henry M. Hoyt quoted an opinion of Justice Bradley with "through" respelled as "thru." Chief Justice Melville Fuller, holding a copy of the brief, asked whether the extract was meant to quote Bradley's official opinion. "In all except the spelling," Hoyt replied. "Ah!" said Fuller, in a tone that the *Evening Star* said "caused a general smile through the court room." Hoyt explained that the Department of Justice had tried to follow the new order but did not think it had any business changing the spelling of judicial opinions, and that such changes "would be guarded against" from then on. The exchange was about a lawyer's brief, not the Court's own printing, and the Court never adopted the system.

### Congress and the power of the purse
Roosevelt carried the reform into his own messages to Congress. The Associated Press reported that his Sixth Annual Message, read in the Senate on December 4, 1906, used "thru," "past" for "passed," and "demagog." Congress printed the message in the old spelling in the *Congressional Record*. When his Panama message followed on December 17, the AP reported that at Senator Lodge's suggestion it would be reprinted for the Senate in conventional spelling, and it was.

By then the House had already acted, in two steps. On Wednesday, December 12, the House sat as the Committee of the Whole on the legislative, executive and judicial appropriation bill. The Appropriations Committee had written in a paragraph requiring the Printing Office to follow "the rules of orthography established by Webster's or other generally accepted dictionaries." It was struck on a point of order, as legislation on an appropriation bill. Representative Bingham of Pennsylvania at once offered the same idea as a limit on spending: no money in the act could be used to print documents "unless the same shall conform to the orthography recognized and used by generally accepted dictionaries of the English language." On a division called for by Representative Mann, it carried 142 to 25.

The next day, Thursday, December 13, Charles B. Landis of Indiana, chairman of the Committee on Printing, brought up House Resolution 663. It was taken up by unanimous consent and adopted without a division, declaring it "the sense of the House" that the Printing Office "should observe and adhere to the standard of orthography prescribed in generally accepted dictionaries of the English language," including in publications "emanating from the Executive Departments." The committee's report dated the "Executive order" to August 27, 1906, the day of Roosevelt's letter.

This was the real lesson of the episode. A president could give orders to his own printer, but Congress paid the printer, and Congress could simply refuse to pay for the order. Landis went to see Roosevelt on December 13, and Roosevelt told him he didn't want spelling to crowd out more important business and would revoke the order if the House went on record. On December 14, on Representative Tawney's motion, the House struck the spelling limit from the appropriation bill without debate, "because of the promise of the president to revoke his order to the public printer." So the 142-to-25 vote never became law.

No written revocation has been found. The press reported only that the President "will issue an executive order withdrawing the order." Joseph Bucklin Bishop wrote in 1920 that "on December 13, the President rescinded the order," but cited no document. What is clear is that Roosevelt gave the order up and it lapsed in mid-December.

Brander Matthews scolded him for quitting. Roosevelt answered on December 16, 1906, in a letter printed from his papers by Joseph Bucklin Bishop:

> "I could not by fighting have kept the new spelling in, and it was evidently worse than useless to go into an undignified contest when I was beaten. Do you know that the one word as to which I thought the new spelling was wrong — thru — was more responsible than anything else for our discomfiture? But I am mighty glad I did the thing anyhow. In my own correspondence I shall continue using the new spelling."

Losing didn't change his mind about the reform. Nine days after backing down, he wrote to the Board's secretary accepting membership on the Simplified Spelling Board.

## Legacy
The order is remembered as one of Roosevelt's few clear defeats, and it shows something real about the presidency. An executive instruction reaches only as far as Congress is willing to pay for it, and an order the legislature will not pay to carry out is no order at all.

The Printing Office's own record of the year is quiet about the defeat. Its annual report for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1907, prints no revocation and does not mention the episode, though its list of work done includes a second edition of *Simplified Spelling* in pamphlet, pocket and card editions. The Superintendent of Documents added that "the issue of pamphlets on simplified spelling gained wide notoriety and proved for us another means of materially increasing our sales."

The reform movement kept going for years. Carnegie stopped funding the Board in 1915, frustrated at how little his money had accomplished, and the Board carried on without him until about 1920. Carnegie died in August 1919.

The campaign left almost no trace in official American English. What survives are forms that were already winning before the Board existed, such as "honor," "color" and "center," plus the informal "thru" and "tho" on signs and in notes. Roosevelt kept his word to Matthews, and the White House said publicly, the day after the collapse, that he would go on using the spellings in his own letters. Writing to Hamlin Garland on November 23, 1907, almost a year later, he still spelled it "thru."

The episode lasts mainly as a human story about a president who cared enough about the small machinery of government to try to fix its spelling, and who took the ridicule with a grin. Most Roosevelt actions show how far the office could reach. This one shows where it stopped.

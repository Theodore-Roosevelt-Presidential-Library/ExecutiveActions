## At a glance
With the national forests reorganized, Roosevelt's executive orders returned to their usual mix of civil-service exceptions, land reservations and housekeeping. Threaded through the late summer of 1908, though, was a burst of wildlife protection: seven bird reservations in seven weeks. They included Klamath Lake on the Oregon–California line, which the Fish and Wildlife Service calls "the Nation's first waterfowl refuge," and Lake Malheur in eastern Oregon, today's Malheur National Wildlife Refuge.

## Background
Seven of these orders set land aside under the Department of Agriculture "as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds," each forbidding the taking of birds, eggs or nests:

- **Key West, Florida** (Executive Order 923, August 8)
- **Klamath Lake, Oregon and California** (Executive Order 924, August 8)
- **Lake Malheur, Oregon** (Executive Order 929, August 18)
- **Chase Lake, North Dakota** (Executive Order 932, August 28; its title says "Reserve," not "Preserve")
- **Pine Island, Florida** (Executive Order 939, September 15)
- **Palma Sola, Florida** (Executive Order 942, September 26)
- **Matlacha Pass, Florida** (Executive Order 943, September 26)

The orders share a form but are not identical. Executive Order 929 reserved "all smallest legal subdivisions which touch the shoreline of Lakes Malheur and Harney and the streams and waters connecting these lakes," together with the islands and unsurveyed lands inside the lakes' meander lines, "subject to valid existing rights." Its prohibition carries an exception:

> The taking or destruction of birds' eggs and nests, and the taking or killing of any species of native bird for any purpose whatsoever, except under such rules and regulations as may be prescribed by the Secretary of Agriculture, is prohibited, and warning is expressly given to all persons not to commit within the reserved territory any of the acts hereby enjoined. This reserve to be known as Lake Malheur Reservation.

The Klamath order has neither the "valid existing rights" clause nor that exception. It reserved lakes, islands and "marsh and swamp lands unsuitable for agricultural purposes." Neither order states an acreage. The government of the day did not count one either: a 1912 Biological Survey list notes that "as most of the reservations are unsurveyed, their area is uncertain." Later figures are reconstructions. For Klamath, the Fish and Wildlife Service's land records give 81,619 acres. For Malheur, a 1935 Supreme Court decision gives "81,786 acres of unsurveyed lands in Harney County, Oregon … within a meander line 105.36 miles in length."

Chase Lake carried the refuges into North Dakota to protect a large colony of American white pelicans. The three Florida reservations extended the Gulf Coast chain that had begun at Pelican Island in 1903 ([Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1)).

The rest of the run was routine:
- forest-boundary redefinitions for the Deschutes and Fremont in Oregon (912, 913)
- a further withdrawal inside the Gila National Forest, New Mexico, to protect the water supply of Fort Bayard (919)
- Alaska Signal Corps reservations at Tanana beside Fort Gibbon (920) and at Circle, for a wireless telegraph station (945)
- a Canal Zone penal-code amendment on grand larceny (928)
- civil-service reinstatements and appointment exceptions (910, 915, 916, 917, 931, 935, 940, 941, 944)
- a day off for federal employees on Labor Day (926)
- more time for the Spanish Treaty Claims Commission (921)
- a California lighthouse reservation (936)
- an agricultural-experiment reservation within the Belle Fourche irrigation project in South Dakota (934)
- an Interdepartmental Statistical Committee (937)

## Historical context
The bird reservations answered the same threat that had produced Pelican Island: market and plume hunters were stripping the country's waterbird colonies. In the Pacific Northwest the campaign had two drivers, the Oregon photographers and naturalists William L. Finley and Herman T. Bohlman. Traveling to Harney County in 1908 to photograph nesting white herons (now called great egrets) on Malheur Lake, they learned that most of the birds had been killed by plume hunters a decade earlier. They took the case to the Oregon Audubon Society, which was seeing the same losses at Klamath Marsh and pressed for both areas. Finley, as the society's president, carried the proposal to Roosevelt himself. Roosevelt already knew the two men from the Three Arch Rocks reservation of 1907 ([The Bird Reservations of Late 1907](story:95)). Finley later told the story of the plume trade in "The Trail of the Plume-Hunter," published in the *Atlantic Monthly* in 1910.

These were part of a larger series. The Biological Survey's own numbered list counts 51 bird reservations from Pelican Island in March 1903 to Bogoslof, Alaska, in March 1909, covering every reservation of Roosevelt's presidency; sixteen of them came in a single order of February 1909. Big-game preserves were counted separately. On that list, numbered in date order, Lake Malheur is No. 19 and Chase Lake No. 20. The Fish and Wildlife Service counts the same series as the 51 wildlife refuges Roosevelt created, the beginnings of what became the National Wildlife Refuge System.

## Political impact
The bird reservations drew little national press but real gratitude from the young Audubon societies that had asked for them. The orders banned taking native birds, eggs and nests, not hunting in general, and the Malheur order let the Secretary of Agriculture permit exceptions by regulation. No local opposition has been documented in 1908, 1909 or 1910. The first sign of friction comes in the Biological Survey's report for 1911, which notes that "various questions have arisen on Lake Malheur Reservation concerning the rights and privileges of residents on adjoining land — such questions as cutting hay between the meander line and the shore line, burning tules, grazing hogs on Pelican Island, destroying coyotes, and trapping fur-bearing animals." These were "so met as to maintain friendly relations with the residents."

The deeper conflict was over water, and it was written into the Klamath order from the start. The reservation "is made subject to and is not intended to interfere with the use of any part of the reserved area by the Reclamation Service acting under the provisions of the act approved June 17, 1902, or any subsequent legislation." The refuge yielded to reclamation in the very order that created it. What followed bore that out. The Klamath Water Users Association persuaded Woodrow Wilson to cut the Lower Klamath reservation in 1915. The Survey's reports of 1916, 1918 and 1919 record homestead applications rejected inside the boundary, organized movements "to drain the water from both the Klamath and Malheur reservations," and "deplorable conditions … on account of uncertainty concerning the status of certain lands." Oregon claimed title to Malheur Lake in 1916, a fight not settled until the Supreme Court ruled in 1935.

The routine personnel and land orders of the same weeks drew no notice at all.

## Legacy
The two Oregon reservations became cornerstones of the refuge system. Klamath Lake grew into the Lower Klamath National Wildlife Refuge, still described by the Fish and Wildlife Service as the nation's first waterfowl refuge, though that framing is the agency's: the 1908 order uses the same "native birds" language as every other order in the series. The refuge shrank sharply as reclamation drained much of Lower Klamath Lake. Four later executive orders, beginning in 1915, took it from about 81,619 acres down to 46,902. The refuge covers 50,092 acres today.

Lake Malheur became the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge, one of the largest freshwater marsh and wetland refuges in the West and a major stop on the Pacific Flyway. Today's refuge is more than twice what Roosevelt set aside: additions in 1935 and 1941 brought it to about 187,000 acres. In 2016 an armed group occupied its headquarters in a standoff over federal control of Western land, a modern flare-up of the federal-versus-local tension that has followed the reservation through much of its history.

Chase Lake remains a national wildlife refuge in North Dakota, 4,385 acres with a 4,155-acre wilderness designated in 1975. The Fish and Wildlife Service says it has at times hosted nearly a third of the continental white-pelican population. The executive order fixes its founding date at August 28, 1908.

Of the Florida reservations, Key West, Pine Island and Matlacha Pass survive in the J. N. "Ding" Darling refuge complex. Key West now covers about 209,000 acres, nearly all of it open water, taking in the Marquesas Keys and 13 other islands. Pine Island covers about 608 acres, and Matlacha Pass has, in the agency's words, "grown to 31 islands encompassing over 500 acres." Palma Sola did not last. It was renamed the Palma Sola National Wildlife Refuge in 1940 and revoked in 1948 by an Interior Department order that described "a small unsurveyed island in Palma Sola Bay" of "approximately two acres." That order gives no reason for the revocation.

The routine orders of these weeks left no independent legacy that has been traced.

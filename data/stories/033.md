## At a glance
In 1902, his first full year as President, Theodore Roosevelt signed nineteen proclamations that created or reshaped forest reserves across the West and Alaska. Thirteen set aside new reserves, from the San Isabel in Colorado to the Alexander Archipelago in Alaska; the rest enlarged, merged, or redrew existing ones. Little noticed at the time, they were the quiet opening of a forest program that grew enormously over his presidency.

## Background
Between April 11 and September 4, 1902, Roosevelt signed nineteen forest-reserve proclamations. Each withdrew described public land from sale and settlement, set it apart as a federal reservation, and warned settlers off, while protecting claims already lawfully on record. None handed the land to a new owner or made a park. The ground stayed public domain, now managed rather than sold.

**Thirteen new reserves:**
- **San Isabel**, Colorado (April 11, the first of the year)
- **Santa Rita, Santa Catalina, Mount Graham, and Chiricahua**, Territory of Arizona
- **Lincoln**, Territory of New Mexico
- **Niobrara and Dismal River**, Nebraska
- **Medicine Bow**, Wyoming
- **Little Belt Mountains, Madison, and Absaroka**, Montana
- **Alexander Archipelago**, Territory of Alaska

**The adjustments:**
- **San Francisco Mountains**, Arizona: a reserve McKinley had created in 1898, enlarged by "consolidating into one reserve" the existing reserves and nearby land.
- **White River**, Colorado: Benjamin Harrison's White River Plateau Timber Land Reserve of 1891, the second forest reserve ever created. Roosevelt's proclamation redrew its boundary, renamed it White River, and shrank it by roughly 227,000 acres.
- **Big Horn**, Wyoming: enlarged.
- **Yellowstone and Teton**: reorganized on May 22, and Yellowstone enlarged again three weeks later.
- **Medicine Bow**: boundary changed fifty-five days after the reserve was created.

Arizona, New Mexico, and Alaska were territories in 1902, and the proclamations say so. Arizona and New Mexico would not become states until 1912.

**Two laws, not one.** Most of the proclamations rested on section 24 of the act of March 3, 1891, a single sentence allowing the President to "set apart and reserve, in any State or Territory having public land bearing forests, in any part of the public lands wholly or in part covered with timber or undergrowth, whether of commercial value or not, as public reservations." That law only gave the power to *create*. Changing an existing reserve required the act of June 4, 1897, which let the President "at any time to modify any Executive order that has been or may hereafter be made establishing any forest reserve." Six of the nineteen proclamations cite that later act, and the Big Horn enlargement rests on it alone.

**Not quite a template.** The documents look alike but are not identical. The phrase "reserved from entry or settlement" appears in fifteen of the nineteen. The Alexander Archipelago proclamation warns people not to "unlawfully enter upon or occupy" the land rather than not to "make settlement upon" it. The San Isabel and Santa Rita proclamations warn settlers off "the tract of land reserved by this proclamation," in the singular, though they reserve "all those certain tracts, pieces or parcels"; the rest say "the lands." Even the countersignatures vary: David J. Hill, as Acting Secretary of State, signed the first two; John Hay signed Big Horn and White River; Alvey A. Adee signed the Alexander Archipelago.

The familiar captions, such as "Establishing the San Isabel Forest Reserve," are editorial headings added much later. In the *Statutes at Large* each is headed only "By the President of the United States of America. A Proclamation," and carries a number in the volume's own series. San Isabel, Proclamation 467 in the standard series, appears there as No. 13.

**Who ran the reserves.** In 1902 the reserves belonged to the General Land Office in the Interior Department, which managed them through its Division "R," set up on February 28, 1901 under Filibert Roth. Gifford Pinchot's Bureau of Forestry in the Agriculture Department had no authority over them. It could advise Interior, and only when asked. Pinchot's own *Report of the Forester for 1902* says its reserve work "has arisen from the request upon the Secretary of Agriculture from the Secretary of the Interior," and that the Nebraska nurseries would be set up "with the consent of the Secretary of the Interior." The reserves would not pass to Agriculture until the Transfer Act of February 1, 1905.

**Nebraska's experiment.** The Niobrara and Dismal River reserves had a different purpose from the rest. They grew from the idea that the Sandhills could be planted to trees. William L. Hall, Chief of the Section of Tree Planting, investigated the ground in 1901 and recommended to Pinchot that land be set aside for experimental planting, and on Pinchot's recommendation the two reserves were proclaimed. Charles Bessey, often credited with the idea, described that chain himself in a 1912 manuscript. According to the General Land Office, Dismal River covered 85,123 acres and Niobrara 123,779, together 208,902; the proclamations themselves give no acreage.

## Historical context
This was the utilitarian conservation of its day, not a modern love of wilderness. Forests on the public land protected the watersheds that fed western irrigation and held the timber a growing country would need. Left open to unrestricted entry, they invited the fraud and monopoly that reformers feared would let a few operators corner the public domain. The proclamations made the case in a phrase repeated seventeen times: "the public good would be promoted" by reserving the land. (The Big Horn and White River changes, made under the 1897 act, did not need it.)

Watershed protection was written into the law that governed the reserves. The 1897 act provided that no reserve "shall be established, except to improve and protect the forest within the reservation, or for the purpose of securing favorable conditions of water flows, and to furnish a continuous supply of timber for the use and necessities of citizens of the United States." That was general policy, not a reserve-by-reserve plan. The Reclamation Act was signed on June 17, 1902 ([The Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21)), but ten of these nineteen proclamations came after it, and none ties a reserve to a particular irrigation project.

Roosevelt was not the first President to use section 24 heavily. The power, later nicknamed the "Creative Act," had come in bursts. Harrison set aside roughly 13.4 million acres in fifteen reserves in under two years. Grover Cleveland reserved about 4.9 million acres in 1893, stopped for four years, and then on February 22, 1897 proclaimed thirteen reserves totaling 21,378,840 acres in a single day, the "Washington's Birthday Reserves." Congress suspended eleven of them within months. Roosevelt's first proclamation came about seven months into his presidency, and by September 1902 he had signed nineteen.

On May 22, 1902, the same day as three of these proclamations, Congress carved Crater Lake National Park out of the Cascade Range Forest Reserve. That was an act of Congress, not a proclamation.

## Political impact
One by one, these proclamations drew little national notice. A search of digitized 1902 newspapers in the states concerned, all of them well covered, turns up almost nothing. San Isabel, Santa Rita, Chiricahua, and Absaroka each made a single page of a single in-state paper. The Alexander Archipelago reached six pages in five papers in Alaska, Washington, and Oregon. Lincoln's twenty pages were all in New Mexico. The proclamations' own phrase "reserved from entry or settlement" appears on four newspaper pages in all of 1902. Papers almost never printed the texts.

That quiet should not be mistaken for a quiet subject. "Forest reserve" appears on thousands of newspaper pages in 1902, in every month of the year, and pages mentioning wool-growers alongside forest reserves were already turning up in January, before the first of these proclamations, mostly in the states about to receive them. Local stockmen, loggers, and homesteaders who lost open range or free timber grumbled, and some western papers and members of Congress were uneasy about a President reserving land on such a scale by his own signature. Those page counts are not the same as articles read, though, and how the reserve policy as a whole was received in 1902 is still an open question. The fair verdict is the narrow one: each of these proclamations was a lawful use of an existing statute that raised no recorded national controversy of its own.

**Inside the government.** The reserves changed hands within three years. In 1902 the General Land Office ran them, and Pinchot's Bureau of Forestry worked on them only when Interior asked; even the Nebraska nurseries needed "the consent of the Secretary of the Interior." The Transfer Act of February 1, 1905 moved the reserves to the Agriculture Department, and a separate appropriation act of March 3, 1905 gave Pinchot's bureau a new name, the Forest Service, from July 1 of that year. In the Sandhills, seed beds for the new nursery in the Dismal River reserve were laid out in the autumn of 1902, and the first field planting followed in 1903.

**Quick changes.** Some of the 1902 reserves did not keep their shape for long. The Absaroka reserve lasted just 147 days: on January 29, 1903, a new proclamation folded it, with the Teton reserve, into the Yellowstone and dropped its name ([Proclamation 496](doc:proc-496)). The Little Belt Mountains reserve was enlarged and renamed simply "Little Belt" on October 3, 1905 ([Proclamation 602](doc:proc-602)).

**The fight of 1907.** The open confrontation came five years later. The appropriation act of March 4, 1907 renamed the "forest reserves, which shall be known hereafter as national forests," and two pages further on carried the Fulton Amendment: "*Provided further*, That hereafter no forest reserve shall be created, nor shall any additions be made to one heretofore created within the limits of the States of Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado, or Wyoming, except by Act of Congress." Roosevelt answered with the "midnight forests," thirty-three proclamations covering 15,645,631 acres by the Forest Service's count ([The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)).

## Legacy
Nearly all of these reserves survive, though few carry the names Roosevelt gave them. In 1907 the forest reserves became national forests, and over the following decades they were enlarged, split, and recombined many times.

### Arizona
- **Santa Rita and Santa Catalina** were combined with the Dragoon reserve in 1908 by Executive Order 908 to form the new **Coronado National Forest**.
- **Chiricahua** joined Coronado in 1917, when Executive Order 2630, approved June 6 and effective July 1, transferred the whole Chiricahua National Forest to it.
- **San Francisco Mountains** became the core of the **Coconino National Forest** by a proclamation of July 1908. It was the only entire forest folded in; Coconino was assembled from four units, the others being parts of the Tonto, Black Mesa, and Grand Canyon forests.
- **Mount Graham** went partly into the **Crook National Forest** in 1908, and the rest returned to the public domain. When Crook was abolished and divided in 1953 by Public Land Order 924, its lands went to today's **Coronado, Gila, and Tonto** national forests.

### Alaska
The Alexander Archipelago did not simply grow into the Tongass. The **Tongass National Forest** was created separately on September 10, 1907, on a mainland tract of 2,262,624 acres. The Alexander Archipelago, 4,506,240 acres as proclaimed, was folded into it by Executive Order 908 on July 2, 1908, and its name was dropped, leaving a combined forest of 6,756,362 acres. A 1909 Roosevelt proclamation enlarging the Tongass recites that "an Executive Order dated July second, nineteen hundred and eight, consolidated the Alexander Archipelago and Tongass National Forests under the name of the Tongass National Forest." The Tongass is today the country's largest national forest, about 16.7 million acres by the Forest Service's 2025 assessment.

### Montana
- **Little Belt Mountains**, renamed Little Belt in 1905, was combined into the Jefferson National Forest in 1908; Executive Order 5834 of April 8, 1932 moved the whole Jefferson into the **Lewis and Clark**.
- **Madison** was divided three ways on December 16, 1931, by Executive Orders 5757, 5759, and 5760, among the Beaverhead, Deerlodge, and Gallatin forests. Its lands are today in the **Beaverhead-Deerlodge** and **Custer Gallatin**.
- **Absaroka** is the counterexample to any story of durability. The 1902 reserve was gone by January 1903. A different Absaroka National Forest, created in 1908 from part of the Yellowstone and all of the Crazy Mountain forest, was later divided between the Gallatin and the Lewis and Clark by Public Land Order 305 in 1945.

### Colorado, New Mexico, and Wyoming
- **San Isabel** still carries its own name, administered together with the Pike since 1975 as the **Pike-San Isabel**.
- **White River**, which Roosevelt renamed rather than created, is today's **White River National Forest**.
- **Lincoln**, in New Mexico, has kept its name since 1902.
- **Medicine Bow** was split in 1908. The Colorado portion became the Colorado National Forest in 1910 and was renamed the **Roosevelt** by Executive Order 5826 of March 28, 1932; it is now part of the **Arapaho and Roosevelt**. A Wyoming Medicine Bow was re-established in 1910 and has been administered with the Routt since 1995.

### Nebraska
Niobrara and Dismal River were combined with the North Platte reserve into the **Nebraska National Forest** in 1908. In 1971 Congress renamed the Niobrara division the **Samuel R. McKelvie National Forest**, a change of name only. The Dismal River tract is today the **Bessey Ranger District**, home of what the Forest Service calls "the oldest seedling nursery managed by the USDA Forest Service," and part of what it calls the largest hand-planted forest in the western hemisphere. The 2022 Bovee Fire burned roughly 5,000 acres of the district.

### The bigger picture
Pairings such as Pike-San Isabel, Medicine Bow-Routt, Custer Gallatin, and Helena–Lewis and Clark are administrative combinations: the forests within them remain legally separate national forests under one supervisor.

The 1902 proclamations are little remembered on their own, but together they mark the early, quieter phase of a program that grew enormously. By the Forest Service's own reports, the reserves totaled about 47 million acres in 1901 and more than 194 million acres gross by June 30, 1909, four months after Roosevelt left office, a figure that includes his large proclamations of February and March 1909. Roosevelt summed it up in his 1913 *Autobiography*: "The area of the National Forests had increased from 43 to 194 million acres."

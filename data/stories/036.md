## At a glance
The St. Louis World's Fair was supposed to open in 1903, the hundredth anniversary of the Louisiana Purchase. It wasn't ready, and neither were the nations and states that wanted to exhibit. Congress moved the date to 1904, and on July 1, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt issued this proclamation so that the whole world would "definitely and formally" know the new schedule. The delay it announced is also the reason the first Olympic Games held in the United States took place in Missouri.

## Background
Congress chartered the fair by an act of March 3, 1901, to celebrate the centennial of the 1803 Louisiana Purchase. The proclamation that first set its dates, opening by May 1, 1903, and closing by that December, was William McKinley's, issued August 20, 1901. McKinley signed that invitation to "all the nations of the earth" seventeen days before he was shot at Buffalo. Ten months later it fell to Roosevelt to move the date.

Organizers could not be ready in time. So Congress tucked a clause into the sundry civil appropriations act of June 28, 1902, that reset the calendar. The buildings would be *dedicated* by April 30, 1903, keeping the anniversary year, but the exposition would not open to visitors until as late as May 1, 1904, and would close by December 1 of that year.

Proclamation 480 does nothing more than announce that change "to the end that it may definitely and formally be known," putting the President's seal on the new dates so exhibitors and foreign governments could plan around them. It was countersigned by David J. Hill, Assistant Secretary of State, as Acting Secretary. Assistant secretaries signed proclamations that way often enough that nothing should be read into it, and why Hill signed rather than John Hay that day is not recorded.

One small wrinkle for careful readers: the proclamation says "section 8 of the Act of Congress approved June 28, 1902" fixed the new date. Strictly, the 1902 act amended section 8 of the 1901 chartering act, and it is that amended section that carries the new dates. The language the proclamation quotes is word-for-word accurate; only the citation is loose.

## Historical context
In 1902 a world's fair was a matter of national prestige, and getting one ready was a real logistical problem. St. Louis planned on an enormous scale: about 1,240 acres in and around Forest Park, nearly two square miles, with vast temporary "palaces" for art, industry and machinery. The *Official Guide* compared it with Chicago's 633 acres in 1893, Paris's 336 in 1900, Buffalo's 300 and Philadelphia's 236. It was the largest world's fair held to that date.

Foreign governments needed time to appropriate money, assemble displays and ship them across oceans, and so did the American states. The fair's president, David R. Francis, laid out four reasons for the delay in a statement of May 2, 1902: "the repeated requests of both domestic and foreign exhibitors for postponement"; "advices from foreign Governments, to the effect that they had not sufficient time in which to make preparations for a representative exhibit in 1903"; "the statement of Governor Taft that the Philippines would not be able to install their exhibits in a creditable manner if the Exposition should not be postponed to 1904"; and "the expressed fear of the Government architect that he could not procure material for the Government building by 1903." Participation was the heart of the argument.

The split written into the appropriations act, dedication in 1903 and opening in 1904, was the compromise: the centennial year would still get its ceremony, while the public exposition waited until everything was in place. A national celebration authorized by statute, paid for through appropriations and made official by presidential proclamation: the document shows the machinery of the period as much as its ambitions.

## Political impact
The proclamation itself stirred no controversy; it recorded a decision Congress had already made. It moved on the wire and ran in some eighteen papers from Missouri and New York to Nebraska, Utah, Alabama, Texas, North Dakota and Hawaii, usually in three sentences. The *St. Louis Republic* explained on July 4 why it surfaced late: "The proclamation for postponement of the World's Fair was signed July 1, but copies were not prepared for the public until to-day."

Exhibitors welcomed the extra year. The organizers had not always. On January 18, 1902, the Exposition Company's executive committee resolved unanimously that "the Exposition will open and close in the year 1903," and Francis wired that he "ha[d] not talked postponement nor admitted at all." They reversed course over the next three months. The real fight that followed was over money, not dates: a $4,500,000 federal loan passed the House 172–115 in February 1903 over what the commission's *Final Report* called "the most deep-seated, persistent opposition to granting another dollar to the fair."

The dedication went ahead as the law required. On April 30, 1903, Roosevelt spoke in person in the Liberal Arts Building, followed by Grover Cleveland, his only living predecessor. The *Final Report* records that "their addresses were listened to by 80,000 persons." Francis called the gathering to order, Cardinal James Gibbons gave the invocation, Thomas H. Carter spoke for the National Commission, and Francis presented the buildings before the President's dedication address and Cleveland's. Bishop E. R. Hendrix offered a prayer and the Rt. Rev. Henry C. Potter the benediction, and the day closed with a hundred-gun salute and a parade of 12,000 troops under Maj. Gen. Henry C. Corbin. Ambassadors and ministers of twenty-six governments sat on the stand, with representatives of more than forty states and territories. Members of the Cabinet and the Supreme Court had seats on the platform, though wire reports did not name them; that evening Secretary of War Elihu Root left with Roosevelt on his train, to ride as far as Topeka. Secretary of State John Hay was in Washington. The dedication ran three days: Centennial Day, Diplomatic Day and State Day.

## Legacy
### The fair
The Louisiana Purchase Exposition opened, as this proclamation set out, in 1904, and ran from April 30 to December 1. By the Exposition Company's own reckoning it was the largest world's fair held to that date. Roosevelt did not attend the opening. He pressed a gold key in the White House East Room at 1:13 that afternoon, answered by a twenty-one-gun salute from the Washington Monument grounds, while Secretary of War William Howard Taft represented the government in St. Louis. Roosevelt came on November 26, 1904, arriving before dawn with Edith and Alice, touring all day, reviewing the Philippine Scouts and Constabulary, and leaving just after midnight on the 28th. He signed the register "Theodore Roosevelt, the White House." The fair lives on in popular memory, not least through the 1944 film *Meet Me in St. Louis*.

### The Olympics
The postponement also reshaped Olympic history. The 1904 Games had been awarded to Chicago. After St. Louis moved its fair to 1904 and threatened rival games of its own, the International Olympic Committee shifted them to St. Louis by a postal vote of 14–2 in December 1902. They were the first Olympics held in the United States, and they were absorbed into the fair itself as Department P, Physical Culture. The *Official Guide* put it plainly: "By a decision of the International Olympic Committee all sports and competitions during the World's Fair are designated as Olympic events." The opening ceremony was May 14. Competition ran from July into late November, with the track-and-field week, the "Olympic Games proper," from August 29 to September 3. There was no closing ceremony, and the record keepers count the last day differently: Olympedia gives the competition dates as July 1 to November 26, and the IOC as July 1 to November 24.

### The Philippine Exposition
The fair is also remembered, in a more critical modern light, for its "anthropological" exhibits, which displayed Indigenous people in ways that reflected the era's imperial and racial assumptions. The largest was the Philippine Exposition: forty-seven acres, more than 1,100 people on the reservation and over a million dollars spent, with Visayan, Moro, Bagobo, Igorot, Negrito and Manguian groups on display alongside four hundred Philippine Scouts and the Philippine Constabulary. It was organized by the Philippine Exposition Board under Act 514 of the Philippine Commission, and the *Official Guide* says it was "planned by Secretary of War William H. Taft, when he was Civil Governor of the Islands." Collecting began in the islands in October 1902, and the reservation opened with the fair in 1904. Three days after this proclamation, Roosevelt's July 4, 1902, amnesty recited that the Philippine insurrection was at an end and peace established everywhere "except in the country inhabited by the Moro tribes, to which this proclamation does not apply" ([Pardon and Amnesty in the Philippines, July 4, 1902](story:34)). The fair's own guidebook noted that "it is against the Lanao Moros that General Leonard Wood is now engaged on a punitive expedition," while Lanao Moros were on exhibit in St. Louis. On August 12 and 13, 1904, the fair staged "Anthropology Days," in which people from the ethnological villages, Filipinos among them, were made to compete athletically against Olympic standards.

### The proclamation
Against all that, Proclamation 480 is a small procedural footnote. Its only work was to make the 1904 date official.

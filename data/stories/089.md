## At a glance
Between June 16 and August 17, 1906, Roosevelt signed forty-one executive orders, mostly the routine of a Washington summer: appointments, land-office business and the customary short Saturdays in the heat. A few did more. One put the American consular service on a merit footing. Another renamed the Board on Geographic Names and gave it a say over how the government drew its maps. Two others held public land in South Dakota and Wisconsin for farming experiments and forestry.

## Background
On August 10, 1906, Roosevelt signed a two-paragraph order. Executive Order 493 begins: "The official title of the United States Board on Geographic Names is changed to United States Geographic Board." It then reached past place names to the maps themselves. The board received "advisory powers … concerning the preparation of maps compiled, or to be compiled, in the various bureaus and offices of the Government, with a special view to the avoidance of unnecessary duplications of work; and for the unification and improvement of the scales of maps, of the symbols and conventions used upon them, and of the methods of representing relief." Important mapping projects were to be "submitted to this Board for advice before being undertaken."

The board dated from President Benjamin Harrison's order of September 4, 1890, which opens: "As it is desirable that uniform usage in regard to geographic nomenclature and orthography obtain throughout the Executive Departments of the Government … I hereby constitute a Board on Geographic Names." Roosevelt's 1906 order pushed it from standardizing names toward standardizing maps. (It had received other new duties in January; see [The Executive Orders of Early 1906](story:87).)

**A merit system for consuls.** Executive Order 469 of June 27 carried out the consular reorganization act of April 5, 1906. Vacancies above class 8 were to be filled by promotion for ability and efficiency. A Board of Examiners, drawn from the State Department and the Civil Service Commission, would give written tests on a modern language, the commercial resources of the United States, political economy, and international, commercial and maritime law. Candidates had to be 21 to 50 years old and score 80 out of 100. The order was blunt about politics: "neither in the designation for examination or certification or appointment will the political affiliations of the candidate be considered." Two orders of August 13 (EOs 495 and 496) revised the consular regulations and set a new schedule of consular fees, starting November 1, rounding out the package.

**Land for an experimental farm.** Executive Order 463 of June 19 reserved land within the Belle Fourche Irrigation Project in South Dakota for agricultural experiments. It was one of many withdrawals that went with the reclamation program begun under the [Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21). Belle Fourche had been authorized in 1904 and construction began in 1905, so the order came mid-build, two years before the first water reached the fields. The order's text has not been found. The Reclamation Service reported the next year that a quarter-section on the project "has been set aside for an experimental farm," where experiments would be run by the Department of Agriculture. That farm was most likely the land in question.

**Holding ground for Wisconsin's forests.** Executive Order 465 of June 22 withdrew unclaimed public land north of Township 33 from entry for ninety days. Despite its catalog title, it did not cover the whole state. The General Land Office applied it to "all of the unappropriated public lands in the Wausau land district north of township thirty-three." And it was a conservation measure. It held the land while Congress passed the act of June 27, 1906, which let Wisconsin select up to twenty thousand acres of public land for forestry. The timing was close: the land office had wired the Wausau office on June 21 to stop accepting entries.

**Other notable orders in the run:**

- **EO 461 (June 16)** ended the six-month limit on hiring temporary forest guards, a staffing step for a Forest Service only a year old.
- **EO 474 (June 30)** listed the Isthmian Canal commissioners by name with their salaries: Theodore P. Shonts as chairman at $30,000 a year; Charles E. Magoon at $17,500; Mordecai T. Endicott, Peter C. Hains and Benjamin M. Harrod at $7,500 each; and John F. Stevens with nothing beyond his pay as chief engineer. Shonts, Magoon and Stevens also got furnished houses on the Isthmus. The order carries a note on its timing: "(11.30 p. m.; after adjournment of Congress.)"
- **EO 477 (July 14)** adjusted the Fort Bayard, New Mexico, military reservation, leaving roughly 8,840 acres.
- **EO 483 (July 21)** took land from the Malabang military reservation on Mindanao for the town, "which town has been organized as a municipality."
- **EO 490 (August 2)** moved part of the El Reno land district in Oklahoma to the Lawton district.
- **EO 492 (August 8)** extended the Spanish Treaty Claims Commission to January 1, 1907.
- **EO 494 (August 13)** moved the Coeur d'Alene Indian Agency headquarters from DeSmet, Idaho, to Tekoa.
- **EO 479 (July 14)** classified four Smithsonian employees working on the International Catalogue of Scientific Literature.
- **Summer hours.** EO 462 shortened Saturdays at the Washington Navy Yard; EO 468 (June 25) extended that to all navy yards and naval stations; EO 466 covered the Government Printing Office, EO 470 War Department arsenals, depots and headquarters, and EO 484 the Engineer Department at Large. The first three paid work past four hours at 50 percent above normal; the last two did not. EO 485 was different: it gave the Office of Public Buildings and Grounds "the usual half holiday on Saturday during the months of July, August and September," except for the Park Police.

## Historical context
The geographic-board order belongs to the Progressive-era push for "national efficiency." The Geological Survey, the General Land Office, the Coast and Geodetic Survey and the Army engineers each made their own maps, on their own scales, with their own symbols. "Unnecessary duplications of work" made the case for coordination.

The consular reform answered a decades-old complaint from American exporters: consulships were patronage jobs handed to men who spoke no local language and knew nothing of trade, just as the country's export business was outgrowing the service meant to help it.

The Belle Fourche and Wausau orders show the administration's steady use of withdrawals to manage the public domain. The Wisconsin case is a reminder that conservation in these years was not only federal. The state was building its own forest reserve, and the President's withdrawal power held ground for a state program.

The summer-hours orders followed a long federal custom of shortening the Washington workday in the capital's punishing July and August heat.

## Political impact
Little of this summer's paperwork drew national notice. The consular reform was the exception, because it had a real constituency: chambers of commerce and export houses had pressed for a merit service for years. The Associated Press carried EO 469 the day it was issued. The Fresno *Evening Democrat* of June 30, 1906, ran it under the head "New Regulations for the Consular Service: A Re-organization of It on a Merit Basis and Divorced from Political Party Affiliations," describing it as "giving effect to the plans of Secretary Root."

The geographic-board renaming mattered mainly to the mapping bureaus and the geographers and surveyors who worked with them. It stirred no recorded controversy, and no California newspaper coverage of it has been found. The Belle Fourche reservation concerned South Dakota settlers watching the project take shape, and the Wausau withdrawal concerned settlers and timber interests in northern Wisconsin. A ninety-day hold drew none of the anger that permanent forest and coal withdrawals provoked, though local reaction in those states has not been studied. The military, land-district and summer-hours orders passed as routine.

## Legacy
The line from the 1906 board to today's is broken, not continuous. Franklin Roosevelt's Executive Order 6680 of April 17, 1934, ordered "that the United States Geographic Board be, and it is hereby, abolished," moving its work, staff and records to the Interior Department and revoking Harrison's 1890 order. The modern United States Board on Geographic Names was created by Congress in 1947 (Public Law 80-242) and still standardizes place names on federal maps.

The mapping power was the shorter-lived half. Woodrow Wilson's Executive Order 3206 of December 30, 1919, rescinded it and created a new Board of Surveys and Maps "to coordinate the activities of the various map-making agencies of the executive departments of the Government, to standardize results, and to avoid unnecessary duplication of work," Roosevelt's idea restated thirteen years later. That board was later renamed the Federal Board of Surveys and Maps.

The consular merit system outlasted both in practice. Amended many times, and by the act of May 21, 1908, it pointed toward the career Foreign Service the Rogers Act would build in 1924.

The Belle Fourche Project still runs. Through Belle Fourche Dam (formerly Orman Dam), a 185,200 acre-foot reservoir, 94 miles of canals and 450 miles of laterals, it waters 57,068 acres around Newell, Vale and Nisland in western South Dakota. The local irrigation district has operated it since 1949; the Bureau of Reclamation still owns it. The Wisconsin withdrawal lapsed on its own terms, but the twenty thousand acres it protected went to the state for forestry.

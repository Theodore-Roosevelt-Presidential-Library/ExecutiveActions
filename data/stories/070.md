## At a glance
Roosevelt's last two proclamations of 1907 could hardly be less alike. On May 27 he reserved a sixty-foot strip of public land along the Mexican border in California, Arizona, and New Mexico, to keep it "free from obstruction as a protection against the smuggling of goods." On October 25 he set apart Thursday, November 28 as Thanksgiving, with a text that warned "prosperity has been fraught with danger." The quiet customs strip, later called the "Roosevelt Reservation," has had a long and contested afterlife.

## Background
**The border strip.** Proclamation 758 reserved "all public lands within sixty feet of the international boundary" with Mexico, "within the State of California and the Territories of Arizona and New Mexico." It withdrew them from "entry, settlement or other form of appropriation under the public land laws" and set them apart "as a public reservation." Where a river formed the boundary, as the Colorado does for a stretch below Yuma, the strip ran sixty feet back from the river's margin. Secretary of State Elihu Root countersigned.

The reservation was hedged in three ways that are easy to miss:
- It reached only *public* land. Ground already in private hands, under a lawful entry or filing, or covered by a right of way of record was excepted.
- Lands already withdrawn for a purpose "to which this reservation for customs purposes is repugnant" were also excepted.
- The strip could be used "for public highways but for no other purpose whatever."

Texas is not named. The proclamation does not say why, but the reason is structural: Texas kept title to its public lands when it entered the Union in 1845, so there was no federal public domain on its stretch of the border to reserve.

**Thanksgiving.** Proclamation 776 set apart Thursday, November 28, 1907, the last Thursday of the month, as "a day of general thanksgiving and prayer." It was signed on October 25 and given to the press the next day. Its long central paragraph reads in part:

> "Ever thruout the ages, at all times and among all peoples, prosperity has been fraught with danger, and it behooves us to beseech the Giver of All Things that we may not fall into love of ease and of luxury; that we may not lose our sense of moral responsibility; that we may not forget our duty to God and to our neighbor."

"Thruout" and the closing "affixt" are not misprints. Both are on the Simplified Spelling Board's list of 300 words, which Roosevelt had ordered the Public Printer to adopt in August 1906. The House resolved on December 13, 1906 that government publications should follow "generally accepted dictionaries," and Roosevelt withdrew his printing order, yet ten months later he was still using reformed spelling in his proclamations.

## Historical context
The border strip was a small, practical piece of customs work, and the document calls it a "reservation for customs purposes." The aim was to keep homesteads, buildings, and fences off the line itself, across a long and mostly unfenced frontier, so that customs officers could patrol it. The Associated Press explained it in period terms: "Since the abolition of the old 'Zona Libre,' or the free zone between Mexico and the United States, it has been found increasingly difficult to prevent smuggling across the boundary, hence the proclamation" (*Los Angeles Herald*, May 31, 1907). A longer Los Angeles story a month later named the targets: stores and other buildings straddling the line, "with one end of the structure in the United States and the other in Mexico," which it called "harbors of refuge for contraband Chinese and storehouses of dutiable articles." Neither the proclamation nor the press names the department that asked for it.

The strip ran through country where "public lands" was not a simple category. In southern Arizona the line crossed the homeland of the Tohono O'odham, then usually called Papago. No reservation existed there in 1907; the one that now adjoins the border was created by executive order on February 1, 1917. The 1907 press framed the strip only as an anti-smuggling measure and did not mention the O'odham at all, and no O'odham response from 1907 is known.

Thanksgiving belonged to the annual line of proclamations unbroken since Lincoln's of 1863 ([Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20); [Thanksgiving Day, 1902](story:41)). This one's timing is striking. Roosevelt signed it in the opening days of the Panic of 1907; the Knickerbocker Trust Company had suspended payment on October 22, three days earlier. The proclamation says nothing of the financial crisis and speaks of a year "free from famine, from pestilence, from war," even as it warns that prosperity brings danger.

## Political impact
The border strip was reported as news, and favorably. The Associated Press story ran on May 31 in the *Los Angeles Herald* ("President to End Smuggling"), the Santa Barbara *Morning Press* ("President's Plan to Bag Smugglers"), the Riverside *Enterprise*, and the *San Bernardino Sun*, and a short item reached small weeklies such as the *King City Rustler*. The fullest account, "Strikes Blow at Smugglers," in the *San Pedro Daily News* (June 25) and the *Long Beach Telegram* (June 24), said smuggling "has suddenly become less attractive and less profitable to the lawless bands that for years have operated along the California, Arizona and New Mexico borders." It quoted "a United States secret service official just in from the vicinity of Nogales" that "the good effects of the innovation already are noticeable," and warned that all line-straddling buildings "now standing on public lands will have to be removed." No objection, from border merchants or anyone else, turned up in the California papers.

The Thanksgiving proclamation was printed widely, in full or nearly so. The Ventura *Morning Free Press* called it a "Characteristic Document of Nation's Executive," and the *Humboldt Times* ran it under the deck "Freedom from War, Famine and Pestilence and at Peace with All Mankind." Readers put its words to local use: a letter to the *Sacramento Union* on November 3, arguing for an ordinance to keep saloons out of the city's home districts, quoted the warning against "love of ease and of luxury" and the call for "manliness and rugged hardihood" as a "clarion call of the President." None of the coverage read connects the passage on prosperity to the Panic then under way on Wall Street.

## Legacy
The sixty-foot strip became known as the "Roosevelt Reservation," and Congress recognized it. The Act of July 28, 1937, extending the Papago Indian Reservation in Arizona, provided that the extension would not affect "the reservation of a strip of land sixty feet wide along the United States-Mexico boundary made by proclamation of the President dated May 27, 1907." A year after the Mexican strip, Roosevelt reserved a matching one along the Canadian border ([The Canadian-Frontier Strip](story:73)).

Because the strip is already federal land, it has figured prominently in border-barrier construction:
- **September 2019.** The Interior Department announced the transfer of about 560 acres of the reservation to the Army for three years, for some 70 miles of wall, in Yuma County, Arizona, the El Paso area, and San Diego County, California.
- **April 11, 2025.** A presidential memorandum directed that the Defense Department be given jurisdiction over federal border lands, "including the Roosevelt Reservation and excluding Federal Indian Reservations." In New Mexico, land including the strip became a National Defense Area about 170 miles long.
- **December 10, 2025.** Interior announced the transfer of roughly 760 acres in San Diego and Imperial Counties, California, to the Navy for three years, describing the land as "originally reserved in 1907 by President Theodore Roosevelt for border protection purposes."

No published total of how much of the strip is now under military jurisdiction has been found.

The 1907 proclamation also reached a federal courtroom. On August 14, 2026, in *Tohono O'odham Nation v. Mullin*, Judge Richard J. Leon of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia denied the Nation's request for a preliminary injunction against wall construction along about 62 miles of its border. He found that the Roosevelt Reservation "likely exists along the international border" there, relying on the 1937 act, a 2004 tribal resolution acknowledging the strip, and a 1934 Interior Solicitor's opinion, and he rejected the Nation's argument that aboriginal title meant its land was never "public land" in 1907. The ruling was preliminary, not a final judgment. A customs measure written to keep fences and shanties off the line has become part of present-day border policy and of an unresolved dispute over Native land.

The Thanksgiving proclamation simply joined the annual line. The date rested on custom until Franklin Roosevelt moved the holiday a week earlier in 1939–1941, and a joint resolution approved December 26, 1941 fixed it on the fourth Thursday of November. In 1907 the last Thursday and the fourth Thursday were the same day.

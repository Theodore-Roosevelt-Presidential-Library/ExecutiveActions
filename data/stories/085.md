## At a glance
These orders open Roosevelt's 1905, from the first week of January through the end of August. Most are the everyday upkeep of a growing government: one-name appointments, small civil-service amendments, and land-office housekeeping across the West. Two stand out. Executive Order 296 created a commission whose report led to the Naturalization Act of 1906. And Executive Order 296-A set aside four small islands in Stump Lake, North Dakota, as the nation's third federal bird reservation.

## Background
**The naturalization commission.** On March 1, 1905, Roosevelt ordered a commission formed to study how the United States made citizens. The order's own text has not turned up in print, but the commissioners described it in their letter of transmittal:

> By an Executive order, dated March 1, 1905, you directed that a commission be formed, to consist of an officer from the Department of State, one from the Department of Justice, and one from the Department of Commerce and Labor, to investigate and report to you upon the subject of naturalization in the United States. The order provided that such report should be submitted not later than the 15th of November, 1905, and that the Commission should prepare a draft of a proposed naturalization law. The undersigned were selected by the respective heads of their Departments.

So the order did not name the commissioners; the department heads chose them. They were Milton D. Purdy of the Department of Justice, chairman; Gaillard Hunt of the Department of State; and Richard K. Campbell of the Department of Commerce and Labor. They finished a week early, submitting their report on November 8, 1905. Roosevelt sent it to Congress with a two-line message:

> To the Senate and House of Representatives:
>
> I transmit herewith for the consideration of the Congress the report of the Commission on Naturalization, appointed by Executive order of March 1, 1905.
>
> THE WHITE HOUSE, December 5, 1905. THEODORE ROOSEVELT.

**Stump Lake.** Eight days later, on March 9, Executive Order 296-A reserved four small islands in Stump Lake, North Dakota, 27.39 acres in all, "for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." The order plots them lot by lot from a township survey approved just three weeks earlier: two islands in Section 10 of 12 and 7.64 acres, one in Section 11 of 2.22, and one in Section 15 of 5.53. It names no county, only Township 151 North, Range 61 West; the lake lies in Nelson County. It was the third federal bird reservation in the country, after [Pelican Island](story:1) in 1903 and [Breton Island](story:22) in October 1904, and the first in North Dakota. (Breton's founding order was unnumbered; a second Breton order, EO 369-A, came later in 1905, which can make Stump Lake look like the second if only the numbered series is consulted.) The Audubon association printed the Stump Lake order in its annual report.

**The rest of the half-year.** Most orders filled one job at a time, reinstating or appointing a clerk, copyist, watchman or special agent "without regard to the civil-service rules." Others moved classes of work into or out of the examined service: Alaska customs posts (291), Interior special agents and Indian-Service physicians (298–300), Post Office cashiers and finance clerks (313), Bureau of Immigration positions (318), War Department firemen (320). A sampler of the rest:

- **Laborers.** EO 290 (January 12) treated as classified those Washington laborers who had been reported in 1902 as doing work "of the grade performed by classified employees," and let them be promoted to clerical grade or transferred under the rules.
- **Panama and reclamation.** EO 289 excepted more Isthmian Canal positions from examination; EO 307 and 308 appointed named men to the Isthmian Canal Service; EO 321 moved a forest assistant into the Department of Agriculture's young forestry corps.
- **A board of architects.** EO 306 (March 14) created a Consultative Board of Architects for the public buildings of the District of Columbia: Bernard R. Green as chairman, with Daniel H. Burnham, Charles F. McKim, Augustus Saint-Gaudens and Frederick Law Olmsted, Jr. The members, it said, "will serve without compensation." EO 310 (March 18) then set their pay, and EO 311 (March 27) revoked that pay order. Roosevelt reversed himself twice in thirteen days and ended where he began. The board itself survived.
- **War claims.** The Spanish Treaty Claims Commission, settling claims left from the 1898 war, was extended twice (292 and 345, to March 2, 1906).
- **Land offices and reservations.** The lettered orders were mostly land business: General Land Office districts abolished or merged (Tucson to Phoenix, 341-D; Prescott to Phoenix, 342-A; Ashland and Eau Claire into Wausau, Wisconsin, 344-C) and small tracts reserved for a lighthouse depot on Revillagigedo Island, Alaska (343-C), the Santa Barbara Island light in California (347-B), the Pueblo of Santa Clara in New Mexico (344-B), and additions to the Uintah Indian Reservation in Utah to protect the Utes' water rights on Rock Creek (342-B, 343-A).

## Historical context
By 1905 the power to make citizens was scattered among thousands of courts, each with its own fees, procedures and certificates, and no one supervising them. The system invited fraud and made a person's citizenship hard to prove. The commission counted the courts: "a total of 5,160 courts naturalizing aliens in the United States, 157 of them being Federal courts and 5,003 being State courts." It documented the disorder and recommended a uniform, federally supervised process.

Around that one consequential order ran the business of a presidency stretched by two big new enterprises. The canal in Panama kept forcing a civil-service system built for Washington clerks to bend around a construction project in the tropics. The [Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21) was spinning up irrigation projects that the land-office orders quietly served, as districts were redrawn to keep pace with settlement. Roosevelt had cut his reform teeth as a Civil Service Commissioner from 1889 to 1895. Now he signed "without examination" orders and merit-system amendments in the same weeks: the two-handed work of a President who believed in the classified service but ran a government full of jobs it did not yet cover.

## Political impact
None of these orders became a public fight. California papers treated both the naturalization commission and the Panama exceptions as routine Washington news. An AP dispatch of March 10 reported that Roosevelt had before him reports of "many specific instances of gross fraud and error" under the naturalization laws, and had seen such evils himself "while connected with the municipal government of New York City." A second dispatch, on March 25, announced the three members and said the work would "commence at once." On the canal, Secretary Murphy of the Isthmian Canal Commission announced on January 7 that every position exempted from examination had been filled, and that further appointments would go through the Civil Service Commission. No editorial objection to either turned up. The personnel and land-office orders were argued over, if at all, inside the Civil Service Commission and the departments.

## Legacy
The half-year's lasting mark is the naturalization commission. Its report led directly to the Naturalization Act of June 29, 1906, which made the federal government the arbiter of naturalization and standardized the process and the certificate nationwide. The act did not create a new bureau; it renamed an existing one. In its own words: "That the designation of the Bureau of Immigration in the Department of Commerce and Labor is hereby changed to the 'Bureau of Immigration and Naturalization.'" That bureau is the institutional ancestor of today's citizenship service. Richard K. Campbell went on to head its Naturalization Division and served as Commissioner of Naturalization from 1913 to 1922.

Stump Lake's afterlife is stranger. The reservation endures as Stump Lake National Wildlife Refuge, managed from the Devils Lake Wetland Management District. But in the Fish and Wildlife Service's words, "in 1993, the levels of both Stump Lake and adjacent Devils Lake began to rise. By 2005, the Refuge islands were completely submerged and to this day are under 15 feet of water." It is a Roosevelt bird reservation still on the books, closed to the public and entirely under water.

The rest leave no independent legacy. The Panama and reclamation orders are early pieces of the personnel systems that built the canal and the irrigation projects, the land-office mergers are steps in the long redrawing of the public-land map, and the individual appointments fold back into the ordinary record of the service.

## At a glance
On June 2, 1905, Theodore Roosevelt declared the entire Wichita Forest Reserve in Oklahoma Territory a game preserve, where hunting game animals and birds on federal land became a crime. It is generally counted as the first national game preserve. Two years later, fifteen bison rode the rails from the Bronx to the preserve, an early, deliberate attempt to bring a nearly vanished species back to its old range.

## Background
The Wichita Forest Reserve, in the granite hills of southwestern Oklahoma Territory, had been set aside in 1901. On January 24, 1905, Congress passed "An Act for the protection of wild animals and birds in the Wichita Forest Reserve," letting the president mark off areas within it "for the protection of game animals and birds" to "be recognized as a breeding place therefor."

Roosevelt didn't pick out a corner. He took the whole reserve. Proclamation 563 declared that the Wichita Forest Reserve "is designated and set aside for the protection of game animals and birds, and shall be recognized as a breeding place therefor," and that hunting, trapping, killing, or capturing game on the federal lands inside it was unlawful except under rules set by the Secretary of Agriculture. Under the 1905 act, breaking those rules could bring a fine of up to one thousand dollars, a year in prison, or both.

Pelican Island in 1903 had set aside ground for birds ([Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1)). The Wichita did the same for big game. On May 29, 1906, Roosevelt enlarged the reserve's boundaries, and in 1907, when the forest reserves were renamed national forests, it became the Wichita Mountains National Forest and Game Preserve.

## Historical context
One animal explains why a big-game preserve seemed urgent in 1905. The American bison had once ranged the Great Plains in the tens of millions. By the 1880s and 1890s only a few hundred wild animals were left, and the great southern herd that once filled the country around the Wichita Mountains was gone entirely. To the conservation-minded people around Roosevelt, that collapse was the sharpest proof that the nation was squandering its wild capital. The frontier had been declared closed, resources were finite, and the next generation was owed something.

Roosevelt had hunted the plains before the herds disappeared, and he was a founder of the Boone and Crockett Club, the big-game hunters' organization that tied the sportsman's ethic to conservation. On December 8, 1905, fourteen men met in the Lion House of the New York Zoological Park and founded the American Bison Society. They elected the Park's director, William T. Hornaday, president, with Roosevelt as honorary president, a post he had accepted before the meeting was called. The Society's idea was bold for its day: don't just guard the last bison behind fences; put wild herds back on protected federal range. The new Wichita preserve, sitting on historic bison country, was the obvious place to try it first.

## Political impact
The proclamation itself caused little stir. Unlike the big forest withdrawals, it covered land the government had already reserved, in a lightly settled corner of a territory that was not yet a state, and it rested on a specific act of Congress rather than a disputed claim of presidential power.

Congress did put money behind it. The agricultural appropriations act of June 30, 1906 set aside $15,000 "for the erection of a wire fence and necessary sheds on the Wichita Forest and Game Preserve, to provide a range for a buffalo herd presented by the New York Zoological Society, and to provide for the maintenance of said herd." According to the American Bison Society's report, the money paid for a steel-wire fence seven feet six inches high, shelter sheds, a hay barn, flood-gates, and the first year's hay. Work began in late summer 1906. It was the first federal money spent developing land for wildlife.

The preserve became famous in the fall of 1907, when the plan was carried out in public view. Fifteen bison were crated at the Zoological Park on October 10, loaded into two express cars at Fordham Station, and sent off on October 11 on a journey of 1,858 miles. Frank Rush, the Forest Service guard chosen to manage the herd, rode with them along with H. R. Mitchell, the Zoological Park's chief clerk. The two cars split at St. Louis. Rush's car of seven reached Cache on a Wednesday afternoon, and those bison were in the corrals before midnight. The second car of eight arrived the next evening and went out by wagon the following morning.

The shipment is usually credited to the American Bison Society. The Society's own reports, and the 1906 statute itself, credit the New York Zoological Society. Hornaday led both organizations, which explains the mix-up. It is remembered as one of the first deliberate reintroductions of a native species to its former range anywhere in North America.

The arrival carried more weight than the logistics. Comanche people, who had lived by the bison before the herds were destroyed, came to see the animals come home. One eyewitness wrote that when the wagons were loaded at Cache, "the entire population of Cache turned out, together with a band of Comanche Indians, resplendent in their gayest clothes." The *Zoological Society Bulletin* printed a photograph of a Comanche man captioned "He saw Bison in the valley in his younger days," and noted that a Comanche band camped at the corrals on October 20. That scene, more than the signing in 1905, fixed the Wichita in the public mind as the place where Americans first tried not only to stop a species' destruction but to reverse it.

## Legacy
It worked. The herd took hold and grew, and the Wichita became a template for later big-game refuges, including the [National Bison Range](story:19) in Montana in 1908. The preserve Roosevelt proclaimed is today the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge, run by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. It protects 59,020 acres, about 90 square miles, of mixed-grass prairie and granite uplands. Descendants of that first bison herd still graze there, along with Rocky Mountain elk and Texas longhorn cattle kept as a living link to the region's ranching past.

The bigger legacy is a new idea of what conservation could do. Pelican Island and the forest reserves drew a line around something and said *leave it be*. The Wichita went further: a species all but erased could be deliberately brought back to the land it had been driven from. That idea is ordinary now. In 1905 it was close to untried, and the country's first federal game preserve is where it was first tested.

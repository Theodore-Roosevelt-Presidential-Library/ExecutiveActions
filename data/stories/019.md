## At a glance
On May 23, 1908, Theodore Roosevelt signed a law that set aside land on the Flathead Indian Reservation in western Montana as a permanent range for bison, paid the tribes for it, and fenced it. Private donors bought the herd, and thirty-seven bison went onto the range in October 1909. More than a century later, Congress returned the whole range to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes.

## Background
This was a law, not a proclamation. The bison-range provision is one paragraph tucked into the agricultural appropriations act for 1909, and Roosevelt's part was his signature. It directed the President to set aside "not to exceed twelve thousand eight hundred acres" of unallotted Flathead Reservation land "near the confluence of the Pend d'Oreille and Jocko rivers, for a permanent national bison range for the herd of bison to be presented by the American Bison Society." It appropriated $30,000 to pay the tribes the appraised value of the land and $10,000 for the Secretary of Agriculture to fence it and build sheds: $40,000 in all.

That was something new. At the [Wichita preserve](story:18), Congress had protected land the government already owned and then paid to fence it. Here, Congress had to buy land out of an Indian reservation before anything could be built. The 1906 Wichita money was the first federal spending to develop land for wildlife; the 1908 act was the first to buy it.

The country here isn't river bottom. It is an isolated block of native Palouse prairie standing roughly two thousand feet above the valley floor: bunchgrass and forbs on the open slopes, Douglas-fir and ponderosa pine higher up.

The deal had a catch that shows how it was supposed to work. The government would supply land and fence, but the animals had to come from private generosity. Note the words "to be presented": in May 1908 the American Bison Society hadn't yet raised the money or bought a single animal.

The 12,800-acre limit didn't last. An act of March 4, 1909 let the President enlarge the range to as much as twenty thousand acres, using leftover money from the original $40,000. The land actually reserved, by a schedule approved June 10, 1909, came to 18,523.85 acres.

## Historical context
This is the Wichita story carried one decisive step further. Within a single lifetime, tens of millions of bison had been cut down to a few hundred. Hide hunters, the railroads, and market slaughter all played a part, along with a federal policy that saw destroying the herds as a way to break the Plains nations who depended on them. To Roosevelt's circle, the near-loss of the continent's largest land animal was proof that a rich country was carelessly using up its wild capital.

The American Bison Society, founded December 8, 1905 in the Lion House of the New York Zoological Park, was the organized answer. William T. Hornaday was elected president, Ernest Harold Baynes secretary, Edmund Seymour treasurer, and Theodore Roosevelt honorary president, a post he held through his presidency. He had agreed ahead of time: the Society's first report records that "President Roosevelt was invited to become the Honorary President, and, on his acceptance, arrangements were made for organization."

The Wichita herd of 1907 is often credited to the Bison Society, but the Society's own reports give the credit to the New York Zoological Society, which Hornaday also led. What the Bison Society did do was push for a second, larger federal range farther north. At its first annual meeting, on January 10, 1907, it resolved to have the Flathead and Crow reservations examined. University of Montana biologist Morton J. Elrod made the inspection that year and recommended the Flathead site. The Society promised to raise about $10,000 for a founding herd if Congress provided the land, and it beat the goal, closing its campaign at $10,560.50 in May 1909.

## Political impact
As a wildlife measure, the range drew little of the anger aimed at the big forest withdrawals. It rested on an act of Congress, and a bison preserve in a quiet corner of Montana gave Western critics little to attack.

But the cost landed where conservation's costs often landed: on people with the least say. The range was carved out of the Flathead Reservation, homeland of the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes. Congress set aside $30,000; the appraisal came to $28,955.48, about $1.56 an acre, and that is what the Tribes were paid. The celebrations of the time left out a hard irony: the same animal whose destruction had been used to dispossess the Plains nations was being rescued on land taken from one of those nations, under a plan that gave the Tribes no role in running it.

### Stocking the range
Both halves of the bargain came together in 1909. The American Bison Society's treasurer, Clark Williams, certified the herd fund closed at $10,560.50 on May 5. On June 10 the President approved the schedule of land reserved under the enlargement act. In September, after an inspection of the C. E. Conrad estate herd at Kalispell, the Society agreed on a price of $275 a head, delivered to the range near Ravalli.

The stock train reached the siding at Ravalli on October 16, 1909, and the next day, October 17, thirty-seven bison went onto the fenced range. The Society had bought thirty-four of them, twelve bulls and twenty-two cows, each crated separately and hauled from Ravalli. The Conrad estate donated two more, including the bull Kalispell Chief, and Charles Goodnight of Texas gave a cow, the surviving half of a pair he had offered. The purchase left only $724.75 in the Society's bison fund by January 1, 1910.

Other promised animals came later or not at all. Three bison from the Blue Mountain Forest Association arrived on November 18, 1910. The animals James J. Hill had pledged never came; he wrote in September 1910 that he had had them killed because they had become too much trouble to keep.

## Legacy
### A herd that held
The herd took hold and became one of the anchor herds that pulled the plains bison back from the edge. From 1933 to 1959 the range was home to Big Medicine, a white bison, born May 3, 1933, who died August 25, 1959. He was leucistic rather than albino, with blue eyes and a dark brown topknot. Bob Scriver mounted his hide for the Montana Historical Society, where it was dedicated in 1961 and is still on view. In October 2022 the Society's trustees transferred ownership to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, and Big Medicine remains there on loan until the Tribes choose a permanent home for him.

### The price, revisited
The Tribes challenged the 1908 price for generations, and they won. Under a special 1946 law that let them sue, the case went to the U.S. Court of Claims. In *Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes v. United States*, decided January 22, 1971, the court held that in reserving the land "for 'just the figure Congress wanted to pay,'" the government "was exercising its power of eminent domain," and that the bison-range acres "were taken by defendant, within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment." The court's judgment, $6,066,668.78 plus interest, covered all 485,171.31 acres disposed of under the 1904 allotment act. The bison range was one parcel among settlers' patents, state school lands and other reserves, and the court said the record allowed no reasonable way to split out a value for each kind of land. The Tribes themselves have calculated the bison range's share at about $231,548, or roughly $14 an acre.

### The land goes home
For more than a century the range was part of the National Wildlife Refuge System. Then the land went home. The Montana Water Rights Protection Act, enacted December 27, 2020, provides that "all land comprising the National Bison Range (including all natural resources, interests, and appurtenances of that land) shall be held in trust by the United States for the benefit of the Tribes." The transfer took effect that day. Interior issued its recording directives on January 15, 2021; a Bureau of Indian Affairs notice of June 24, 2021 confirmed the transfer and gave the acreage as 18,800.22; and the Tribes took over full day-to-day management on January 2, 2022. The same law repealed the 1908 provision, and the 1909 enlargement language was dropped from the U.S. Code as well.

Today it is the CSKT Bison Range, tribally owned and tribally managed. Congress restored all of it, every acre, by statute. It isn't the only refuge land returned to a tribe: in February 2025 the Fish and Wildlife Service completed the transfer of 680 acres of the White Horse Hill National Game Preserve in North Dakota into trust for the Spirit Lake Nation, land Roosevelt himself had carved out of that tribe's treaty territory in 1905. Congress didn't call the Bison Range transfer a first. It called the circumstances "exceptional" and wrote a "No Precedent" clause into the law.

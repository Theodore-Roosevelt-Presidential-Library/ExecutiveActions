## At a glance
In 1904 Roosevelt signed two short proclamations that freed small parcels of federal land for town parks in Oklahoma Territory. At Lawton, 120 acres once held for the Fort Sill Indian boarding school were opened for the city to buy. At Woodward, a four-acre lot set aside for a courthouse went to the town instead. Each answered a local request, and each needed the President's signature because a presidential proclamation had reserved the land in the first place.

## Background
Both proclamations do the same modest thing. They release a small tract the government had set aside for one public purpose, once that purpose had lapsed, so a growing town could use it for a park. Each names who asked.

**Lawton (Proclamation 520, March 29, 1904).** In the 1901 opening of the Kiowa, Comanche, Apache and Wichita lands, 120 acres adjoining the new town of Lawton had been reserved "for the use of the Fort Sill Indian boarding school of Kiowa agency." By 1904 the school no longer needed the land, and "the city authorities of said city desire to make entry thereof for park purposes." The proclamation restored it to the public domain, "to be disposed of to said city, for park purposes."

The law Lawton would use was the act of September 30, 1890, a short statute letting incorporated cities and towns "purchase for cemetery and park purposes not exceeding one-quarter section of public lands not reserved for public use." Two things follow. The words "not reserved for public use" are why the proclamation was needed at all: while the 120 acres stood reserved for the school, Lawton could not enter them at any price. And Lawton was to *buy* the land, not receive it as a gift. At 120 acres, the tract fit under the act's 160-acre ceiling.

**Woodward (Proclamation 532, October 13, 1904).** When President Cleveland opened the Cherokee Outlet in 1893, he reserved a townsite for the seat of County "N," now Woodward County, "excepting one acre reserved for Government use for the site of a land-office, and four acres to be reserved for the site of a court-house, which tracts are to be contiguous." That four-acre "Court House Reserve," lot one of block forty-eight, sat beside the federal land-office acre in the planned civic center of town. By 1904 the county commissioners had given up their claim and "consented and recommended that the same be patented to the 'Town of Woodward' for use as a public park." This parcel went under a true townsite law, section four of the act of May 14, 1890, which allowed unsold lots to be "reserved for public use as sites for public buildings, or for the purpose of parks," with the Secretary of the Interior to "execute proper conveyances."

Both proclamations were countersigned by Secretary of State John Hay. The Lawton proclamation is signed out in full; the Woodward one is signed "T. Roosevelt."

## Historical context
Oklahoma Territory was a patchwork of lands opened to settlers piece by piece since 1889. The land runs and lotteries had platted new towns almost overnight and scattered federal reservations across the map: agency and school tracts, land-office and courthouse lots, school sections. As towns grew and the original need passed, these parcels had to be released one at a time before anyone could take title. Lawton, laid out for the Kiowa-Comanche-Apache opening of August 1901, was not yet three years old. Woodward, the County N seat from the 1893 Cherokee Outlet opening, was eleven.

**Why the President?** Neither law behind the original openings actually gave the President a power to *restore* land. The 1893 act ratifying the Cherokee Outlet agreement let him "make such other reservations of lands for public purposes as he may deem wise and desirable" in his opening proclamation. The 1900 act for the Kiowa, Comanche and Apache lands simply authorized an opening by proclamation; the school land had been kept out by McKinley's blanket exception for land "used, occupied, or set apart for military, agency, school … or other public uses." Both 1904 proclamations rest on an inference, that a reservation made by presidential proclamation could only be undone by another. The Interior Department evidently treated that as settled: Roosevelt had done the same for Anadarko's park in Proclamation 478 in June 1902 and for Lawton's cemetery in Proclamation 492 that September.

The Lawton tract also sat on contested ground. The Kiowa-Comanche-Apache cession was the subject of *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock*, in which the Supreme Court held on January 5, 1903, that Congress could dispose of the tribes' lands without the three-fourths consent the Medicine Lodge treaty required. By March 1904 the question was closed in law. The land this proclamation restored to the public domain was land the Kiowa had gone to court to keep.

## Political impact
As presidential documents these were uncommon. Volume 33 of the *Statutes at Large* prints forty proclamations, from March 1903 to March 1905, and only these two restore a single small tract to a named town. The others in the volume that return land to the public domain do so wholesale: forest-reserve eliminations, further Cheyenne and Arapaho lands opened to settlement, and the release of a 160-acre St. John's Mission School tract on the Cheyenne River lands in South Dakota (see [The Sioux Land Openings of 1904](story:51)). The General Land Office restored withdrawn land by departmental order all the time. Restoration by proclamation, as here, happened where an opening proclamation had made the reservation in the first place.

For the two towns the effects were practical. Lawton could now apply to buy the 120 acres under the cemetery-and-park law, which it could not do while the land stood reserved for the school. Woodward's former courthouse lot was to be patented to the town for a public park, with the Secretary of the Interior executing the conveyance.

Nothing in either document hints at local controversy. Woodward's grant came on the county commissioners' own relinquishment and recommendation, Lawton's on the city's request. Whether anyone beyond the two towns took notice in 1904 has not been established; a search of Colorado newspapers for the spring of 1904 found no notice of the Lawton grant, and the Oklahoma Territory papers themselves have not yet been read. If an objection ever surfaces at Lawton, the likelier source is not the settlers but the Kiowa, Comanche and Apache, whose school land this had been.

## Legacy
These grants carry no national legacy of their own. Each was meant to give a young Oklahoma town a park: Lawton, near Fort Sill in the former Kiowa-Comanche-Apache country, and Woodward, in the old Cherokee Outlet. The Lawton grant is a close sibling of [Restoring Fort Sill Indian Sub-Agency Lands to Lawton for a Cemetery](story:40), which restored a forty-acre Fort Sill sub-agency tract to the city in September 1902, and of the Anadarko park grant of June 1902 told in [The Public-Domain Restorations of 1902](story:37).

What became of the parcels is still unknown. On today's federal survey maps, the 120 acres of Proclamation 520, in the northwest quarter of section 32, township 2 north, range 11 west, lie on the southeast side of central Lawton, in an area now mapped as residential streets such as SE B Avenue and SE Wallock Street, with a small city park, Vernon Park, just north of the section line. Whether Lawton ever completed the purchase and used the tract as a park, and when it gave it up, has not been established. Woodward's lot one, block forty-eight, has not yet been located on a modern plat.

## At a glance
In June 1907 Roosevelt amended the civil-service rules so that federal employees hired on merit, while keeping "the right to vote as they please," would "take no active part in political management or in political campaigns." He had written that formula himself as a Civil Service Commissioner in 1894. The 1907 amendment put the Commission in charge of enforcing it. Congress later wrote the rule into the Hatch Act, and the Supreme Court upheld it twice.

## Background
Executive Order 642 of June 3, 1907 amended Section 1 of Civil Service Rule I. Twelve days later, Executive Order 655 combined its two paragraphs into one. The Supreme Court later described it as "amended to consolidate without changing wording." As the Civil Service Commission printed it in its *Twenty-fourth Annual Report*, the operative text reads:

> No person in the Executive civil service shall use his official authority or influence for the purpose of interfering with an election or affecting the result thereof. Persons who, by the provisions of these rules are in the competitive classified service, while retaining the right to vote as they please and to express privately their opinions on all political subjects, shall take no active part in political management or in political campaigns.

The rule applied only to the *competitive* service, meaning positions filled by examination. It did not cover the wider "classified" service, which included jobs exempt from examination. Newspapers noticed the limit. The New-York *Tribune*'s wire story of June 6 said the order "does not cover postmasters, marshals and others not in the classified service."

Older laws already protected federal workers' paychecks from the parties. An act of August 15, 1876 barred federal employees from requesting, giving or receiving money from one another for political purposes. The Pendleton Act of 1883 went further. It barred officials and Members of Congress from soliciting political contributions from federal employees, and it barred anyone from collecting such money in a federal office, navy yard, fort or arsenal. In Executive Order 193 of October 18, 1902, Roosevelt reaffirmed those rules after the Republican state committees of Pennsylvania and Ohio mailed campaign-fund circulars to federal employees. He reminded every officer that "any official in the Federal service may, without jeopardy to his official standing, contribute or not, exactly as he pleases." The 1907 rule extended the protection from an employee's money to an employee's partisan work.

## Historical context
Few people knew the spoils system better than Roosevelt, and few disliked it more. From 1889 to 1895 he served as a U.S. Civil Service Commissioner under presidents of both parties and became nationally known for enforcing the merit system against patronage politicians in each. Under the spoils system, the government payroll doubled as a party army. Employees were expected to canvass, electioneer and hand back part of their pay at campaign time, and refusing could cost them their jobs. Separating the civil service from that machinery was meant to help two groups: the public, which would get an administration working for it rather than for the party in power, and the employees, who would no longer be shaken down.

The 1907 wording wasn't new. Its first sentence was already Rule I, section 1 of Executive Order 209 of March 20, 1903, which descended from the original rule President Arthur issued in 1883. The second sentence was Roosevelt's own. As a commissioner he drafted a paragraph headed "Offensive Partisanship" for the Commission's *Eleventh Report* (1894), and it identified exactly the problem the 1907 amendment would address: "The rules against offensive partisanship are usually strictly construed against the opponents of the party in power, and leniently against their adherents." It went on:

> "A man in the classified service has an entire right to vote as he pleases, and to express privately his opinions on all political subjects; but he should not take any active part in political management or in political campaigns, for precisely the same reasons that a judge, an army officer, a regular soldier, or a policeman is debarred from taking such active part. It is no hardship to a man to require this."

As president he quoted that passage again. In a letter to the Commission dated June 13, 1902, he wrote that "the man in the classified service, while retaining his right to vote as he pleases and to express privately his opinions on all political subjects, 'should not take any active part in political management or in political campaigns …'" He explained where the language came from: "in the Eleventh Report of the Commission I personally drew up the paragraph which you quote."

So the 1907 amendment didn't invent the rule. It made the rule enforceable. The Commission said so directly. Department heads had been left to apply the rule themselves, and "the adherents of the party in power were often allowed to take a part in politics which was not allowed to the adherents of the party out of power." The amendment "put the matter under the control of the Commission." The *Tribune* added that it came "on the recommendation of the commission" after "numerous complaints from various parts of the country, especially from North Carolina."

Roosevelt grew the merit system throughout his presidency. The classified service went from 108,967 of 235,766 positions in 1901 to about 220,000 of 352,104 by the time he left office, according to figures Joseph H. Choate gave in 1908. The Commission's own count for June 30, 1908 was lower: 206,637 positions subject to competitive examination. Much of that growth came from the government getting larger, not from presidential orders.

## Political impact
The rule passed with very little notice. Unlike the forest withdrawals, it set off no regional backlash, and it drew none of the mockery the spelling order got ([The Simplified-Spelling Order, 1906](story:15)). A single short wire story, datelined Washington, June 5, ran as filler on inside pages for about two weeks. The New-York *Tribune* of June 6 headed it "WARNED BY PRESIDENT. / Persons in Classified Service Must Not Be Active in Politics." The Wilmington *Evening Journal* of June 8 headed it "THE USUAL WARNING," and the headline says a good deal. No editorials, partisan attacks, employee protests or congressional complaints have turned up. Congress wasn't in session; the 60th Congress didn't convene until December 2, 1907.

The coverage that followed was about compliance, not resistance. On July 7 the *San Francisco Call* ran the headline "FEDERAL EMPLOYES MUST GET OUT OF POLITICS" and reported that "several employes who had positions as chairmen or secretaries of republican and Roosevelt clubs tendered their resignations." On July 17 the *Washington Times* reported that the Department of Commerce and Labor was sending the text to its staff and collecting signed acknowledgments. The real change was who enforced the rule. Department heads had applied it unevenly, often more strictly to the party out of power. From June 1907 the Civil Service Commission was in charge.

There was a real tension underneath. A government employee is also a citizen, and barring citizens from campaigning sits awkwardly with ordinary political freedom. Roosevelt and the reformers answered that the rule restricted only public partisan activity, not private belief or the vote, and only as a condition of holding a public job. Patronage politicians in both parties had always opposed the merit system because it took away jobs they could hand out and workers they could command, and this rule took away one more lever. Their resistance was constant in the civil-service fights of the period, but it never came to a head over this rule, and the order stood.

## Legacy
The rule has outlived almost every other Roosevelt executive action, though its history hasn't been a straight line.

### The Hatch Act
The Hatch Act of August 2, 1939 carried the title "An Act to Prevent Pernicious Political Activities." The phrase goes back to Grover Cleveland's message of July 14, 1886, which warned that officeholders' "duty to party" must not be "increased to pernicious activity by office holding." Section 9(a) of the act took Rule I nearly whole:

> "It shall be unlawful for any person employed in the executive branch of the Federal Government … to use his official authority or influence for the purpose of interfering with an election or affecting the result thereof. No officer or employee in the executive branch … shall take any active part in political management or in political campaigns. All such persons shall retain the right to vote as they may choose and to express their opinions on all political subjects."

The first sentence matches Rule I word for word. The second keeps the "active part" formula, adds the word "any," and applies it to nearly the whole executive branch instead of only the competitive service. Congress dropped the word "privately" from the right to express opinions, and the civil-service rules dropped it too on March 5, 1941. Amendments in 1940 extended the restrictions to state and local officers paid with federal funds, and the Supreme Court upheld that extension in *Oklahoma v. Civil Service Commission* (1947).

The 1940 amendments also did something quieter. When Congress had to define "active part in political management or in a political campaign," it didn't write its own definition. It pointed back to the acts "prohibited for employees of the competitive service before July 19, 1940, by determinations of the Civil Service Commission under the rules prescribed by the President." That cross-reference is still in the U.S. Code.

### In the courts
The Supreme Court upheld the law twice, quoting the 1907 text both times. *United Public Workers v. Mitchell* (1947) cited Executive Order 642 and its consolidation as Executive Order 655. *U.S. Civil Service Commission v. National Association of Letter Carriers* (1973) built its historical argument on Rule I and reversed a lower court that had held the Hatch Act unconstitutional. The same day, in *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, the Court upheld a similar state law. The Court hasn't taken up the basic constitutional question since 1973. Hatch Act disputes today are enforcement matters before the Office of Special Counsel and the Merit Systems Protection Board.

### After 1993
The Hatch Act Reform Amendments of 1993 reversed the general rule. Most federal employees today "may take an active part in political management or in political campaigns." Four limits remain: they cannot use official authority to affect an election, solicit or receive political contributions, run for partisan office, or solicit or discourage the political activity of anyone with business before their office. They also cannot do political work on duty, on federal premises, in uniform or in a government vehicle.

Roosevelt's second sentence still applies in full only to a list of "further restricted" employees set out in the law. The list includes the FBI, the Secret Service, the CIA, NSA, DIA and NGA, the National Security Council, the Election Assistance Commission and the Federal Election Commission, the Merit Systems Protection Board, the Office of Special Counsel, IRS Criminal Investigation, career members of the Senior Executive Service and administrative law judges, among others. For them, the meaning of "active part in political management" still comes from rulings the Civil Service Commission made under Rule I after 1907.

The rule's first sentence has never lapsed for anyone. The ban on using official authority to sway an election still binds every federal employee, and together with the on-duty and fundraising limits, it is the part of 1907 an ordinary federal worker still feels directly.

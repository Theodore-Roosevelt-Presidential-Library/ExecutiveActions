## At a glance
In 1908, Roosevelt's last full year in office, he signed twenty-one proclamations that reshaped the national forests. Four created new forests from unclaimed public land: the Ozark in Arkansas, the Ocala and Choctawhatchee in Florida, and the Dakota in the Little Missouri country south of Medora, North Dakota. The rest enlarged, trimmed, renamed, or merged existing forests, nearly all in states where Congress still let the President act alone. One featured proclamation turned the Garden City forest into the Kansas National Forest, a bet that trees could be planted on open range.

## Background
These proclamations are the ordinary paperwork of running the national forests. None founded the system or fought over it; they trimmed, merged, and tidied it. By 1908 the reserves were called "national forests" and were run by the Forest Service in the Agriculture Department under Gifford Pinchot, the Forester. Surveying, entry, and patenting of the land stayed with the Interior Department's General Land Office.

What the twenty-one did:
- **Four new forests** from unreserved public land, each reciting that the lands "are in part covered with timber" and setting them apart "for the use and benefit of the people": **Ozark**, Arkansas (March 6); **Ocala**, Florida (November 24); **Dakota**, North Dakota (November 24); and **Choctawhatchee**, Florida (November 27). These four rest on section 24 of the Act of March 3, 1891.
- **Five consolidations** that built new or reshaped forests mostly out of old ones, adding some new land: **Gila**, absorbing the Big Burros; **Datil**, carved from the old Gila; **Crook**, from part of the Tonto and most of the Mount Graham; **Klamath**, from parts of the Klamath, Trinity, and Shasta; and **Coconino**, from all of the San Francisco Mountains forest and parts of the Black Mesa, Tonto, and Grand Canyon forests. The Coconino proclamation added that "the remaining portion of the Grand Canyon National Forest … shall continue reserved and constitute the Kaibab National Forest."
- **Eight enlargements**: San Bernardino, Monterey, Tonto (which also swallowed the small Pinal Mountains forest), Trinity, Modoc, Jemez, Kansas, and Uinta.
- **Three that both added and excluded land**: Manzano (which also absorbed the Mount Taylor forest), Sierra (adding land in California and Nevada, excluding some in California), and Dixie (adding in Arizona, excluding in Utah).
- **One pure reduction**: Blue Mountains, Oregon.

Seventeen of the twenty-one rest on the Sundry Civil Act of June 4, 1897 alone, including every consolidation. The new-named Datil, Crook, Coconino, and Klamath forests were made under the power to "modify" existing reserves, not the power to create. Each proclamation carries the seal; Secretary of State Elihu Root countersigned fifteen, and Acting Secretaries Robert Bacon and Alvey A. Adee the rest.

**The featured proclamation.** Proclamation 808 of May 15, 1908 renamed the Garden City National Forest, set apart in 1905, as the Kansas National Forest and added "certain lands, within the State of Kansas, which are in part covered with timber or undergrowth." It brought the forest to 302,387 acres in five southwestern Kansas counties (Finney, Kearny, Hamilton, Grant, and Haskell), by the Kansas Historical Society's count. Its boundary is drawn on a diagram rather than written out.

**Who asked for them.** The documents do not say. Every one recites only that "it appears that the public good would be promoted," and none names a petitioner. For the Dakota forest, the sporting weekly *Forest and Stream* (December 12, 1908) reported from Washington that "Governor Burke, of North Dakota, is very much interested in this forest and thinks it will be of inestimable value to the people who live in the region." That shows interest; whether he formally asked for it is not known.

## Historical context
Where these forests fall on the map tells the story. In March 1907 Senator Charles W. Fulton of Oregon had won a proviso barring any new forest reserve, or any addition to one, in Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado, and Wyoming except by act of Congress. Roosevelt answered with the "midnight forests" proclaimed just before he signed ([The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)). After that, his power to create or enlarge by proclamation was confined to other states. The 1908 list shows it plainly: the new and enlarged forests sit in California, Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, Nevada, Arkansas, Kansas, Florida, and North Dakota. The one Oregon item, the Blue Mountains proclamation, only shrinks a forest, which the proviso did not forbid.

The proclamations were also one piece of a larger reorganization, most of it done by executive order. A wave of orders dated May 26 through July 2, 1908, nearly all effective July 1, redrew forests across the West. The Blue Mountains forest, six months after its proclamation trimmed it, was divided by orders of June 13 among the new Whitman, Malheur, Umatilla, and Deschutes forests. Inside the Fulton states, an order that only shuffled land already reserved among new names added nothing and needed no proclamation. The proclamations are the pieces that fell outside those states and also took in new land, which is why so many of them consolidate. The Forester's report for 1908 laid out the plan in a few lines. A redistricting of the national forests had been drawn up to take effect July 1, 1908. At the close of the fiscal year, 119 supervisors were in charge of 188 units made up of 171 forests; after the redistricting, 140 supervisors would run 143 units, each one a single forest. The number of forests went down, not up. The goal was to make each national forest a single unit under its own supervisor.

A few of the year's forests were experiments of another kind:
- **Kansas.** The Garden City reserve lay south of the Arkansas River, southwest of Garden City. Planting began in 1906, with 50,000 yellow pines in the ground by that April. It was an attempt to grow a forest on open range.
- **Dakota.** The Dakota forest lay in the Little Missouri country of present-day Slope County, south of Medora, about 14,000 acres of native ponderosa pine. *Forest and Stream* gave the Service's reasons: it secured "an experimental field for forest planting" in "the least forested State in the Union," and its remaining timber "should be conserved exclusively for the use of the home builder." Planting followed on native pine ground. The forester K. D. Swan later recalled riding out from Bowman in April 1912 to plant ponderosa pine, with stock from the Savenac Nursery in Montana, on sites where pines already grew among the scoria buttes. The forest was run from Camp Crook, South Dakota.
- **Florida and Arkansas.** The Ocala's sand pine and longleaf, Choctawhatchee's coastal pine, and the Ozark reserved public-domain timber in the South years before the Weeks Act of 1911 let the government buy eastern forestland. *Forest and Stream* judged the Ocala land "by nature better fitted for the production of forest growth then [*sic*] for any other purpose."

## Political impact
By 1908 the great forest fight was behind Roosevelt. The Fulton proviso had already fixed the boundaries of the argument, and what remained was mostly administration. Local friction went on where it always had, over grazing fees, timber rules, and homesteaders who found new federal lines around land they had counted on. The proclamations themselves show that pressure. The exclusions from the Blue Mountains, Manzano, Sierra, Dixie, Crook, and Coconino forests, and four sections of the old Gila, returned land to the public domain for homesteading. From the Manzano proclamation of April 16 on, the standard text promised that it "shall not prevent the settlement and entry" of land already opened under the Forest Homestead Act of 1906.

Inside the Forest Service, the proclamations fed straight into the redistricting that took effect on July 1, 1908. The Blue Mountains forest, trimmed by proclamation in January, was divided by executive orders of June 13 among the new Whitman, Malheur, Umatilla, and Deschutes forests, and its name lasted only until July 1. The Uinta proclamation of July 1 opened by noting that the Ashley National Forest had been formed from part of the Uinta "by Executive Order of this date," and the Coconino proclamation noted that the rest of the Black Mesa forest had gone by executive order to the Sitgreaves, Apache, and Tonto. The Coconino proclamation also took care not "to modify the proclamations heretofore issued establishing the Grand Canyon National Game Preserve and the Grand Canyon National Monument, both of which include land embraced in the boundaries of the Coconino National Forest."

The December forests reached the national sporting press. *Forest and Stream* (December 12, 1908) ran a full column on the Ocala and Dakota proclamations, plainly drawn from a Washington release, and noted that "to Florida goes the distinction of getting the first National forest created east of the Mississippi River." It gave the Ocala as 201,480 acres and the Dakota as 14,080, and reported North Dakota's Governor John Burke "very much interested" in the Dakota forest. How local newspapers in Kansas, Florida, Arkansas, and North Dakota received these forests has not been checked.

## Legacy
### What became of the forests
Most of these forests are still on the map, some under new names after later consolidations:
- **Still carrying their 1908 names:** San Bernardino, Tonto, Modoc, Sierra, Klamath, Dixie, Gila, Coconino, Kaibab, and Ocala.
- **Monterey** became the Santa Barbara in 1919 and part of Los Padres in 1936.
- **Trinity** was combined for administration into the Shasta-Trinity in 1954.
- **Manzano** was renamed the Cibola in December 1931, and the **Datil** was split between the Cibola and the Gila the same month.
- **Jemez** joined the Pecos to form the Santa Fe in 1915.
- **Uinta** became part of the Uinta-Wasatch-Cache in 2007–08.
- **Ozark** was combined with the St. Francis in 1963 as the Ozark–St. Francis.

Coconino and Kaibab still frame the Grand Canyon, which Roosevelt set aside as a monument the same year ([Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3)).

### The experiments
The plains and Badlands experiments fared worse. The Kansas National Forest, the Garden City reserve renamed by the featured proclamation, never grew its promised timber on the dry plains. A 1911 drought killed 90 percent of the living trees, and the forest was abolished by Proclamation 1314 of October 14, 1915, effective December 1, its land made available to homesteaders. The Dakota National Forest, south of Medora, was abolished by Proclamation 1387 of July 30, 1917.

### From forest to airfield
The Choctawhatchee took the longest road. In 1911 it was combined with the Ocala as the Florida National Forest. The Ocala division was separated out again in 1927, and the remainder was renamed the Choctawhatchee by executive order that November. On June 27, 1940 the Choctawhatchee forest, about 340,000 acres, was transferred to the War Department and became Eglin Field, today Eglin Air Force Base.

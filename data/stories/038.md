## At a glance
When Cuba became an independent republic on May 20, 1902, its ships suddenly lost the protection they had enjoyed under American occupation and began paying a steep surcharge in United States ports. On July 3, 1902, once Cuba gave proof that it treated American ships evenly, Theodore Roosevelt lifted that surcharge under a statute dating back to 1828. It was a small, conditional, tit-for-tat order, issued while the much bigger fight over Cuban sugar was stalled in Congress.

## Background
Proclamation 482 belongs to a long series presidents issued under one statute, section 4228 of the Revised Statutes, carried into the 1874 revision from an act of 1828 and two later acts of 1830 and 1832. It let the President waive the extra "discriminating" tonnage and import duties charged on foreign ships and their goods, but only after a country gave "satisfactory proof" that it charged no such duties on American ships. An act of 1897 added the option of suspending the surcharges in part for countries that discriminated only in part. It was a reciprocity switch: treat American vessels evenly, and yours will be treated evenly here.

This proclamation flips that switch for Cuba. It suspends both the surcharge on Cuban vessels and any discriminating duty "on the produce, manufactures, or merchandise imported in such vessels," "so long as" Cuba keeps American ships exempt. It does not touch the ordinary tariff on Cuban goods, which every flag paid alike, and it lapses on its own if Cuba ever changes course.

The money was real. Without such a proclamation, Secretary of the Treasury Leslie M. Shaw explained, a foreign vessel paid an extra dollar a ton on top of the regular tonnage tax of three cents a net ton. For a large coastwise steamer that ran past a thousand dollars a voyage: the *Cienfuegos* was charged $1,346 on a single entry at New York.

## Historical context
Cuba had been a republic for just six weeks. The American military occupation that followed the War of 1898 ended on May 20, 1902, when Tomás Estrada Palma took office as Cuba's first president. Independence itself created the problem this proclamation solved.

During the occupation, Congress had protected Cuban-owned ships by statute. An act of February 10, 1900, gave vessels "owned by citizens of Cuba and documented as such by officers of the United States" the rights of the most favored nation, after Cuban ships sailing on American military permits had been charged the stateless-vessel surcharge in 1898–99. Then, Shaw reported, on May 20, 1902, "Cuban customs officers canceled these documents issued by officers of the United States, thus depriving the vessels of the privileges bestowed by the act of February 10, 1900." For six weeks Cuban ships paid the surcharge again: the *Cienfuegos* at New York on June 14, the *Santiago* on June 23 and the *Olinda* on June 24. The Cuban notice that the statute required, Shaw wrote, "was not received until July." The proclamation followed on July 3.

The request came through ordinary diplomatic channels, not a lobby. An Associated Press dispatch from Washington reported that the assurance "has been received through Mr. Squiers," Herbert G. Squiers, the American minister at Havana, and that once Cuba became independent "the law was self-acting, and the duties began to run."

All this sat beside, but apart from, the larger and far more contentious question of the moment: tariff reciprocity for Cuban goods, above all sugar. In a special message to Congress on June 13, 1902, Roosevelt argued that "[t]he granting of reciprocity with Cuba is a proposition which stands entirely alone," and framed it in the period's frank terms of national interest: "We expect Cuba to treat us on an exceptional footing politically, and we should put her in the same exceptional position economically." The House had passed a reciprocity bill in April, but it stalled in the Senate, where Republicans from the beet-sugar states would not move it, and Congress adjourned on July 1 without acting. Proclamation 482 touched none of that. It settled the narrow matter of ships and their cargoes, something the executive could do alone under a standing statute once Cuba's notice arrived.

## Political impact
The proclamation stirred no controversy; it applied a law then nearly three-quarters of a century old. It drew notice without argument. An Associated Press item from Washington explaining the order and its cause ran on July 6, 1902, in at least three California papers: the *San Francisco Call* ("Cuban Ship Duties Removed"), the *Los Angeles Herald* and the *San Diego Union*. It was carried by the wire, but a search of Utah and Washington state papers for July 1902 did not turn it up, so the coverage found is Californian, not national. The same dispatch claimed that such duties had never been charged on Cuban ships under Spanish rule or the American occupation, which the Treasury's own record of the 1898–99 surcharges partly contradicts.

The working audience was the customs service. The Treasury put the order in the hands of every collector of customs by Department Circular No. 83 of July 7, and the surcharge on Cuban ships and their cargoes stopped, for as long as Cuba kept American ships exempt.

The one sequel was practical. In December 1902 Secretary Shaw asked Congress to refund the $7,352 in surcharges collected from four Cuban steamers in 1898–99 and in June 1902, calling them "a consequence of exceptional conditions following the transfer of sovereignty over Cuba from Spain through the United States to the Cuban people." Congress did so by an act of January 14, 1903.

Whatever political heat surrounded Cuba in 1902 attached to the sugar-reciprocity fight and to the terms of the new relationship under the Platt Amendment, not to this evening-out of shipping duties. That fight moved from Congress to treaty-making after the July 1 adjournment.

## Legacy
The order has no real legacy of its own. It was the shipping-side preliminary to the far more consequential commercial settlement that followed. The Cuban Reciprocity Treaty was signed at Havana on December 11, 1902; ratifications were exchanged March 31, 1903, and it was proclaimed December 17, 1903, taking effect December 27, ten days after its implementing act. It cut tariffs on Cuban sugar entering the United States and bound the two economies tightly together for decades.

Section 4228 of the Revised Statutes, the authority behind this proclamation, remained the standard tool for equalizing foreign shipping duties well into the twentieth century. Franklin D. Roosevelt was still invoking it, as amended in 1897, for New Zealand in Proclamation 2455 of January 31, 1941.

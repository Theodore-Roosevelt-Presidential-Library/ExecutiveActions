## At a glance
These five proclamations are the odd remainders of 1908. One cut the tariff on French champagne in return for French concessions. One trimmed the Lincoln National Forest in New Mexico, though a printer's error labeled it a "National Monument." One traded land with Puerto Rico in San Juan, one opened the southern Rosebud Sioux lands to homesteaders by lottery, and the last named the year's Thanksgiving Day.

## Background
**Champagne for coffee (Proclamation 797, January 28).** The Dingley Tariff Act of 1897 allowed the President to lower duties on a few listed goods in return for concessions abroad (see [The Reciprocity Proclamations of 1906](story:64)). On January 28, 1908, Secretary of State Elihu Root and French Ambassador Jules Jusserand signed an additional agreement, and Roosevelt proclaimed it the same day. Its reach was narrow. Starting February 1, champagne and all other sparkling wines came in at lower rates: six dollars per dozen quart bottles, three dollars per dozen pints, a dollar and a half per dozen half-pints, and a dollar ninety per gallon on anything over a quart. In return, France and Algeria gave American "coffee, cacao, chocolate, vanilla and other food products known in the French tariff law as 'denrées coloniales de consommation,' except sugar and its byproducts and tobacco" their lowest rates, and did the same for American mineral oils. France could withdraw those concessions if the United States raised duties on French goods.

**A forest, not a monument (Proclamation 801, March 3).** This proclamation removed certain lands in the Territory of New Mexico from the Lincoln National Forest. The released tracts went back to the public domain, to be opened for homesteading on a date the Secretary of the Interior would set. Gifford Pinchot's *Report of the Forester* for 1908 puts the cut at 109,312 acres, which reduced the forest from 627,435 to 518,123 acres. The document is catalogued as *Diminishing Lincoln National Monument, New Mexico*, but the error goes back to the contents table of the *Statutes at Large* volume. The proclamation's own margin note and every word of its text say "Forest." No Lincoln National Monument has ever existed. The proclamation also recites that the forest was "set apart and reserved by public proclamation dated April twenty-fourth, nineteen hundred and seven." That is mistaken too. The April 1907 proclamation only enlarged the forest, and the Lincoln Forest Reserve had been created on July 26, 1902.

**A land swap in San Juan (Proclamation 819, August 4).** In 1903 Roosevelt had reserved an eighty-acre tract along the Caguas road in San Juan for the Navy (see [The Puerto Rico Land Reservations of 1903](story:44)). An act of March 4, 1907 allowed him to hand back what the Navy did not need, provided Puerto Rico deeded over three tracts in exchange. Governor Regis H. Post and Commodore Karl Rohrer signed that deed on April 27, 1908. The proclamation then conveyed the eighty acres to the island's government "for the use and benefit of the people of said Island," except six parcels the Navy kept: the naval hospital, the old and new wireless stations, the caminero (road-crew) house, and strips near the Puerta de Tierra jail. The document contradicts itself on one date. Its opening recital correctly dates the 1903 reservation to June 26, but the operative clause says January 26. That slip appears in the official 1908 printing, so the mistake is in the original document.

**The southern Rosebud lands (Proclamation 820, August 24).** Under the Act of March 2, 1907, Roosevelt opened the unallotted lands of the Rosebud Sioux Reservation lying "south of the Big White river, and east of Range 25 west" to homesteaders. That land is in Tripp County, South Dakota, which was not fully organized until June 15, 1909. It is often confused with Gregory County next door, where the 1904 opening took place and where the two filing towns, Dallas and Gregory, stand. Settlers would win claims by lottery: registration ran in October 1908, the drawing followed, and entry on the land began March 1, 1909. The Interior Department's announcement of August 27 put the land at 838,000 acres and promised "5,300 farms of a quarter section each." Those two numbers don't match, since 838,000 acres makes about 5,237 quarter sections. Within three weeks the Commissioner of the General Land Office said publicly that he could not give the number of quarter sections available.

**Thanksgiving (Proclamation 822, October 31).** The annual proclamation set apart Thursday, November 26.

## Historical context
Four of the five were ordinary government business for the time. The French arrangement was one of a chain of narrow tariff bargains, settled by diplomatic notes rather than a treaty, that kept European markets open to American goods. A head of state signed a document whose whole effect was the duty on sparkling wine, which shows how routine these deals had become. The Lincoln boundary change was part of the steady adjustment of forest lines after the forests passed to Pinchot's Forest Service in 1905, and returning land to the homestead laws was what settlers in the Territory wanted. The San Juan exchange sorted out which ground the United States would keep and which would go to the island's government. The city had grown up around the Navy's tract, and by 1907 both governments wanted a cleaner line. Congress authorized the trade, the island's legislature authorized the deed, and the proclamation confirmed a bargain already settled.

The Rosebud opening belongs to a harder history. Federal policy divided reservation land into individual allotments for tribal members and declared the rest "surplus" for white settlement. This was the second big Rosebud opening, after Proclamation 526 of 1904 had opened about 382,000 acres in Gregory County (see [The Sioux Land Openings of 1904](story:51)). It continued the breakup of a reservation the Sicangu (Brulé) Lakota had been guaranteed. Roosevelt saw these openings as moving Indians toward citizenship and self-support. The tribes experienced them as loss.

Only the Thanksgiving proclamation broke from routine, and even it followed a fixed yearly custom (see [Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20)). Its 1908 text is unusually pointed. In the proclamation of October 31, 1908, Roosevelt wrote that material well-being "is an indispensable foundation. But the foundation avails nothing by itself," and that "in the nation as in the individual, in the long run it is character that counts."

## Political impact
None of the five caused national controversy. The champagne deal mattered mostly to importers. Secretary Root and the French ambassador had exchanged notes through January 1908, the proclamation followed the agreement the same day, and the new rates took effect three days later. The forest and San Juan actions were administrative and passed without comment. The Lincoln cut returned land to the homestead laws, which is what settlers in the Territory wanted from a forest boundary.

The Rosebud lottery made regional headlines. Registration ran from October 5 to 17, 1908, a span the press called "twelve days" (Sunday the 11th was skipped). Applicants swore their affidavits before a commissioner, court officer or notary in any of six towns: Chamberlain, Dallas, Gregory or Presho in South Dakota, or O'Neill or Valentine in Nebraska. The busiest was O'Neill, where 45,808 oaths were sworn, followed by Chamberlain with 20,248. Sealed envelopes then had to reach the superintendent of the opening, James W. Witten, at Dallas or Gregory by 4:30 on the afternoon of October 17, "either by mail or in person, or otherwise, but not by registered mail." Nobody had to set foot in either town. Dallas received 80,635 envelopes and Gregory 34,134.

The drawing opened at ten in the morning on October 19. The *Santa Fe New Mexican* reported that day that "the cans contained the applications of 114,769 persons who had registered," which put the odds at worse than twenty to one for roughly 5,300 claims. Wire services covered it as a land-rush spectacle. Even the number of claims was shaky: within weeks of the Interior Department's promise of 5,300 farms, the Commissioner of the General Land Office said he could not give the number of quarter sections available, since it was not known how many allotments had gone to Indians. Apart from the Sicangu people themselves, whose objections carried little weight under the policy of the day, there was no organized opposition.

The Thanksgiving proclamation was received like any other year's.

## Legacy
These are minor documents, and most of them left a small mark.

### The champagne deal
The French arrangement, like the other Dingley-era bargains, was swept away by the Payne-Aldrich Tariff Act of August 5, 1909, which replaced them with a maximum-and-minimum tariff. Section 4 of that act told the President to give six months' notice to every country holding such an agreement, counted from April 30, 1909. Secretary of State Knox's formal notice of August 7 told the French government that the agreements of 1898, 1902 and 1908 "will cease to be in force at the expiration of the period of six months dating from April 30, 1909, namely, on October 31, 1909." France asked for more time and was told there was no constitutional way to grant it. Customhouses on both sides stayed open on Sunday, October 31, 1909, the last day the old rates applied. The champagne deal had lasted twenty-one months.

### The Lincoln National Forest
The Lincoln National Forest still covers part of south-central New Mexico, and its lines have moved many times since 1908. Woodrow Wilson cut it again by Proclamation 1474 on August 9, 1918, and Calvin Coolidge did the same by Proclamation 1706 on July 17, 1924; the two carry the same title, *Diminishing Area of the Lincoln National Forest, New Mexico*. Neither states an acreage, referring only to eliminations shown on an annexed diagram. A Philadelphia newspaper report of August 13, 1918 put the 1918 cut at close to 28,000 acres, made to simplify the boundary and open tracts found valuable for farming. The General Land Office's notice that year restored about 15,950 acres of public land within the excluded areas, open to homestead entry from October 9, 1918 and to other disposition from October 16. That counts only the public land restored, not the whole area cut from the forest. No similar notice has been found for 1924.

### San Juan
Little record survives of what became of the San Juan exchange itself. The Puerta de Tierra strip it released is now dense city. The ground had a naval history before Roosevelt: McKinley's Proclamation 435 of March 29, 1899 had already reserved it for naval purposes and referred to a commandant of the naval station at San Juan. A separate 1903 proclamation reserved land near San Antonio bridge for the Marine Hospital Service, very likely the "proposed Marine Hospital site" that Proclamation 819 names. The best-traced of the six parcels the Navy kept is the wireless station. Navy Radio San Juan opened in Puerta de Tierra in December 1903, the first wireless station on the island. It raised 300-foot steel towers in 1913, moved its transmitting to Cayey around 1918, and moved its operations to Isla Grande in the 1950s and Fort Allen in the 1970s; the successor command was disestablished in 2004. No source traces any of the six parcels by acreage or boundary, so what became of each one is not known. The wartime naval hospital begun at San Juan in January 1942 stood on a different site, near San Patricio.

### Rosebud
The Rosebud opening is the one with lasting consequences. The openings of 1904, 1907 and 1910 left the reservation a checkerboard of tribal, allotted and privately owned land. The question of its boundaries reached the Supreme Court in *Rosebud Sioux Tribe v. Kneip* (1977). The Court described the three acts as having "successively disposed of all unallotted lands in Gregory County (1904 Act), in Tripp and Lyman Counties (1907 Act), and in Mellette County (1910 Act)," and held that they "did clearly evidence congressional intent to diminish the boundaries of the Rosebud Sioux Reservation." Justice Marshall, joined by Justices Brennan and Stewart, dissented: "The Court holds today that in 1904, 1907, and 1910, Congress broke solemn promises it had made to the Rosebud Sioux Tribe and took from them, without any guarantee of compensation, three-quarters of their reservation." The tribe and later scholars have disputed the ruling, and *McGirt v. Oklahoma* (2020) cast new light on it.

### Thanksgiving
The 1908 Thanksgiving proclamation left no legacy beyond the yearly custom, which Congress fixed to the fourth Thursday of November by a joint resolution of December 26, 1941.

## At a glance
In 1905 Roosevelt signed fifty-three forest-reserve proclamations, adding nearly 23 million acres, far more than in any earlier year of his presidency. Thirty-six created new reserves, from California's Plumas and Shasta to Colorado's San Juan and Idaho's Sawtooth, and even onto the plains of Kansas. The rest enlarged, consolidated or redrew reserves already on the map. It was also the year Congress handed the reserves to Gifford Pinchot's foresters, who renamed their bureau the Forest Service.

## Background
Read one by one, the year's proclamations sort into thirty-six new reserves, one consolidation, and sixteen enlargements or boundary changes. A roundup by state:

- **Arizona:** Pinal Mountains (March 20) and Tonto (October 3), new; the Grand Canyon reserve enlarged (May 6).
- **California:** Plumas, Trinity, Klamath, Lassen Peak, Diamond Mountain, Shasta and Yuba, new; the Sierra enlarged; Lake Tahoe enlarged and renamed the Tahoe Forest Reserve.
- **Colorado:** Leadville, Gunnison, San Juan, Park Range, Wet Mountains, Cochetopah, Montezuma, Uncompahgre and Holy Cross, new; Pikes Peak formed by consolidation; the San Isabel enlarged; Battlement Mesa redrawn.
- **Idaho:** Henrys Lake, Weiser, Sawtooth, Payette and Cassia, new.
- **Montana:** Elkhorn, Big Belt and Hell Gate, new; the Madison and the Little Belt enlarged.
- **Oregon and Washington:** Wallowa, Wenaha, Chesnimnus and Maury Mountain, new; the Washington reserve redrawn.
- **Utah:** Sevier and Dixie, new; the Payson enlarged; lands added to the Uintah.
- **New Mexico:** Portales and Jemez, new; the Gila enlarged; the Lincoln redrawn.
- **The plains:** Short Pine in South Dakota and Garden City in Kansas, new.
- **Other boundary changes:** Madison (March), Medicine Bow, Bitter Root and Yellowstone.

Two of these were not what they seem. Pikes Peak (May 12) was a consolidation: the proclamation declared three earlier reserves, the Pikes Peak Timber Land, Plum Creek Timber Land and South Platte, "superseded," and set up the Pikes Peak Forest Reserve "in place thereof," taking most of their land plus additions. And the Little Belt was not new; Proclamation 602 enlarged the Little Belt Mountains reserve of 1902 and renamed it the Little Belt Forest Reserve.

The new reserves rested on section 24 of the General Revision Act of 1891, the "Creative Act," which let the President reserve forested public land by proclamation. The enlargements, reductions and boundary changes rested on a second law, the Sundry Civil Act of June 4, 1897, which provided that "the President is hereby authorized at any time to modify any Executive order that has been or may hereafter be made establishing any forest reserve, and by such modification may reduce the area or change the boundary lines of such reserve, or may vacate altogether any order creating such reserve." Several of the redrawings cut land out as well as adding it, restoring "the lands hereby excluded from the reserve" to settlement.

Every new-reserve proclamation carried the same "public good would be promoted" recital and the same warning against settlement, even those for the plains reserves at Garden City and Portales, which recite like the rest that the lands "are in part covered with timber." The year's last proclamation, Yuba (November 11), changed the form: it described no boundaries in words, reserving instead the lands "shown as the Yuba Forest Reserve on the diagram forming a part hereof," set apart "for the use and benefit of the people." The Montezuma Forest Reserve proclamation of June 13, reproduced on this site, stands in for the set.

What set 1905 apart was administrative. On February 1, 1905, Congress passed the Transfer Act, directing that the Secretary of Agriculture "shall, from and after the passage of this Act, execute or cause to be executed all laws affecting public lands" reserved under section 24. Surveying, entry and patenting stayed with the General Land Office in the Interior Department. Pinchot's Bureau of Forestry took charge of the reserves. The agricultural appropriation act of March 3, 1905, then funded the bureau for the year beginning July 1 under a new heading, "FOREST SERVICE," with "one forester, who shall be chief of bureau." The same act let "all persons employed in the forest reserve and national park service" make arrests for violations of reserve rules. The reserves themselves were still "forest reserves"; the name "national forest" came in 1907.

## Historical context
The reasons were the working creed of conservation in its practical, use-minded phase, told more fully in [The Forest Reserves of 1902](story:33) and [The Forest Reserves of 1903](story:42): fear of a coming "timber famine," distrust of speculators and monopolists cornering timber and range, and the conviction that forested headwaters had to stay public to protect the streams that farms, towns and the new federal irrigation projects ([The Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21)) depended on.

The new agency put its creed in writing the day it took over. The Secretary of Agriculture's letter of instructions of February 1, 1905, signed by James Wilson and drafted by Pinchot, laid down that "all land is to be devoted to its most productive use for the permanent good of the whole people," and that conflicts would be settled "from the standpoint of the greatest good of the greatest number in the long run." That summer's *Use Book* gave the reserves' purpose as "preserving a perpetual supply of timber for home industries, preventing the destruction of the forest cover which regulates the flow of streams, and protecting local residents from unfair competition in the use of forest and range."

To Pinchot and his foresters, the Transfer Act put the reserves in the hands of trained people committed to managed use, rather than with a land office whose business had been getting rid of the public domain. The burst of new reserves followed. Many had been in the works for some time: lands were first withdrawn from entry pending examination, and the Bureau of Forestry then recommended which should become reserves. In Idaho alone, the *Morning Oregonian*'s Washington bureau reported in April 1905, more than four million acres stood withdrawn "pending the creation of new forest reservations."

By the General Land Office's year-end figures, the reserves grew from 59 reserves covering 62,763,494 acres in 1904 to 83 reserves covering 85,627,472 acres in 1905, an increase of nearly 23 million acres. Roosevelt's previous high, in 1902, had added about 13.8 million.

The same year's proclamations both created and shrank reserves, which shows this was working administration, not grand gesture. Lines were drawn around timbered plateaus and watersheds as surveys came in, and trimmed where land proved more valuable for settlement or where earlier claims had to be honored.

## Political impact
The Idaho reserves drew a fight before they were signed. In April 1905 Senator Weldon B. Heyburn of Idaho, a Republican, objected that "many lands valuable for agriculture" had been swept into the Idaho withdrawals, wanted mineral lands kept out, and meant to ask the President to remove certain areas. The Bureau of Forestry answered that "forest reserves do not interfere with mineral development." On July 15, after the five Idaho reserves had been proclaimed, the *Oregonian* ran "HEYBURN STOPS HIS FIGHT": he had written Pinchot "half way apologizing for his violent opposition to the recent establishment of forest reserves in Idaho," though he did "not go so far as to accept the administration's views and policies as correct." The truce was short. By late September, the *Oregonian* reported, he was arguing that a separate, still-pending withdrawal in Shoshone County was farmland settled by homesteaders, while a General Land Office special agent reported that the claims were timberland and that most of the claimants lived in Wallace, Heyburn's home town. His case, that farm and mineral land belonged to settlers and miners and not in a reserve, would grow into the wider Western argument against presidential reservations.

Elsewhere the friction was local and economic. Cattlemen, sheepmen, loggers and miners saw a new reserve as a "lock-up" of range and timber they had used freely. West-central Colorado, the country of the Gunnison, Uncompahgre, Cochetopah and Holy Cross reserves, was by one regional history's account a "hotbed of anti-forest reserve agitation," led in Washington by Senator Henry M. Teller.

Colorado's local papers show both sides. The Pueblo *Colorado Daily Chieftain* of June 28 reported that the proclamations "have been received at the Pueblo land office" and gave the Wet Mountain reserve as 235,000 acres. The Gunnison reserve drew open protest. The *Surface Creek Champion* of Cedaredge reported on June 9 that "The Gunnison News-Champion backed by several of the citizens of that town are objecting in very strong language," claiming "that the reserve was established through the influence of sheepmen in order to get the ranges of that section." The Delta County paper answered from experience with its own reserve that it was "working an immense amount of good" in "preserving the forests and holding water," and that "the reserve gives the small cattlemen as much privilege on the ranges as the large cattle owners." In August the *Gunnison News-Champion* pointed to evidence from the Oregon land-fraud trials, a letter seeking to enlarge a forest reserve "so as to furnish lands for a further extension of their sheep range and to crowd out the cattlemen," and warned that "There are all sorts of influences at work in their establishment."

Within months of the transfer, the new Forest Service announced grazing fees, first charged in 1906, which stockmen would resent more than any boundary. Those complaints grew louder as the reserves multiplied and fed directly into the 1907 fight told in [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9). The press record of 1905 found so far is regional, not national.

## Legacy
Two and a half years later, in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907, Roosevelt would call conservation "the fundamental problem which underlies almost every other problem of our National life."

Nearly all the 1905 reserves survive inside today's national forests, though names and boundaries have been reshuffled many times. The Plumas, Klamath, Sawtooth, Tonto, San Juan and Dixie National Forests trace directly to 1905 reserves, and the Tahoe takes its name from the 1905 renaming. The Payette name dates from 1905, but the 1905 Payette lands went to the Boise National Forest in 1944, when the Weiser (itself a 1905 reserve) and the Idaho forest were combined to form a new Payette to the north. Trinity, Shasta and Sevier kept their names; Trinity and Shasta are now joined as the Shasta-Trinity.

Others were absorbed or divided:

- **Arizona and California:** Pinal Mountains joined the Tonto in 1908. Lassen Peak's forest became the Lassen National Forest, and the 1907 Lassen Peak and Cinder Cone monuments were carved from it ([The Volcanic Monuments: Lassen Peak and Cinder Cone](story:23)). Diamond Mountain went partly to the Plumas and the rest to the Lassen. Yuba joined the Tahoe in 1906.
- **Colorado:** the reserves live on in the San Isabel, Pike, Grand Mesa–Uncompahgre–Gunnison, White River, San Juan, Rio Grande and Routt forests. Park Range was split in 1908 between the Routt and Hayden; Wet Mountains joined the San Isabel in 1908; the Cochetopah (renamed Cochetopa in 1908) was divided in 1944; Holy Cross went to the White River in 1945; Montezuma was divided in 1947 between the San Juan and Uncompahgre; Leadville was broken up in 1930.
- **Montana:** Elkhorn went into the Helena, today the Helena–Lewis and Clark. Big Belt was divided in 1908 between the Gallatin and the Helena. Hell Gate was split among the Beaverhead, Deerlodge, Missoula and Bitterroot.
- **Idaho:** Henrys Lake joined part of the Yellowstone to form the Targhee in 1908. Cassia became part of the Minidoka in 1908, which joined the Sawtooth in 1953.
- **Oregon and Washington:** Chesnimnus and Wallowa were combined as the Imnaha in 1907, renamed Wallowa in 1908. The reserves lie today within the Wallowa-Whitman, Umatilla (which absorbed the Wenaha in 1920), Ochoco, Okanogan-Wenatchee and Mount Baker–Snoqualmie. The 54,220-acre Maury Mountain reserve lies largely in the Ochoco.
- **New Mexico:** Jemez was combined in part with the Taos to form the Carson in 1908.

The plains experiments did not last as timber forests. Roosevelt revoked Portales on March 16, 1907, its lands "no longer required for experimental forest purposes." Garden City was renamed the Kansas National Forest in 1908. Short Pine went into the Sioux National Forest in 1908, then the Custer in 1920, and so into today's Custer Gallatin.

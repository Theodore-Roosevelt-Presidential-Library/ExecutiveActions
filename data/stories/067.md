## At a glance
In 1907 Theodore Roosevelt signed seventy-eight forest-reserve proclamations. Thirty-two of them came in a two-day rush on March 1 and 2, days before a new law took away his power to create or enlarge reserves in six Western states. The rest of the year he kept going everywhere else, from Nevada and New Mexico to Arkansas and southeast Alaska.

## Background
These seventy-eight documents are short proclamations, signed by Roosevelt and countersigned for the State Department, most of them following one or two standard forms, with the boundary carried on an attached diagram rather than spelled out in words. Read together, they did six different things:

- **29** set apart a brand-new reserve or forest.
- **2** merged older reserves under one name: the **Imnaha**, Oregon, which replaced the Wallowa and Chesnimnus reserves, and the **Blue Mountains**, Oregon, which absorbed the Maury Mountain reserve.
- **34** enlarged an existing reserve.
- **7** both added and cut out land: Big Belt, Park Range, Weiser, Washington, Lincoln, Diamond Mountain, and Magdalena.
- **5** only cut out land: Little Belt, Pryor Mountains, Alexander Archipelago, Chugach, and Medicine Bow.
- **1** abolished a reserve outright: the **Portales**, New Mexico, created in 1905, which the government said was "no longer required for experimental forest purposes." Its land went back to the public domain, open to settlement at once and to entry after ninety days' notice.

The calendar tells the story of the year. Nine proclamations came between January 15 and February 15. Then came the burst of March 1 and 2, the episode historians call the "midnight forests," told in full in [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9) and [Bear Lodge Forest Reserve, Wyoming](story:10). Thirty-seven more followed between March 16 and December 30.

Two laws stood behind the documents. The twenty-nine that set apart new land rested on section 24 of the Act of March 3, 1891, which let a president "set apart and reserve" timbered public land "as public reservations." The other forty-nine, every enlargement, trim, merger, and the revocation, cited a different law: the Sundry Civil Act of June 4, 1897, which authorized the President "at any time to modify any Executive order that has been or may hereafter be made establishing any forest reserve," to "reduce the area or change the boundary lines," or to "vacate altogether" the order. One, the **Sacramento**, New Mexico, cited both, because it created new land and took in part of the Lincoln. The 1891 act let a president create a reserve; the 1897 act let him change one.

The **Little Rockies Forest Reserve** in Montana, signed March 2, shows the creating form at its plainest. It declares that "the public good would be promoted" by the reservation, sets the land apart "for the use and benefit of the people," protects lawful claims already on record, leaves any coal-land withdrawal in force, and closes with a flat warning "to all persons not to make settlement upon the lands reserved by this proclamation."

## Historical context
By 1907 the forest program sat on new ground. The Transfer Act of February 1, 1905 had handed the reserves to the Secretary of Agriculture, though Interior's General Land Office still surveyed, entered, and patented the land. A month later the bureau doing the work took the name Forest Service. Gifford Pinchot, its head, held the title "Forester," and his office now drew reserve boundaries from its own field work. As Pinchot put it of the March rush, "Our field force had already gathered practically all the facts."

Western anger at a president reserving millions of acres by his own signature had also reached Congress. Senator Charles W. Fulton of Oregon offered an amendment from the Senate floor, close in wording to a bill Senator Weldon B. Heyburn of Idaho had already introduced. It rode on the Agricultural Appropriation Act of March 4, 1907: "That hereafter no forest reserve shall be created, nor shall any additions be made to one heretofore created within the limits of the States of Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado, or Wyoming, except by Act of Congress."

Before that bill became law, Roosevelt and Pinchot moved. On March 1 and 2 Roosevelt signed thirty-three proclamations, counting Bear Lodge: thirteen establishing reserves and twenty enlarging or redrawing them. By the Forest Service's own reckoning they added 15,645,631 acres. Then, at 9:43 on the morning of March 4, he signed the bill. Most of the March reserves lay in the six Fulton states, but not all: the **Toiyabe** was in Nevada, and the **Las Animas** straddled Colorado and New Mexico Territory.

The same act quietly changed the vocabulary. A parenthetical in the Forest Service's appropriation line renamed "forest reserves" as "national forests," and the documents show the switch overnight. Every proclamation through March 2 says "Forest Reserve"; every one from Portales on March 16 says "National Forest," even for reserves created years before. A second change followed that spring. Beginning with **Big Burros**, New Mexico, on April 6, most proclamations warned that land was closed to settlement "unless and until" the Secretary of Agriculture listed it and opened it under the Forest Homestead Act of June 11, 1906.

The reasons given were the practical conservation of the day: protect watersheds, keep a working timber supply against a feared "timber famine," and keep public timber and range away from speculators and monopolies. At the end of that year, in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907, Roosevelt called conservation "the fundamental problem which underlies almost every other problem of our National life."

## Political impact
The storm broke over the March burst, not over any one reserve. Western critics saw a president defeating a limit Congress had just written in the very days before it took effect. Roosevelt cast the rush as saving the land from "land grabbers," as he later put it in his *Autobiography* (1913), and he was unrepentant: "the opponents of the Forest Service turned handsprings in their wrath; and dire were their threats against the Executive; but the threats could not be carried out, and were really only a tribute to the efficiency of our action." The confrontation is one of the sharpest tests of executive power in his presidency.

For the rest of the year the work went on under the power Congress had left him. Two later proclamations did fall in Fulton states, **Pryor Mountains**, Montana (June 1) and **Medicine Bow**, Colorado and Wyoming (September 27), but both only cut out land, which the proviso did not forbid. Four Fulton-state actions had also come before the burst: Caribou, Idaho and Wyoming (new); Cascade Range, Oregon (enlarged); Ouray, Colorado (new); and Little Belt, Montana (reduced). The new and enlarged forests from April through December lay in Arizona, New Mexico, California, Nevada, Utah, Arkansas, and Alaska.

California papers printed the later forests as brief Washington announcements, without comment. The **Inyo**, set apart May 25, ran in the *Santa Cruz Surf*, the *San Bernardino Sun*, and the *San Pedro Daily News*, which headlined it "INYO FORESTS ARE RESERVED. President Aids Conservation of Water Supply For Los Angeles County" and called it "the northermost part of the ground which was proposed to reserve for the conservation of the water supply of the city of Los Angeles." The *San Francisco Call* headed its dispatch on the **Chugach** "VAST FOREST RESERVE CREATED IN ALASKA," giving "approximately 4,960,000 acres" and reporting that "Lumbermen at Valdez within the Chugach forest anticipate a good trade in the future." The **Tongass**, signed September 10 and countersigned by Acting Secretary of State Alvey A. Adee, appeared as a one-line filler: 2,006,000 acres, "composed of rough land, but its islands and mainland are well covered with trees." None of these items reports opposition. Who requested the Inyo, Chugach, Tongass, Arkansas, and Vegas forests, and how the Arkansas, Nevada, and Alaska press greeted them, is not yet known.

## Legacy
The forests of 1907 stuck. Midnight reserves and ordinary ones alike remain the backbone of national forests across the northern Rockies, the Cascades, the Colorado high country, the Southwest, and coastal Alaska, though most now carry later names after the consolidations of 1908 and after. The 1907 Tongass was merged with the Alexander Archipelago reserve of 1902 by Executive Order 908 in July 1908 (see [The Forest Reserves of 1902](story:33)); today's Tongass is the largest national forest in the country, at about 16.7 million acres.

The Fulton proviso did end presidential creation and enlargement of forest reserves in six states, a limit that bound presidents in those states from then on. But it came only after the March proclamations were signed, and Roosevelt kept using his 1891 and 1897 powers everywhere else, adding, by his own account, "more than forty-three million acres" to the National Forests that year. The phrase "midnight forests" itself is a historians' label: the earliest use found is Harold K. Steen's "so-called midnight reserves" in 1976. The fight it names, over how far and how fast a president may act to protect public land against the wishes of Congress and local industry, runs from the Antiquities Act monument battles through [The Coal-Land Withdrawals, 1906–1909](story:27) and into the present.

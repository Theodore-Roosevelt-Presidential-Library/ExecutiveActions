## At a glance
On December 4, 1908, Roosevelt signed five short proclamations reserving small pieces of Hawaiian public land "for light-house purposes": at Hana Bay and McGregor's Point on Maui, Kahala Point and Lae o Kokole on Kauai, and Ka Lae, the southern tip of the Island of Hawaii. Every one of the five already had a working light. The proclamations secured federal title to ground already in use, and four of those lights are still Coast Guard aids to navigation.

## Background
The five sites ringed the islands' shipping approaches. All rested on the same legal footing. The 1898 joint resolution annexing Hawaii had said that the general public-land laws of the United States would not apply to Hawaiian lands and that Congress would pass special laws for them. So each parcel was reserved "subject to such legislative action as the Congress of the United States may take with respect thereto." Each proclamation was countersigned by Acting Secretary of State Alvey A. Adee.

The parcels were small and varied:

- **Hana Bay, Maui (Proclamation 826):** no survey at all. It reserves "All of the land, the property of the United States of America, on the island of Puukii (sometimes known as Puuiki) on the south side of the entrance to the harbor variously known as Hana, Pueokahi and Kauiki."
- **Lae o Kokole, Kauai (Proclamation 827):** a 5.5486-acre station plot plus a 10.6998-acre right of way to the government road, run through the "Mana Lots" and across a swamp. At about 16.25 acres in all, it was the largest of the five.
- **McGregor's Point, Maui (Proclamation 828):** a 1.32-acre "Light-house site" and a separate 4.2-acre "Dwelling site."
- **Kahala Point, Kauai (Proclamation 829):** a 4.5202-acre station plot and a twenty-foot-wide right of way of 1.1524 acres to the Anahola wharf landing.
- **Ka Lae, Island of Hawaii (Proclamation 830):** the largest single station plot, 10.02 acres "exclusive of seashore and Pali." Its survey plat, "Survey of Ka Lae Light-House Premises," was prepared in 1908 under Captain C. W. Otwell, Corps of Engineers, assistant to the engineer of the 12th Light-House District at Honolulu.

Ka Lae was the southern tip of the Hawaiian Islands, though not of the United States. In 1908 the country also held Guam, the Philippines and Tutuila in American Samoa, all well to the south.

## Historical context
These proclamations followed from Roosevelt's 1903 order folding the old Republic of Hawaii's lighthouse establishment into the federal system (see [Lighthouses in Hawaii](story:48)). That December he asked Congress for money for "building light-houses in Hawaii, and taking possession of those already built" (Third Annual Message, December 7, 1903). Under the Light-House Board, small lights spread quickly along the coasts.

By December 1908 the lights at all five sites were already working. Otwell's report for the year ending June 30, 1908, printed in the *Hawaiian Star* on November 20 under the headline "OTWELL WRITES OF LIGHTHOUSE," counted McGregor's Point among the lens lanterns already in service. It reported that "During the year there were four lens lanterns established": Kauiki Head on Maui, and Kahala Point, Makahuena Point and Lae o Kokole on Kauai. It also noted new keepers' dwellings at McGregor's Point, Keahole and Ka Lae. The Ka Lae lens lantern, on a thirty-four-foot wooden mast, dated to March 5, 1906. The proclamations themselves show the lights in place: the McGregor's Point description is measured "From the mast of McGregor's Point Light Station," and the Ka Lae plat shows a "LIGHT HOUSE" and a "Keeper's dwelling" already inside the boundary.

The same report shows why title mattered. Work on two new lights, at Nakalele on Maui and Kailua on the Island of Hawaii, would begin "as soon as title can be acquired." For a new station on Molokaʻi, the question of setting aside land "was taken up with the Executive Office of the Territory of Hawaii," which agreed. Ceded land in Hawaii was managed by the Territory, so a federal light on it needed both the Territory's agreement and a formal federal reservation. The December proclamations supplied that reservation for five stations already running. Money questions between Honolulu and Washington were also still open: local officers had again recommended that the Territory be paid back for keeping up the lights between annexation and the federal takeover.

## Political impact
The proclamations were routine: five small survey parcels under lights already burning, signed on a single December day and countersigned by Acting Secretary of State Alvey A. Adee. No opposition has been found. No press notice has turned up either, though the search was limited: the University of Hawaiʻi's digitized *Hawaiian Star*, *Pacific Commercial Advertiser* and *Maui News* for December 1908 through February 1909 show nothing, and their scanned text is uneven, so the island papers may still have noticed them.

Their one built-in point of friction was the caveat in each text that Congress held the last word on Hawaiian land. Behind it sat the larger arrangement: ceded land in Hawaii was managed by the Territory, so a federal light on it needed both the Territory's agreement and a federal reservation. Money between Honolulu and Washington was also still unsettled, with local officers again recommending that the Territory be repaid for keeping up the lights between annexation and the federal takeover.

In Washington the reservations left little trace. The Light-House Board's *Annual Report* for fiscal 1909 never mentions the proclamations. Its only notice of these stations is a list of Twelfth District lights that "should be provided with quarters for the keepers," among them Kahala and Lae o Kokole.

## Legacy
All five lights passed with the rest of the national system from the Light-House Board to the Bureau of Lighthouses in 1910 and to the U.S. Coast Guard in 1939. As automation spread, the keepers left and small concrete towers and poles replaced the masts. LighthouseFriends traces the later histories:

- **Ka Lae:** a forty-five-foot steel tower replaced the mast in 1928, its beacon run from batteries charged by a wind-driven generator. The light was automated in 1949, replaced by a thirty-two-foot concrete pole in 1972, and now runs on solar-charged batteries. The light is owned by the Coast Guard.
- **McGregor Point:** a twenty-foot reinforced-concrete pyramidal tower had replaced the mast by 1915.
- **Kaʻuiki Head:** the Hana Bay light stands on the islet of Puʻukiʻi just off Kaʻuiki Head, whose name the station has always carried. A fourteen-foot concrete tower went up there in 1914, and the light was converted to acetylene and automated. The islet is now a bird sanctuary closed to visitors.
- **Kahala Point:** automated in 1913.

These four remain Coast Guard aids to navigation. A light called Kokole still stands east of Kekaha on Kauai, but whether it sits on the 1908 Lae o Kokole parcel has not been confirmed.

### Ka Lae and statehood
Ka Lae also became an early test of what statehood meant for ceded land. In 1960 the Coast Guard declared about 5¾ acres of the reservation surplus. Governor William F. Quinn and Representative Daniel K. Inouye argued that this "ceded land which was set aside for the use of the Coast Guard" fell under section 5(e) of the Hawaii Statehood Act and should go back to the State (Inouye to Representative Herbert C. Bonner, June 3, 1960). Whether it did, and whether any of the other four reservations has gone back, is not yet known.

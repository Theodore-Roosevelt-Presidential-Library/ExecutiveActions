## At a glance
Most of the orders from late summer and autumn 1907 were routine personnel and reservation business. Two were not. Executive Order 675 set aside the shifting "mud lumps" at the mouths of the Mississippi as the Tern Islands Reservation, and Executive Order 682 set aside the Shell Keys off the Louisiana coast. Both were links in the chain of fifty-one bird reservations Roosevelt created by executive order. A third order, Executive Order 669½, laid down the rules for the Dominican customs receivership, the working machinery of the Roosevelt Corollary.

## Background
**Two bird reservations.** Both orders used the simple device Roosevelt had first tried at Pelican Island in 1903 (see [Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1)). He took small, uninhabited islets already in federal hands that no one else much wanted and, with a single paragraph, reserved them "for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds." Both came under the Bureau of Biological Survey, which ran the growing chain of refuges. (It had been a Division until 1905.)

Executive Order 675 (August 8, 1907) is the shorter of the two:

> It is hereby ordered that all small islets, commonly called mud lumps, in or near the mouths of the Mississippi river, Louisiana, located within the area segregated and shown upon the diagram hereto attached and made a part of this order, are hereby reserved and set aside for the use of the Department of Agriculture, as a preserve and breeding ground for native birds. This reservation to be known as Tern Islands Reservation.

Shell Keys took an extra step. The islets, a small group in the Gulf of Mexico about three and a half miles south of Marsh Island, already sat inside a lighthouse reservation. Executive Order 682 (August 17, 1907) describes that reservation as created by "the Executive Order of July 9, 1855." The order first declared that reservation "vacated and set aside," then reserved the islets anew "for the use of the Department of Agriculture as a reserve and breeding ground for native birds." The double use of "set aside" can look odd. Applied to an order, "vacated and set aside" was the legal phrase for cancelling it, the way a court sets aside a judgment. The drafters seem to have noticed the clash, because the same sentence switches to "reserved and set apart" for the new bird reservation.

**The Dominican receivership.** Executive Order 669½ (July 25) laid down the general regulations for the Dominican customs receivership and placed it under the Bureau of Insular Affairs. The regulations took effect August 1, 1907.

**Labor Day.** Executive Order 677 (August 10) gave Labor Day off to federal workers paid by the day. Salaried and classified employees already had the holiday. Citing the act of June 28, 1894, the order reached "all per diem employees and other day laborers in the Federal public service, wherever employed, whose employment extends through and beyond the first Monday in September."

The rest of the run is the usual ledger: clerks and examiners appointed or reinstated without regard to the civil-service rules, military reservations along the Philippine coast enlarged and then revoked, and a boundary corrected in Alaska.

## Historical context
The bird reservations answered a crisis everyone understood. Wading birds and shorebirds were being slaughtered for the millinery trade. Egret and tern plumes sold by the ounce to decorate hats, and whole colonies along the Gulf and Atlantic coasts were shot out in the breeding season. The Audubon movement and the American Ornithologists' Union pushed for federal sanctuaries. Roosevelt, a lifelong bird-watcher who needed no persuading, gave them the one tool that needed neither money nor an act of Congress: an executive order reserving public land the government already owned.

The Dominican order belonged to a very different story. Under Roosevelt's 1904 corollary to the Monroe Doctrine, the United States took over the collection of Caribbean debts itself rather than let European creditors collect them by force. The convention making the arrangement formal was concluded February 8, 1907. The Senate advised ratification on February 25, the President ratified it June 22, ratifications were exchanged July 5, and the convention was proclaimed July 25, 1907. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 669½ the same day. It was the domestic housekeeping that put the convention into operation.

## Political impact
None of these orders drew much public reaction at the time.

**The bird reservations.** They were the kind of quiet stroke Roosevelt could make without asking anyone, and that is exactly what made the practice controversial in principle. Ranchers, plume hunters and some members of Congress objected that the President was locking up land and resources by fiat. But these two reservations were a few acres of mud and shell apiece, and neither started a fight. Both went to the Department of Agriculture, where the Bureau of Biological Survey added them to the chain of refuges it ran.

**The Dominican receivership.** The regulations sat inside a policy that was genuinely contested. The Senate had refused the first version of the arrangement outright. An executive agreement signed January 20, 1905, drew fire for going around the Senate. Recast as a treaty and signed February 7, 1905, it never reached a two-thirds majority and died without a final vote. Roosevelt then put the receivership into effect anyway, through a temporary arrangement (a modus vivendi) of April 1, 1905. The Senate consented only to a renegotiated convention, and then quickly, seventeen days after it was signed. Critics north and south saw the Roosevelt Corollary as a claim to police the hemisphere. The order that carried the convention into effect, though, was administrative and passed without notice. Its regulations took effect August 1, 1907, under the Bureau of Insular Affairs.

## Legacy
### Shell Keys
The two Louisiana refuges outlived the administration that made them, though only one lasted. Both became part of the modern National Wildlife Refuge System. Shell Keys National Wildlife Refuge is still there: an eight-acre refuge of shell islets south of Marsh Island in Iberia Parish, reached by boat offshore, administered from the Southwest Louisiana refuge complex, and one of the oldest units in the system. The Fish and Wildlife Service's mapped location sits within a minute or two of the description in the 1907 order.

### Tern Islands
Tern Islands did not last. Its "mud lumps" were some of the least permanent real estate in America. Public Land Order 892, signed April 15, 1953, by Secretary of the Interior Douglas McKay under authority delegated by Executive Order 10355, and published in the *Federal Register* on April 22, 1953, revoked the 1907 order outright. Its caption reads "LOUISIANA — REVOKING EXECUTIVE ORDER OF AUGUST 8, 1907." The revocation was a Secretary's action under delegated authority, not a president's.

### Fifty-one bird reservations
Together the two orders are part of the fifty-one bird reservations Roosevelt created by executive order, the practical foundation of the federal wildlife-refuge system. The National Park Service counts "51 Federal bird reservations, and 4 national game reserves." The Fish and Wildlife Service's figure of 55 is the same total with the four game preserves folded in.

### The Dominican receivership
Executive Order 4353 replaced the 1907 receivership regulations in 1925. The receivership itself was abolished March 31, 1941, under the Trujillo–Hull Convention of September 24, 1940. That convention ended in its turn. President Trujillo proclaimed its termination on his own on July 18, 1947. Three days later, on July 21, a check went to the bondholders' representative toward paying off the 1922 and 1926 bond issues in full. An exchange of notes of August 9, 1951, recognized "the automatic termination of the convention of September 24, 1940, on October 1, 1947." The receivership remains a textbook example of the Roosevelt Corollary at work, debated both as a milestone of hemispheric stability and as an act of U.S. intervention.

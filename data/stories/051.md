## At a glance
In the spring of 1904 Roosevelt signed two proclamations opening "surplus" Sioux reservation land to white homesteaders: about 382,000 acres of the Rosebud Reservation in South Dakota and about 88,000 acres at Devils Lake in North Dakota. More than 100,000 people registered for the Rosebud lottery alone. In both cases Congress had rewritten the tribes' 1901 agreements before enacting them. A third proclamation simply released one 160-acre former mission-school tract.

## Background
**Two openings and a small outlier.** Proclamations 526 (Rosebud, May 13, 1904) and 530 (Devils Lake, June 2, 1904) put into effect land cessions Congress had already enacted. Proclamation 521 (March 30, 1904) did something much smaller. It released for disposal under the Sioux Act of 1889 a single 160-acre tract in the old Cheyenne River Agency reserve that had been set apart for St. John's Mission School, after the Episcopal Church's Domestic and Foreign Missionary Society said it no longer wanted the land. It amended Proclamation 497 of February 1903, which had released the rest of the old agency reserve (see [The Indian-Land Withdrawal Revocations of 1903](story:46)).

**How an opening worked.** In both openings a United States Indian Inspector, James McLaughlin, negotiated an agreement with the tribe to give up its unallotted lands: at Rosebud on September 14, 1901, and at Devils Lake on November 2, 1901. Two and a half years later Congress "accepted, ratified, and confirmed" each agreement "as herein amended and modified." The President then issued a proclamation fixing the opening day, the price homesteaders would pay, and an elaborate procedure of registration and drawing to decide who got which claim.

**The amendments mattered.** The 1901 Rosebud agreement, signed by 1,031 of the reservation's 1,359 adult men, sold about 416,000 acres outright for $1,040,000. Congress never ratified it, because, as its House sponsor, Representative Charles H. Burke of South Dakota, explained, "it provided that the Government should pay for the lands outright." The 1904 act instead made the United States a trustee that would sell the land to settlers and pay the tribe only as the money came in. McLaughlin returned to Rosebud in 1903 to win consent to the change. He got a majority, on condition that the price be raised, but not the three-fourths the 1868 treaty required. Congress passed the act anyway.

**What the proclamations did.**
- **Rosebud (526).** Opened the unallotted land in Gregory County, which the proclamation says "approximates three hundred and eighty-two thousand (382,000) acres," on August 8, 1904. Registration ran July 5–23 at Chamberlain, Yankton, Bonesteel and Fairfax, with a public drawing beginning July 28 at Chamberlain to fix the order of entry. Prices ran from four dollars an acre in the first three months down to two dollars and fifty cents.
- **Devils Lake (530).** Opened roughly 88,000 acres, including the abandoned Fort Totten military reserve, on September 6, 1904, at "four dollars and fifty cents per acre." It held back 6,160 acres for allotments to sixty-one Indians still entitled to them. Registration ran August 8–20 at Devils Lake and Grand Forks.
- **Reserved tracts.** In both openings sections sixteen and thirty-six of every township went to the state for the common schools. Tracts were held back for a sub-issue station, day schools, the Fort Totten school and its Grey Nuns Department, the agency, and Catholic, Congregational, Presbyterian and Episcopal missions.
- **A park.** The Devils Lake act let the President reserve "a tract embracing Sullys Hill ... about nine hundred and sixty acres, as a public park." Proclamation 530 used that power, reserving land "for public use as a park to be known as Sully's Hill Park."

## Historical context
These openings were the ground-level machinery of the allotment policy launched by the Dawes Act of 1887. The idea, widely shared at the time by reformers and Western settlers alike, was to break up communal reservations: each Indian family would receive its own allotment to farm, and the "surplus" would be sold to white homesteaders. Reformers called it assimilation and uplift. Western states and land-hungry settlers saw millions of acres finally coming onto the market.

The two reservations had different origins. Rosebud was one of the separate reservations into which the Sioux Act of March 2, 1889, had divided the Great Sioux Reservation. Devils Lake was never part of it: the treaty of February 19, 1867, set it apart for the Sisseton and Wahpeton bands, with the Cut-Head Yanktonais.

The legal ground had just shifted. In *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (January 5, 1903) the Supreme Court held that Congress could dispose of tribal land without the consent a treaty required, and Congress leaned on that holding when it passed the Rosebud act after McLaughlin's 1903 canvass fell short. Devils Lake went the same way. The 1901 agreement there, signed by 216 of 296 adult men, sold the land outright for $345,000 and was to take effect once signed by McLaughlin and a majority of the men "and when accepted and ratified by the Congress of the United States." The Act of April 27, 1904, rewrote it "to read as follows": the fixed price became a promise to sell to homesteaders and pay the tribe "the proceeds derived from the sale," with the United States "as trustee for said Indians," and the agreement would now take effect simply "when accepted and ratified by the Congress." Nothing sent the new terms back to the tribe.

The Senate noticed. When the bill came up on April 2, 1904, Senator Fred T. Dubois of Idaho asked about the $4.50 price: "Is this the agreement made with the Indians?" Orville Platt of Connecticut answered, "No." North Dakota's Henry C. Hansbrough, managing the bill, called the price "a subsequent arrangement which has been made by those who are interested in passing the bill." Dubois was objecting on the settlers' behalf, not the tribe's. Representative Thomas F. Marshall of North Dakota had introduced the bill in the House that January.

On paper the proclamations read as pure land-office procedure, page after page on registration certificates, sealed envelopes and the order of the drawing. What they carried out was the transfer of another large share of tribal land into non-Indian hands.

## Political impact
The Rosebud lottery drew a crowd on a national scale. The Commissioner of Indian Affairs' 1904 report, quoting the General Land Office, counted 106,308 registered applicants for 385,817.11 acres opened at Bonesteel on August 8, "divided into 2,412 claims," odds of about one in forty-six. The South Dakota State Historical Society's marker at Bonesteel gives the registration town by town: 57,432 at Yankton, 35,176 at Bonesteel, 8,700 at Fairfax and 6,100 at Chamberlain. The crowds brought gamblers and confidence men, and Bonesteel's townspeople fought a street battle to drive them out, the "Battle of Bonesteel," in which one man was killed. The smaller Devils Lake opening drew 15,086 registrants for 88,948.39 acres in 556 claims. Daily drawing results were "given to the press to be published as a matter of news," as the proclamations directed.

The registration was news well beyond the Dakotas. Connecticut's *Norwich Bulletin* carried wire dispatches through July. From Bonesteel it reported a crowd of 316 who had waited all night in a driving rain, with about 1,500 people on hand when the registration office opened (July 6, 1904), and officers expecting two to three thousand registrations a day (July 7). From Yankton it reported registration opening there in the rain (July 22). In New York, *Collier's* ran a long illustrated feature by Richard Lloyd Jones on August 13, 1904, that pictured the land seekers as an army a hundred thousand strong marching into South Dakota and described grafters taking hold of Bonesteel. Neither treated the opening as controversial; both covered the crowds and the disorder.

In Congress, the Devils Lake bill drew a pointed question but little more. Representative Thomas F. Marshall of North Dakota introduced it in the House on January 26, 1904, and Senator Henry C. Hansbrough of North Dakota managed it in the Senate. When Senator Fred T. Dubois of Idaho challenged the $4.50 price on April 2, Hansbrough explained that it had been agreed between the House and Senate sponsors and approved by the Secretary of the Interior. Dubois was speaking for the settlers who would pay it, not for the tribe, and the bill simply went over for the day. It became law on April 27. The Indian Office's 1904 report described the 1901 Devils Lake terms and then said only that the agreement "was ratified by act of April 27, 1904," though the act had rewritten both the price and the way the agreement took effect.

Within the tribes the story was different. At Rosebud the record itself documents the grievance: a cession enacted on terms that three-fourths of the adult men never accepted. The fairness of these agreements was disputed at the time and long afterward.

## Legacy
### Rosebud
The Rosebud opening turned out to be the first of three cessions, in 1904, 1907 and 1910, whose combined effect the Supreme Court weighed in *Rosebud Sioux Tribe v. Kneip* (1977). By a 6–3 vote the Court held that these "unilateral Acts of Congress without the consent of three-quarters of the members of the tribe required by the original Treaty" were meant to shrink the reservation. They removed from it the unallotted lands of Gregory County (1904), Tripp County and a small part of Lyman County (1907), and Mellette County (1910). The ruling remains central to jurisdiction disputes on and around the reservation.

### Sully's Hill
The Devils Lake opening left a lasting mark of a different kind. The roughly 960-acre Sully's Hill Park that Proclamation 530 reserved came to be counted by the National Park Service among the national parks of Roosevelt's presidency. Congress funded a big-game preserve inside it in 1914 and turned the whole park into Sullys Hill National Game Preserve in 1931. In December 2019, at the Spirit Lake Tribe's request, Congress renamed it White Horse Hill National Game Preserve. The 994-acre preserve still shelters bison and elk near the shore of Devils Lake, within the boundaries of the Spirit Lake Tribe's reservation as drawn in 1867.

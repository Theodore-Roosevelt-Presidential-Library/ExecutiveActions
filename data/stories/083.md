## At a glance
Roosevelt's first twenty-seven executive orders of 1904 were mostly about people: a clerk rehired here, a curator appointed there. Two touched the year's big stories. When Senator Mark Hanna died in February, Roosevelt closed federal offices in Ohio for his funeral. And as Russia and Japan went to war, he ordered every federal official, civil, military, and naval, to stay neutral not only in action but in speech.

## Background
Most of the group did the ordinary work of the classified service. Eighteen orders let a department hire, keep, reinstate, transfer, or promote one named person outside the usual rule (EOs 243, 244, 245, 247, 248, 249, 251, 252, 255, 257, 260, 261, 263, 264, 265, 266, 268, 269). A few examples:

- **Reinstatements** of Evelyn M. Ford (243), Elizabeth C. Peters (245), Kate L. Croggan (248), and Katherine C. Masterson (260) waived Rule IX's one-year limit on returning to federal work.
- **EO 249** let James M. Cooper and Ralston Williams work for "the board of medical officers appointed to investigate the origin and spread of typhoid fever in United States military camps in 1898 until the completion of the report of the board."
- **EO 255** placed Francis Walker, of Massachusetts, in the year-old Bureau of Corporations.
- **EO 257** transferred Merrit O. Chance from clerk to the Secretary of War to superintendent of post-office supplies, explaining his qualifications at length.
- **EO 264** made Dr. William L. Ralph "curator of the section of birds' eggs in the National Museum, at a salary of $100 per month."
- **EO 266**, for State Department clerk Edna K. Hoyt, carries a note: "Done at the request of Secretary Hay, who says she is now the best clerk of her class that he has."
- **EO 251**, as described in the only source found for it, let Matthew J. McCarthy move without examination from special deputy surveyor to deputy surveyor and inspector at the customs port of Syracuse, New York.

**EO 252** reads in full: "Mrs. Marie L. Baldwin, whose name appears upon the Minnesota clerk register, may be certified for appointment as clerk at $900 in the Office of Indian Affairs without regard to the provisions of Civil-Service Rule VII." She was Marie Louise Bottineau Baldwin, Métis/Turtle Mountain Chippewa, whose family had lived in Minneapolis, where she clerked in her father's law office.

The rule changes were small. EO 253 (February 17) added "All commissioners for the national military parks" to the War Department positions excepted from examination; each of the four parks had a board of three commissioners who by law had fought in the battles commemorated. EO 259 widened a private-practice exception for contract physicians earning not more than $720 a year from those "at Indian schools" to those "in the Indian service." EO 262 let a person reinstated at a lower grade be promoted back to the former grade without examination. EO 250 revoked a 1902 special rule that had made people employed by the military government of Cuba eligible for retention and employment in the classified service; that government had ended when the Cuban republic was inaugurated on May 20, 1902. EOs 246 and 267 extended the Spanish Treaty Claims Commission, EO 246 carrying it to September 2, 1904.

EO 256 (March 1) classified "All positions in the civil service of the War Department in the Philippines, except those filled by persons employed merely as skilled or unskilled laborers, and by persons appointed by the President." Present employees commended by their chiefs could stay, and after three years would be "eligible for transfer to competitive positions in the United States." These were the War Department's own civilian staff in the islands, not the insular government's separate Philippine Civil Service.

**The Hanna order.** Senator Marcus A. Hanna of Ohio, William McKinley's campaign manager in 1896 and 1900, chairman of the Republican National Committee since 1896, and the leading figure of the party's conservative wing, died of typhoid fever at his Washington residence, close by the Arlington Hotel, at about 6:30 p.m. on February 15, 1904. Three days later Roosevelt issued EO 254, a single sentence:

> As a mark of respect to the memory of Hon. Marcus A. Hanna, late senator from the state of Ohio, it is hereby ordered that all federal offices in Ohio be closed during the hours of the funeral tomorrow, Friday, the 19th instant.

## Historical context
The Hanna order is the smallest possible official gesture toward a large political fact. Through the winter of 1903–04, anti-Roosevelt financiers and some party men had talked up a Hanna candidacy for the 1904 Republican nomination, and Hanna let the talk run without declaring. In December 1903 the two men met at length and reached an understanding. Hanna never left his Washington residence after the Gridiron Club dinner of January 30, and Roosevelt called on the afternoon he died. Hanna also chaired the Senate Committee on Interoceanic Canals; the Senate took up the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty while he lay dying and gave its advice and consent on February 23, eight days after his death ([Governing the Panama Canal Zone: the Executive Orders of 1904](story:14)).

EO 258, of March 10, belongs to the other great event of early 1904, the outbreak of war between Russia and Japan. It followed Roosevelt's neutrality proclamation of February 11 ([Declaration of Neutrality in the War Between Japan and Russia](story:13)). The order directed "All officials of the Government, civil, military, and naval," "not only to observe the President's proclamation of neutrality in the pending war between Russia and Japan, but also to abstain from either action or speech which can legitimately cause irritation to either of the combatants." Then it gave its reasons:

> "It is always unfortunate to bring old-world antipathies and jealousies into our life, or by speech or conduct to excite anger and resentment toward our nation in friendly foreign lands; but in a government employee, whose official position makes him in some sense the representative of the people, the mischief of such actions is greatly increased. A strong and self-confident nation should be peculiarly careful not only of the rights but of the susceptibilities of its neighbors; and nowadays all the nations of the world are neighbors one to the other."

It went on: "Courtesy, moderation, and self-restraint should mark international, no less than private, intercourse."

## Political impact
The personnel and rule orders were internal business, of interest to the employee, the department, and the Civil Service Commission, which printed them in its annual reports. The one standing question beneath them ran through every year of the series: whether appointments "without examination" honored or hollowed out the merit system.

The Hanna order was a courtesy to the man who had been his party's national chairman since 1896. Newspapers printed it the day it was issued.

EO 258 was front-page news. The Washington *Evening Star* printed it the day it was signed, under "Absolute Neutrality," calling it an "important and significant executive order" and "of marked significance" as an answer to the Russian belief that the United States was unfriendly to Russia. The Associated Press text ran across the country the next day. From St. Petersburg, an AP dispatch of March 14 reported that the order "has produced a great impression here" and that the *Official Gazette* "accepts it unreservedly as a complete answer to the charges that the American Government is hostile to Russia."

At home it drew some objection. A *Chicago Post* editorial reported that "Some members of Congrss [*sic*], it is reported, criticise the order in so far as it applies to civil employes." The *Post* itself judged that the order's general effect "cannot fail to be decidedly wholesome," and predicted that army and navy officers would have to stop signing the articles they had been writing about the fighting in Korea and Manchuria.

## Legacy
As a group these orders left little behind; most were spent when the appointment was made, or were superseded as the Civil Service Rules were rewritten (EO 253's military-park clause was amended in 1910). Three threads reach forward. EO 258 is a small marker of the neutral posture that, the following year, let Roosevelt mediate between Russia and Japan at Portsmouth, work for which he received the 1906 Nobel Peace Prize. EO 256 is one step in extending the merit system to federal civilian employees overseas.

EO 252 gains interest in hindsight, through the career of Marie Louise Bottineau Baldwin. She served in the Office of Indian Affairs until 1932 and became, in the National Park Service's words, the agency's "highest paid Indigenous woman." In 1914 she became the first Native American graduate of the Washington College of Law. She marched in the 1913 Woman Suffrage Procession and was an officer of the Society of American Indians.

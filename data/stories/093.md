## At a glance
Most of the executive orders of late spring and summer 1907 were routine: clerks appointed without examination, land districts redrawn, military reservations adjusted. Three threads stand out. Executive Order 654 restated the passport rules, making the burden heavier for Chinese American applicants. Two orders set up new administrative bodies: Executive Order 630 paid the members of the Immigration Commission, and Executive Order 651 created a Committee on Grades and Salaries. And three orders reorganized the Panama Canal's purchasing, accounting and Washington offices.

## Background
**The passport rules of 1907.** Executive Order 654, signed June 13, 1907, replaced the 1903 passport rules with fourteen new ones. The basics were plain. Only the Secretary of State could issue passports in the United States, and "he is empowered to refuse them in his discretion." A citizen applied by sworn affidavit, with a one-dollar fee and a personal description. A passport expired "two years from the date of its issuance." Applicants in Porto Rico or the Philippines applied to the Chief Executive of those islands.

Two rules read harshly today. Rule 7 stated that "a married woman's citizenship follows that of her husband so far as her international status is concerned," so a wife's application had to prove *his* citizenship. That language was carried over word for word from 1903. Rule 4 was not carried over unchanged. Under the 1907 rule, a person "of the Chinese race, alleging birth in the United States," had to file supporting affidavits from at least two credible witnesses, "preferably not of the Chinese race." The 1903 rule had stopped at the two witnesses. The 1907 rule added three new demands: the papers had to be filed in duplicate, with three photographs of the applicant, and the applicant had to name in advance the port where he meant to reenter the country. The fact that the burden grew heavier in 1907, not just that it existed, is the real news in this order.

The order rested on Section 4075 of the Revised Statutes, as amended June 14, 1902, which let the President "designate and prescribe" the rules. The next full restatement that has been found is an order of November 13, 1914, whose first rule repeats this order's first rule nearly word for word.

**The Immigration Commission.** Executive Order 630 (May 7) set the pay of the three members the President had appointed to the new Immigration Commission under the immigration act of February 20, 1907: Jeremiah W. Jenks of New York, William R. Wheeler of California and Charles P. Neill of the District of Columbia. Each received $7,500 a year, backdated to their April 1 appointment. For Neill it was a ceiling rather than a raise, since his $7,500 was stated as "including his pay as Commissioner of Labor." The other six members came three from the Senate and three from the House. Chaired by Senator William P. Dillingham of Vermont, the commission would spend four years producing a forty-one-volume report.

**A committee on salaries.** Executive Order 651 (June 11) set up a Committee on Grades and Salaries made up of "one competent employee named by the head of each department and independent establishment." Its job was to prepare "clear and consistent tables of estimates for positions and salaries in conformity with the schedule and recommendations of the Committee on Department Methods of January 4, 1907." In other words, the order put into effect the work of the Keep Commission, the efficiency panel Roosevelt had appointed on June 2, 1905, under Assistant Secretary of the Treasury Charles H. Keep. That commission was working out a way to classify federal jobs by duties instead of by pay. Executive Order 795 of May 13, 1908, carried the work forward.

**Other orders worth a line:**
- **Forest Service and Biological Survey (EO 653, June 13).** Signed the same day as the passport rules, it let these officers and employees accept appointments from states and territories. It's the run's only conservation-related order, and a small piece of the federal-state cooperation Pinchot was building.
- **Terence V. Powderly (EO 644, June 5).** The former Grand Master Workman of the Knights of Labor was appointed, without examination, chief of the Division of Information in the Bureau of Immigration and Naturalization.
- **Fort Bayard's water (EO 637, May 23).** More land in the Gila National Forest, New Mexico, was reserved to protect the post's water supply.
- **Territories (EO 633, May 11).** Communications and reports about certain territories and possessions were routed through the Interior Department.
- **Politics and the civil service (EO 642, June 3, and EO 655, June 15).** Both orders amended the rule on employees' participation in politics, two tries at the same rule within two weeks.
- **Land and reservations.** Lands claimed by the Manila Navigation Company were removed from the Mariveles Military Reservation in Bataan (EO 645, June 7), and the Alaska land districts were redrawn, with a new Juneau district created (EO 635, May 14).
- **The *Zafiro* (EO 632½, May 10).** The vessel was transferred from the Navy Department to the War Department.
- **The Interstate Commerce Commission (EO 633½, May 11).** The commission could hire up to fifteen special agents before July 1 without regard to the civil-service rules. The scramble to staff up shows how fast the Hepburn Act had enlarged its work.
- **The Panama Canal's offices.** Executive Order 663 (July 1) put the purchase of canal supplies under the Chief of Engineers. Executive Orders 664 and 665 (both August 15) reorganized the Commission's accounting and disbursing offices and its Washington office. These three orders are why this run reaches into August. The numbers don't follow date order, and Executive Order 666 of July 15 falls in the next run.

There are no bird reservations in this run. The next two, Tern Islands and Shell Keys, came in August and are told in [The Executive Orders of Late Summer and Autumn 1907](story:94).

## Historical context
By the summer of 1907 the federal government was doing more things, in more places, than its bookkeeping had been built to handle. The passport rules reflected a government newly aware that it had overseas "nationals," residents of Puerto Rico and the Philippines who owed it allegiance without being citizens, and needed one rule for documenting them. The Immigration Commission and the salary committee came from the same pressure.

In his *Autobiography* (chapter X), Roosevelt wrote that he had set the Keep Commission to "find out what changes are needed to place the conduct of the executive business of the Government in all its branches on the most economical and effective basis in the light of the best modern business practice." His letter appointing it laid down nine working principles. The one he found most striking was this: "The existence of any method, standard, custom, or practice is no reason for its continuance when a better is offered."

The canal reorganizations of July and August reflected the largest construction project the nation had ever tried. Its purchasing and disbursing had to be pulled into working order as George Goethals took charge of the works.

## Political impact
None of these orders drew notice on its own. The passport rules were housekeeping. The one thread with a political charge was the reform work behind Executive Order 651. Congress had grown wary of Roosevelt's habit of appointing commissions to study and reshape the executive branch without asking the legislature, believing that such oversight belonged to it.

That resentment came to a head on the last day of Roosevelt's term. The Tawney amendment, section 9 of the sundry civil appropriation act of March 4, 1909, barred the use of appropriated money for any commission, council or board not authorized by law. James A. Tawney of Minnesota chaired the House Appropriations Committee, and in the debate of February 25, 1909, his supporters named their targets: the Council of Fine Arts, the Country Life Commission, the Inland Waterways Commission, "and all the other voluntary commissions appointed by President Roosevelt." The Keep Commission was among them. Roosevelt signed the bill and thought the provision beside the point, since Congress could not stop a president from seeking advice.

The Dillingham Commission's quiet salary order would turn out to matter far more than anyone could see that summer.

## Legacy
Executive Order 654 is a small marker on the road to the modern passport: a two-year document, a one-dollar fee, and rules still openly carrying the discriminatory assumptions of their day. The extra burden on Chinese American applicants fell away only when the Magnuson Act of December 17, 1943, repealed Chinese exclusion.

The marriage rule had a longer, messier end. The Cable Act of September 22, 1922, is usually said to have ended it, and it did break the automatic link in both directions. A woman no longer gained American citizenship by marrying a citizen, and no longer lost it by marrying a foreigner. But the act kept a proviso: "any woman citizen who marries an alien ineligible to citizenship shall cease to be a citizen of the United States." In practice that meant Asian men, the same population rule 4 had singled out for extra proof. That exception was not removed until 1931, and full restoration for the women it had stripped of citizenship came only in 1940.

The Immigration Commission became one of the most consequential investigative bodies of its time. Senator Dillingham presented its abstracts and recommendations on December 5, 1910, the full forty-one volumes carry a 1911 imprint, and the report shaped the restrictive immigration laws of the 1920s. The Committee on Grades and Salaries and the Keep Commission behind it fed, over decades, into the federal job-classification system. The Tawney amendment's ban survives today at 31 U.S.C. § 1346.

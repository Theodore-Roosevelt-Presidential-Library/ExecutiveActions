## At a glance
On June 23, 1908, Roosevelt enlarged the Grand Canyon National Game Preserve, which until then had covered only the Kaibab country north and west of the Colorado River. The new line ran across the canyon and over the Coconino Plateau, taking in the South Rim. Hunting on federal land inside it became a crime except under the Secretary of Agriculture's regulations. Less than a year later, President Taft pulled much of the addition back.

## Background
The game preserve was a year and a half old. Roosevelt had created it on November 28, 1906 ([Grand Canyon National Game Preserve, Arizona](story:31)), taking in "all those lands within the Grand Canyon Forest Reserve, lying north and west of the Colorado River." Its authority was the Act of June 29, 1906 (34 Stat. 607), which let the President designate areas in the Grand Canyon Forest Reserve "for the protection of game animals" and recognize them "as a breeding place therefor." The act's wording matched, word for word, the 1905 law behind [The Wichita Forest and Game Preserve](story:18).

The 1908 proclamation gives a single reason, in its own words: "for the purpose of giving the said Act greater effect." It states no acreage and no metes and bounds. The boundary lives only on the attached Forest Service diagram, which shows the old line along Kanab Canyon on the west and the Colorado River on the south and east. The new line ran south from the plateau, across the canyon, and down over the Coconino Plateau to about Townships 28 and 29 North, taking in the South Rim country and, on the east, a margin of ground across the river. The Grand Canyon National Monument, proclaimed that January ([Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3)), is drawn on the same diagram and lies inside the new line. All of the added ground was already in the Grand Canyon National Forest, as the statute required.

Otherwise this was an ordinary boundary change. The hunting ban, the misdemeanor penalty of up to a thousand dollars or a year in jail, and Forest Service administration stayed as they were, and grazing and other forest uses carried on underneath. The proclamation was countersigned by Alvey A. Adee as Acting Secretary of State.

## Historical context
The idea behind the preserve was a hunter's idea of conservation: set aside a protected "breeding place" where game could multiply and spill over into the surrounding country, safe from hunting and, as people then believed, better off without predators. 1908 was a crowded conservation year, the year of [The Conference of Governors and the National Conservation Commission, 1908](story:28). The Forest Service, which had taken over the reserves from the Interior Department in 1905, was busy redrawing and renaming forest boundaries across the West, and the Grand Canyon diagram is its work.

A Washington notice printed in California papers in early July explained the move. "The extension of the game preserve to include the land south of the Grand Canyon is considered imperative in order to protect the game now in existence," it said. "There are a few deer, wild turkeys and grouse, and occasional small bunches of antelope in the vicinity of Cataract Canyon. This country is gradually becoming more and more accessible to hunters." It added a tourist argument too: the canyon "has become very popular with tourists," and "the protection of the game will add to the interest of tourists, and it is hoped that in the course of time the wild animals may become abundant" (*San Francisco Call*, July 12, 1908). The notice put the addition at "approximately 942,400 acres," a contemporary estimate rather than a surveyed figure. Its uniform wording suggests a government release, but the papers do not say who issued it.

The 1906 preserve had been justified by the Kaibab's isolation and its deer. The 1908 addition reached into different country: the open piñon-juniper and ponderosa tableland of the South Rim, where the Santa Fe Railway had arrived in September 1901 and where tourist traffic, mining claims, and Ralph Cameron's toll on the Bright Angel Trail were already in dispute.

Timing is worth noting. The law allowed game areas only "in the Grand Canyon Forest Reserve," and nine days after this proclamation the Grand Canyon National Forest ceased to exist. A proclamation of July 2, 1908, effective July 1, folded "all of the Grand Canyon National Forest south and east of the Colorado River" into the Coconino National Forest and renamed the rest the Kaibab National Forest. Whether the enlargement was hurried through to beat that change is not known.

## Political impact
The added ground was not a quiet fringe. It was the South Rim, the canyon's railroad terminus and tourist gateway, and already contested ground in 1908. Even so, the enlargement reserved no new land from settlement. The ground already lay inside a national forest, and part of it inside a national monument proclaimed that January; only the hunting rules changed.

About two weeks after signing, the Washington notice explaining the move ran in the *San Jose Mercury* (July 6), the *Tuolumne Independent* (July 7), the front page of the *Salinas Index-Journal* (July 7), the *Long Beach Telegram* (July 8, "EXTENSION OF PRESERVES"), the *Petaluma Daily Morning Courier* (July 10) and the *San Francisco Call* (July 12, "ADDS BIG SECTION TO GAME PRESERVE"). It read as routine government news, not news from Arizona, and the copies read carry no editorial comment. No objection turned up in California papers through the end of July. How the papers nearest the South Rim, such as the *Coconino Sun* and the *Williams News*, covered it has not been checked.

The map kept moving around it. Nine days later the Grand Canyon National Forest itself was divided ([Setting Apart the Coconino and Kaibab National Forests](doc:proc-818)). That July 2 proclamation, countersigned by Acting Secretary of State Robert Bacon, took care to say it was "not intended … to modify the proclamations heretofore issued establishing the Grand Canyon National Game Preserve and the Grand Canyon National Monument, both of which include land embraced in the boundaries of the Coconino National Forest." That was official word, a week after the fact, that the preserve now reached south of the river.

Less than a year later the line was pulled back. On June 3, 1909, President William H. Taft found "that the public good will be promoted by designating a smaller area for the said Game Preserve" (Proclamation 875, countersigned by Secretary of State P. C. Knox). His diagram, now titled "Grand Canyon National Game Preserve within the Kaibab and Coconino National Forests," marked as eliminated the broad southern block of the Coconino Plateau, from roughly Township 31 North down to Township 27 North. It kept the canyon itself, a band of country along the South Rim, and the monument. The 1908 line turned out to be the high-water mark in a short back-and-forth over the preserve's southern edge. How many acres Taft removed is not known.

## Legacy
The Grand Canyon National Game Preserve endured. Its lasting fame comes from the Kaibab deer herd, whose boom and crash, and the long scholarly argument over it, belong to the preserve as a whole ([Grand Canyon National Game Preserve, Arizona](story:31)), not to this boundary change.

After Taft's 1909 cut, the preserve was reduced again when Congress created Grand Canyon National Park in 1919, and once more by President Herbert Hoover's Proclamation 1971 of October 6, 1931. It was never abolished. The game-preserve law survives at 16 U.S.C. §§ 683–687, and the Forest Service still manages the preserve as a designated area of the Kaibab National Forest. The South Rim ground added in 1908 and kept in 1909 lies today largely inside Grand Canyon National Park.

Neither proclamation states an acreage. The only figure found for the 1908 addition is the July 1908 notice's "approximately 942,400 acres," a contemporary estimate rather than a surveyed figure. This particular enlargement leaves little legacy of its own beyond the year when the preserve reached across the canyon.

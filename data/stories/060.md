## At a glance
In May 1905 Roosevelt signed two small land proclamations nine days apart. The first gave the town of Coeur d'Alene, Idaho, 19.45 acres of the abandoned Fort Sherman Military Reservation for a public cemetery, and that ground is still the city's Forest Cemetery. The second returned two square-mile sections in San Diego County to the public domain. They had been reserved for the Capitan Grande Band of Mission Indians, and a settler had been living on part of them for ten years without being able to file a claim.

## Background
**Fort Sherman (Proclamation 545, May 6).** A sundry civil appropriations act of March 3, 1893, let the President grant up to twenty acres of an abandoned military reservation to the town it sat in, for public use. It was the same law behind the [The Fort Marcy Grants to Santa Fe](story:50). Roosevelt used it to withdraw Lot 9 of section 14, township 50 north, range 4 west, Boise Meridian, 19.45 acres of the old Fort Sherman reservation, and grant it to the incorporated town of Coeur d'Alene.

The proclamation names who asked: "the citizens of the town of Coeur d'Alene, Idaho, have petitioned that a portion of the said reservation be granted to the Municipal Authorities of the town of Coeur d'Alene, Idaho, for the use of a public cemetery." It was not raw land. It already held the post's own burying ground, whose oldest marked grave dates to 1881, and the town wanted that cemetery and the land around it for a municipal one. The reservation, created by executive orders between 1879 and 1892 and covering 902.21 acres, had been handed to the Interior Department on October 5, 1900, for disposal under the abandoned-military-reservations act of July 5, 1884.

**Capitan Grande (Proclamation 555, May 15).** This one released Sections 5 and 6 of township 15 south, range 2 east, San Bernardino Meridian, from an 1875 executive order that had reserved them for the Capitan Grande Band or Village of Mission Indians. The commission sent out under the Mission Indian Relief Act of January 12, 1891, had deliberately left those two sections out of the band's selected reservation. Even so, the executive order of December 29, 1891, approving the commission's report froze all such land "from settlement and entry" until a President restored it by proclamation. The band's patent of March 10, 1894, also left the two sections out.

The proclamation states the occasion plainly. On March 10, 1895, Joseph J. Henderson had settled on the southeast quarter of the southeast quarter of Section 5 "for the purpose of taking the land under the homestead law," and he "cannot make the requisite filings on the land occupied by him until it shall have been formally restored to the public domain." The same process had freed neighboring land of the same band in 1902, when Proclamation 476 released Section 7 for the settler Jacob Kuhner ([The Public-Domain Restorations of 1902](story:37)). Who set the 1905 request in motion is not known.

## Historical context
Fort Sherman began as Camp Coeur d'Alene, founded April 16, 1878, on a site General William T. Sherman had recommended after an inspection tour the year before. It became Fort Coeur d'Alene in 1879 and Fort Sherman in 1887. The Idaho State Historical Society gives three reasons for the post: keeping the peace in northern Idaho, protecting railroad and telegraph crews, and guarding the border with Canada. The Coeur d'Alene mining rush came later, in 1883–84, and the troops' mining-related duty came with the labor troubles of 1892.

The main garrison marched out on April 21, 1898, for the Spanish–American War. A small detachment stayed on, filed its last monthly report in July 1900, and moved to Fort George Wright, Washington, in April 1901. That emptying opened the way for the town to claim a piece of the idle reservation.

Coeur d'Alene got its park first, and by act of Congress, not proclamation. A clause in the sundry civil appropriation act of April 28, 1904, set apart twenty acres "on the southeast corner thereof, immediately west of the depot grounds, extending forty rods along the lake front and eighty rods back," and "granted and donated" it to the town "for the use of said municipality as a public park." Before the rest of the reservation was sold at public auction in the summer of 1905, the park and cemetery tracts had been set aside for the city; the Stack and Gibbs Lumber Company bought a large share of what remained. In 1906 Congress gave Kootenai County a strip on the reservation's east side for a public highway.

The California restoration was part of the era's steady housekeeping of the public domain. Lands withdrawn for forest reserves, reservoir sites or Indian purposes and later found unneeded were routinely returned to entry by proclamation.

## Political impact
No public argument over either proclamation has turned up. Both carried out steps that laws and earlier orders had already laid out, and both answered a specific local request: a town's petition in Idaho, a settler's stalled homestead in California.

A search of California newspapers, including the *San Diego Union*, for "Capitan Grande" from May through July 1905 found three items, all about the death of the reservation's schoolteacher, Ellen L. Patterson, and none about the restoration. A search of Washington State newspapers from the spring and summer of 1905 found reports of the Fort Sherman auction under the heading "Fort Sherman Sold" in the *Chewelah Independent* (June 9) and the *Pullman Herald* (June 10), but nothing about the cemetery grant. The Coeur d'Alene papers and the Spokane *Spokesman-Review* were not searched.

## Legacy
The Coeur d'Alene grant is still a cemetery. The tract is today's Forest Cemetery, 1011 North Government Way, run by the city. The city's own history says that in 1905, "by proclamation, President Roosevelt gifted the City of Coeur d'Alene with what is now known as 'Forest Cemetery,'" and the city later bought neighboring land for Riverview Cemetery.

Most of the old post went at the 1905 auction. The college came two generations later: Coeur d'Alene Junior College, founded in 1933 and now North Idaho College, moved to the fort site in 1949 on land donated by the Winton family. The surviving post chapel, built around 1880, is part of the "Fort Sherman Buildings" listing on the National Register of Historic Places (1979).

What happened to the two California sections, and to Henderson's homestead claim, after they were restored is not known.

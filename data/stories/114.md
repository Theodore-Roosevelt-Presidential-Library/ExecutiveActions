## At a glance
Between the end of 1904 and the end of 1908, the government's index of Roosevelt's *unnumbered* executive orders carries just twenty-six entries. They deal with land offices, Army and lighthouse reservations, Canal Zone law, and a pair of civil-service rules. Checked one by one, seventeen of them prove to be numbered orders (or, in one case, a proclamation) listed a second time. Roosevelt was not issuing fewer orders. His filing clerks were catching up with him.

## Background
Most of Roosevelt's executive orders carry a number. A smaller group does not, and those were gathered in 1943 by Clifford L. Lord in a chronological *List and Index of Presidential Executive Orders, Unnumbered Series* (see [The Unnumbered Executive Orders](story:110)). Lord's list carries only twenty-six Roosevelt entries for the last four years of the presidency: thirteen in 1905, two in 1906, five in 1907 and six in 1908. That is a steep drop from twenty-three in 1904 (see [The Unnumbered Orders of 1904](story:113)), and the fall comes between 1905 and 1906.

**Land offices and the West, 1905.**
- **Oregon City, Akron and Ironton.** Three land offices were abolished, each effective June 30, 1905: Oregon City into Portland, Akron (Colorado) into Sterling, and Ironton (Missouri) into Springfield. Lord dates all three March 21, 1905, but only the Oregon City order actually carries that date; he supplied the other two in brackets, his own sign of an inferred date. All three are numbered orders. Oregon City is Executive Order 310-B of March 21. Ironton is Executive Order 321-A of May 1, and Akron is Executive Order 321-F of May 6. A Washington dispatch of May 13 reported that "The President has issued an order abolishing the Land Office at Akron, Colo."
- **Vernal and the Uintah Land District.** On May 1, 1905, Executive Order 321-E, "Establishment of Uintah Land District," made Vernal, Utah, the office of the new district. The act of March 3, 1905 had left the siting of the office to the President, which is why an order was needed at all. The name is *Uintah*, with the *h*, as the act spells it four times over and as the General Land Office spelled it in every report from 1905 to 1911; Lord's entry prints "Uinta." The Commissioner of the General Land Office announced the order in a public notice dated May 9, which said the Vernal office would open on July 1, 1905.
- **Santa Clara Pueblo.** On July 29, 1905, Executive Order 344-B added roughly 32,000 acres to the Santa Clara Pueblo in New Mexico Territory, bringing it to about 49,369 acres.

**The canal, the Navy and the departments.**
- **Bristow's inquiry.** On January 13, 1905, Roosevelt appointed Joseph L. Bristow, then Fourth Assistant Postmaster General, special commissioner to investigate trade and freight rates across the Isthmus and the management of the Panama Railroad.
- **The Commissioner of Railroads.** On February 9, 1905, the duties, records and files of the Commissioner of Railroads moved from the Interior Department to the newer Department of Commerce and Labor. The office itself had been abolished by the act of March 3, 1903, effective June 30, 1904, with its work left at Interior. The transfer did not hold. Seven weeks later, on March 31, Executive Order 313-A revoked it.
- **Notaries.** Notaries public in government employ were barred from taking fees for official notarial work. Lord dates this January 4, 1905, the day newspapers carried it ("No Graft for Notaries"), but it is Executive Order 288½ of December 31, 1904, later amended by Executive Order 319 of March 31, 1905.
- **"U.S.S."** Lord's entry for January 8, 1907 is word for word Executive Order 549 of that date: "the official designation of vessels of war, and other vessels of the Navy of the United States, shall be the name of such vessel, preceded by the words, United States Ship, or the letters U.S.S., and by no other words or letters." (See [The Executive Orders of Early 1907](story:91).)
- **Canal Zone law.** In March 1907 the Canal Zone received two bodies of law by presidential order: rules for executing and recording deeds (March 12, effective April 15) and a Code of Civil Procedure (March 22, effective May 1), signed "Theodore Roosevelt" over the White House dateline.
- **Two civil-service rules.** The order of August 12, 1907 let certain long-serving, meritorious civilian employees overseas be *classified*, not transferred, into the competitive service. It is part of Executive Order 679, which classified A. J. Robinson as train master in the Quartermaster's Department and went on: "In similar cases of exceptional merit, where an employee has had a long and faithful service in a civil capacity beyond the seas … he may be classified by the Department with the approval of the Civil Service Commission." The second, of April 4, 1908, was a Presidential Letter that the Civil Service Commission afterward treated as an executive order. It waived the time limit on reinstatement for a person who lost his health "in line of duty as an incident to heroic conduct in saving life."

**Reservations, 1905–1908.**
- **Alaska telegraph tracts.** Land near Central and at Summit (May 24, 1905) and at Rapids on the Yukon (May 4, 1908) was reserved for the Signal Corps' Washington-Alaska Military Cable and Telegraph System, the line strung between Valdez and Fort Egbert. "Central" was the telegraph station at mile 928, which gave the creek and the settlement their name; it is not the mining camp in the Circle district. Central and Summit are two of the thirteen tracts reserved that day by Executive Order 328-B; Rapids is Executive Order 792.
- **Grass Island, Washington.** Reserved on June 12, 1905 "for purposes of the defense of Grays Harbor" (Executive Order 330-A) and given up nine months later, on March 28, 1906 (Executive Order 432).
- **The Philippines.** Camp Marahui, later Camp Keithley, on Lake Lanao in Mindanao, was established January 19, 1905. Biliran, an island across the strait from Leyte (then the province), was reserved May 13, 1908 by Executive Order 796; the order says only "the Island of Biliran."
- **Hawaii.** A lighthouse reservation was set apart at the Makapuʻu light on Oahu in 1907. The Round Top and Sugar Loaf reservation above Honolulu, Executive Order 978 of November 24, 1908, is the last Roosevelt entry in Lord's list.
- **Indian trust periods.** Two orders of 1906, July 23 and August 28, each extended by ten years the trust period on Oklahoma Territory lands allotted to the Sac and Fox and the Iowa.

**Entries that belong elsewhere.**
- **The Oklahoma "game preserve" of June 2, 1905** is Proclamation 563, which redesignated the Wichita Forest Reserve as a game preserve under the act of January 24, 1905. It is a proclamation, not an executive order, and it says nothing about Fort Sill's water supply (see [The Wichita Forest and Game Preserve](story:18)).
- **The Deschutes and Fremont National Forests, July 1, 1908.** Lord entered the *effective* dates of two numbered orders as if they were separate documents. Executive Order 816, approved June 13, 1908, created the Deschutes, 1,504,207 acres, out of parts of the Blue Mountains, Cascade and Fremont forests. The Fremont had been set apart by proclamation on September 17, 1906, and Executive Order 817 of the same June 13 reorganized it, absorbing the Goose Lake forest. Two later orders of July 14, 1908, Executive Orders 912 and 913, only moved the boundary between the two. (See [The Great Reorganization of the National Forests, June 1908](story:99).)

## Historical context
Why did the unnumbered orders dwindle? Not because Roosevelt slowed down. His output of orders rose sharply in 1907 and 1908. What fell was the unnumbered *residue*, and the reason was a filing practice. Lord explained it plainly: "This numbering was begun by the Department of State in 1907, at which time all of the relatively few Orders then on file in that Department were arranged chronologically and given a number, as was every Order subsequently filed." Whether an order ended up numbered or unnumbered "depends entirely on whether or not the State Department received a copy for its files."

Because the 1907 project reached backward, it swept up older orders as well as new ones, and it caught the most recent years best. That is why so many orders of 1905 and 1906 surface in the numbered series under letter-suffixed "in-between" numbers like 313-A, 321-E, 328-B and 344-B.

It is also why Lord's chronological list cannot simply be counted. The list mixes numbered and unnumbered documents without saying so. Checked row by row against the numbered orders and the proclamations, Lord's 126 Roosevelt entries contain twenty-one numbered documents listed a second time, reaching back to 1902: the order naming a committee to receive Prince Henry of Prussia (Executive Order 162 of January 24, 1902, which Lord enters under February 5), a 1902 order excepting a War Department clerk on White House duty, a 1903 order placing ten appointees in Commerce and Labor, and a 1903 proclamation reserving land in Puerto Rico for naval purposes. In the 1905–1908 stretch alone, seventeen of the twenty-six entries are duplicates. That leaves 105 distinct unnumbered Roosevelt orders across the whole presidency, and only nine of them fall in these last four years: three in 1905, two in 1906, three in 1907 and one in 1908. Alongside 1,006 numbered orders and 407 proclamations, the full body of Roosevelt's executive documents comes to about 1,518.

Some of the wrong dates in Lord trace back to the sources he leaned on. He took the Round Top and Sugar Loaf date, December 1, 1908, from the 1910 War Department compilation of military reservations. Every other witness gives November 24. War Department General Orders No. 200 of December 10, 1908 printed two orders together, Executive Order 978 of November 24 and Executive Order 985 of December 1 (Camp Overton, Mindanao), and the compiler may have taken the date from the wrong one. The General Land Office's own tables for 1907, 1908 and 1910 likewise give May 9, 1905 for the Vernal order, the date of the Commissioner's public notice rather than of the order itself.

## Political impact
Individually these were routine acts, and few drew more than a line or two in the papers. Taken one by one, though, several show the machinery of the government being fitted together.

The canal business is the clearest case. Bristow's 1905 inquiry into Isthmian freight rates and the Panama Railroad was the President's own appointment of a special commissioner. The 1907 Canal Zone deed rules and Code of Civil Procedure came straight from the President because the Isthmian Canal Commission's own power to legislate had ended in March 1905 (see [Governing the Panama Canal Zone: the Executive Orders of 1904](story:14)).

The Commissioner of Railroads transfer is a small study in a new department finding its edges. The shift of the old railroad office's files to the newer Commerce and Labor Department was made and then reversed within seven weeks, and the work stayed at Interior.

The 1908 reinstatement letter had a human story behind it. It arose from the case of Frank Raynor, a Life-Saving Service surfman awarded the gold life-saving medal for rescuing the crew of the schooner *Benjamin C. Cromwell*. Roosevelt's letter noted pointedly that no pension existed for such a man.

On the land, the orders were quieter still: an Army telegraph line threading through Alaska, a defensive post on Grays Harbor taken up and then dropped, a land office placed where Congress had left the choice to the President, and a Pueblo's land base enlarged.

## Legacy
By 1907–1908 the executive order had become a numbered, filed instrument of government. Roosevelt's orders did not slow; his files caught up with him. The last entry in Lord's Roosevelt sequence makes the point on its own: the modest Hawaiian reservation of Round Top and Sugar Loaf is a numbered order, Executive Order 978, sitting in the unnumbered list. The line between the two series was a clerical accident, not a distinction in the President's own practice.

Some of these orders had long afterlives. The Round Top and Sugar Loaf reservation was still being cited by date and number in a 1925 order, in Executive Order 6948 of January 17, 1935, which moved the right-of-way between the two reservations, and in Executive Order 10342 of 1952. The 1908 reinstatement letter for lifesavers survived in the civil-service regulations for decades under the caption "Time limit waived for heroic conduct."

One of Lord's errors grew out of a later document. Taft's Executive Order 1147 of December 15, 1909 reserved about 16,420 acres for Fort Sill's water supply and described land "partly within the Game Preserve made by Executive Order of June 2, 1905." Lord appears to have read that 1909 description back onto the 1905 Wichita proclamation, and in the process turned a proclamation into an executive order.

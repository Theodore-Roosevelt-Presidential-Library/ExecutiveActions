## At a glance
In May 1902 about 147,000 anthracite miners in eastern Pennsylvania walked off the job, and by fall the Northeast faced a winter without heating coal. Roosevelt signed no order. Instead he called both sides to the White House, readied a plan to have the Army run the mines, and brokered a settlement that sent the dispute to a presidential commission. It was the first time the federal government stepped into a major strike as an umpire speaking for the public rather than as a strikebreaker.

## Background
Anthracite, the clean-burning "hard" coal that heated most homes and boilers in the eastern United States, came almost entirely from a few hundred square miles of eastern Pennsylvania. The mines there were owned by a handful of railroads and the financiers behind them. On one side stood the United Mine Workers of America and its 32-year-old president, John Mitchell. On the other stood the coal roads, whose public voice was George F. Baer of the Philadelphia & Reading. The miners wanted higher pay, an eight-hour day, honest weighing of the coal they were paid by the ton to cut, and recognition of their union. The operators would not negotiate at all.

The strike began on May 12, 1902. Engineers, firemen and pumpmen were ordered out separately, effective June 2. In the words of the commission's later report, "nearly the entire body of mine workers, which numbers about 147,000, abandoned their employment, and remained idle until the strike was called off."

Roosevelt first sent the Commissioner of Labor, Carroll D. Wright, to investigate. As winter approached and the operators still refused to move, he summoned both sides to the temporary White House at 22 Jackson Place on October 3, 1902. He was still recovering from the Pittsfield trolley collision of September 3 and leg surgery on September 23, and he received the delegations seated, his left leg on a cushion. He appealed to them in the public interest:

> "I speak for neither the operators nor the miners, but for the general public."

> "With all the earnestness there is in me I ask that there be an immediate resumption of operations in the coal mines in some such way as will, without a day's unnecessary delay, meet the crying needs of the people."

The operators refused and demanded troops to protect strikebreakers instead. Roosevelt then prepared an extraordinary fallback: the U.S. Army would take possession of the coalfields and run the mines. Meanwhile his Secretary of War, Elihu Root, acting with permission as a private citizen so as not to commit the President, worked with J. P. Morgan on a face-saving settlement. Root wrote to Morgan on October 9. The two talked for hours aboard Morgan's yacht *Corsair* in New York on October 11. Morgan pressed the plan on Baer the next day, and on the evening of October 13, Morgan, Robert Bacon and Root laid it before Roosevelt. The operators' arbitration proposal went to the press that night. The White House named the commissioners at 2:20 a.m. on October 16. A miners' convention at Wilkes-Barre voted on October 21 to end the strike, and the men went back to work on October 23.

The commission heard the case through the winter. Its award, dated March 18, 1903, and made public March 21, was more particular than it is usually remembered:
- **Pay:** a ten percent raise for the contract miners only, retroactive to November 1, 1902.
- **Hours:** no across-the-board nine-hour day. Engineers hoisting water and firemen got eight-hour shifts at April 1902 pay. Other hoisting engineers and pumpmen got a five percent raise and Sundays off without loss of pay. All other company men went to a nine-hour day at their old ten-hour pay, with overtime. Contract miners, paid by the piece, got no hours award.
- **Board of Conciliation:** three members from each side, with deadlocks going to an umpire named by a Third Circuit judge, and no strike or lockout while a dispute was pending.
- **Sliding scale:** wages tied to the New York Harbor price of white-ash coal above $4.50 a ton.
- **Term:** the award ran to March 31, 1906.
- **The union:** no recognition, though "no person shall be refused employment, or in any way discriminated against, on account of membership or nonmembership in any labor organization."

## Historical context
To understand why intervention seemed necessary, start with the cold. Anthracite was how the Northeast kept from freezing. As the strike dragged on, schools and hospitals rationed fuel, and newspapers warned of a winter of suffering in the cities. Before the strike, eastern retail coal ran about $5 to $6 a ton. By September 20 the *Evening Star* in Washington reported $13 to $15 a ton under the headline "COAL PRICES SOARING." By October 8 the *New-York Tribune* reported anthracite "selling at $20, $25, $30 and $35 a ton," while admitting that "there was practically no supply of anthracite and therefore no price for it." Coal cost three to six times its normal price, and by October it was nearly impossible to buy at any price.

Roosevelt's private dread shows in a letter marked "Personal" to Senator Marcus A. Hanna, written the evening of the White House conference, October 3, 1902: "A coal famine in the winter is an awful ugly thing, and I fear we shall see terrible suffering and grave disaster."

Federal power had entered labor disputes before, almost always on the employers' side. Andrew Jackson sent regulars to the Chesapeake and Ohio Canal in 1834, though that trouble was violence between rival laborer crews rather than a strike. Rutherford Hayes sent troops in 1877 at governors' requests. Grover Cleveland sent them to Chicago during the Pullman strike in 1894, over the Illinois governor's express objection. Some peaceful federal machinery already existed, including the Erdman Act of 1898, which allowed mediation of railroad strikes but had never been used. Roosevelt's step was to bring both sides of a private dispute to the table himself and produce a commission each of them accepted. He was not strictly neutral, either: he blamed the operators, and he had a general standing by.

His legal footing was thin, and he said so. Attorney General Philander Knox told him repeatedly that he had no right to mediate in this way, and Roosevelt's October 3 statement admitted it: "I disclaim any right or duty to intervene in this way upon legal grounds or upon any official relation that I bear to the situation." He knew what the mine-seizure plan meant. In a confidential letter of October 22, 1902, to Governor Winthrop Murray Crane of Massachusetts, he recalled telling Knox and Root that if he had to act, he "would proceed to take action which I outlined to them, explaining that I knew that this action would form an evil precedent, and that it was one which I should take most reluctantly…"

The operators held a view that was then widely respected: the mines were private property, dealing with a union meant surrendering the right to manage, and the president had no business in the matter. Baer put it in writing on July 17, 1902, replying to William Freeland Clark, a photographer, who had urged him, as a Christian, to settle. In the original letter the sentence reads: "The rights and interests of the laboring man will be protected and cared for - not by the labor agitators, but by the Christian men to whom God in His infinite wisdom has given the control of the property interests of the country, and upon the successful Management of which so much depends." The letter reached the newspapers on August 20, in a reworded wire version. Baer never wrote the words "divine right"; editorial writers supplied the label. That one sentence did more to turn public sympathy toward the miners than any speech Mitchell gave.

## Political impact
The intervention broke precedent, and people at the time knew it. Jonathan Grossman, a later chief historian of the U.S. Department of Labor, reported from the draft in Roosevelt's papers that the President struck a line from his October 3 statement saying that "no precedent of interference in strikes will be created." No other historian reports the deletion, so it rests on Grossman's account alone.

To the operators and to conservative opinion, the danger was plain. A president who could call capital and labor to the White House as equals, and who stood ready to send the Army into private mines, had claimed a power the Constitution nowhere gave him. Knox and much of the legal profession thought the seizure plan indefensible. To organized labor, the same act was a deliverance: for the first time, the federal government had been used to bring an employer to the table rather than to break a strike.

Roosevelt was careful never to give the union what the operators most feared. The settlement withheld recognition of the United Mine Workers, and the award granted a smaller raise and a longer day than the miners had asked for. Mitchell chose to call the new Board of Conciliation a victory anyway.

Even the makeup of the commission captured the era's contradictions. The Root–Morgan proposal of mid-October named five kinds of men, with no place for labor, and that gap is what forced the "sociologist" workaround on October 15–16. One category read "A man of prominence eminent as a sociologist." Roosevelt took them at their word. The White House statement of October 16 named:
- Brigadier General John M. Wilson, retired, as an engineer officer;
- E. W. Parker, as an expert mining engineer;
- Judge George Gray of Wilmington, as a federal judge;
- E. E. Clark of Cedar Rapids, grand chief of the Order of Railway Conductors, "as a sociologist, the President assuming that for the purpose of such a commission the term sociologist means a man who has thought and studied deeply on social questions and has practically applied his knowledge";
- Thomas H. Watkins of Scranton, as a man acquainted with the mining and selling of coal.

Roosevelt added Bishop John L. Spalding of Peoria on his own authority. Carroll D. Wright served as recorder and later became a seventh member with both sides' consent.

Roosevelt enjoyed the maneuver. In his *Autobiography* (1913), chapter XIII, he wrote that "I appointed the labor man I had all along had in view, Mr. E. E. Clark, the head of the Brotherhood of Railway Conductors, calling him an 'eminent sociologist'—a term which I doubt whether he had ever previously heard." (Clark's union was in fact the Order of Railway Conductors, and the official announcement said "as a sociologist.")

## Legacy
The 1902 strike is generally marked as the first time the federal government stepped into a major labor dispute as something other than a strikebreaker: as an umpire claiming to speak for the public.

The era also gave Roosevelt the phrase that would name his domestic program. He used it at City Hall in Lynn, Massachusetts, on August 25, 1902, weeks before the White House conference: the law, he said, "must be framed and so administered as to secure justice for all alike— a square deal for every man, great or small, rich or poor." He never applied the phrase to the strike itself. It became his signature on the 1903 western tour: at the Grand Canyon on May 6 ("All I ask is a square deal for every man"); at Butte, Montana, on May 27 ("acting upon the principle of giving a square deal to each and every man"); and at Syracuse on Labor Day, September 7.

The episode also fed what scholars call Roosevelt's "stewardship theory" of the presidency. He called it "the Jackson-Lincoln theory," and in chapter XIII of the *Autobiography*, just before telling the strike story, he put it this way: "it is the duty of the President to act upon the theory that he is the steward of the people, and that the proper attitude for him to take is that he is bound to assume that he has the legal right to do whatever the needs of the people demand, unless the Constitution or the laws explicitly forbid him to do it."

The strike added momentum to the push for a cabinet voice for labor, though it did not start it. Roosevelt had proposed a cabinet secretary covering commerce, industry and labor in his First Annual Message in December 1901. Congress created the Department of Commerce and Labor on February 14, 1903, while the commission was still holding hearings. A separate Department of Labor followed on March 4, 1913, signed by President Taft on his last day in office. Its first secretary, William B. Wilson, had been secretary of the 1902 Wilkes-Barre convention that ended the strike.

What the settlement did not deliver is part of the record too. The union won no recognition. The raise went to the contract miners, the shorter day went to the company men, and some classes of workers got neither. The harder questions about union rights would be fought over for another generation.

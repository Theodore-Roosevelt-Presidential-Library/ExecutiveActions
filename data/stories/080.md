## At a glance
Most of Roosevelt's remaining 1902 orders kept the merit system and the foreign service running: rule amendments, consular regulations, and one-off exceptions for a telegrapher, a cable engineer, a doctor facing smallpox. One stands out. On October 18, 1902, in the middle of a congressional campaign, Roosevelt published an Attorney-General's opinion condemning fund-raising circulars that Republican committees in Pennsylvania and Ohio had mailed to federal officeholders, and warned every federal employee that the law against such solicitation would be "obeyed and enforced."

## Background
These orders fill in 1902 around [The Civil-Service Orders of Early 1902 and the "Gag Order"](story:78) and [The Passport Orders of July 1902](story:79). They fall into four kinds:

- **General changes to the Civil Service Rules:** EOs 168, 169, 172, 176, 177, 178, 179 and 192, plus EO 173, which declared how an existing rule was to be read.
- **Consular Regulations:** EOs 167, 170 and 194.
- **Standing exceptions by position**, made by amending the rules: a confidential clerk to the New York immigration commissioner (183), an immigrant inspector and law clerk in the same office (184), a solicitor to the collector of the port of New York (186), and temporary Marine-Hospital quarantine staff (187).
- **Special orders** for a single vacancy, person or work force: EOs 174, 175, 185, 188, 189, 191, 195 and 196. Only five name anyone: Alexander C. Caine (175), Raplie M. Cheshire (188), David Lynch (189), F. L. Templeton (195) and Dr. Charles W. Newton (196).

The rule changes adjusted how the merit system worked day to day:

- A person "blanketed in" when his position was classified could no longer transfer elsewhere without passing the entrance examination (168).
- A reinstated employee had to serve six months before transferring (169).
- Navy-yard special mechanics, inspectors and the Navy's draftsmen were placed under the Navy Department's own labor regulations and tests of fitness, replacing EO 155 (172).
- Aliens who had declared their intention to become citizens could sit the examination for copperplate map engraver, because the Commission "had failed to secure a sufficient number of qualified persons" who were citizens (176). EO 204 revoked it on February 19, 1903.
- Unclassified laborers would be appointed under regulations agreed between each department and the Commission (178).
- Employees of the military government in Cuba who had been commended for service "under conditions of personal danger from yellow fever and other tropical diseases" could be kept on in the classified service (179).
- The rule apportioning Government Printing Office appointments among the states by population was struck out (192).

EO 177 had an edge to it. A promotion recommendation now counted only if it came from the employee's own supervisors. An outside recommendation made "with the knowledge and consent of the employee" was grounds to deny the promotion, and a second offense was grounds for removal. The consular amendments covered currency certificates on invoices (167), consuls accepting appointments from foreign states as administrators, guardians or other fiduciaries (170), and whether gold or silver bullion counted as money or currency (194).

The special orders show why the device existed:

- EO 185 let a War Department clerk be appointed without examination and detailed to the White House, because the post called for "an expert telegrapher" with qualifications "which could not be determined by competitive examination."
- EO 189 sent David Lynch to Manila as cable engineer for the Signal Service, since no register existed and no one would take temporary service in the Philippines.
- EO 196 let Dr. Charles W. Newton be appointed physician at the Western Navajo Agency, Arizona, without examination, because "a threatened epidemic of smallpox requires the immediate services of a physician."
- EO 188 had the Commission reissue a certificate to Raplie M. Cheshire, who had turned down a Government Printing Office post in the mistaken belief that his Census Office job would last.

A few orders were of a different kind:

- **EO 171** (April 25) forbade diplomatic and consular officers to attack, prefer charges against, or publicly criticize one another, except in a confidential communication to the Department of State. Its preamble said that publishing such charges "without an opportunity being given for due consideration of both sides" had led "to injustice to the persons attacked and to embarrassment to the Department of State." Secretary of State John Hay sent it to every diplomatic and consular officer the next day.
- **EO 173** (May 29) settled a running dispute over the 1897 rule that removals from the competitive service be "for just cause." The term meant "any cause, other than one merely political or religious, which will promote the efficiency of the service," and nothing in the rule required "the examination of witnesses or any trial or hearing." The Commission itself had asked for the order. Appointing officers, it explained, had been afraid to remove useless employees for lack of proof of willful misconduct, and a trial requirement "would give a permanence of tenure in the public service quite inconsistent with the efficiency of that service."
- **EO 190** (September 10) closed the executive departments, the Government Printing Office, and the navy yard and station at Washington for the Grand Army of the Republic parade of October 8, 1902: "It is hereby ordered that the several executive departments, the government printing office and the navy yard and station at Washington be closed on that day." The purpose was to let Grand Army veterans in the public service march, "and that all others may unite with the citizens of the District of Columbia in showing honor to the Union soldiers and sailors."
- **EO 193** (October 18) is the political-solicitation order.
- **EO 194-A** (December 8) is catalogued as *Duties of Secretary to the President Defined*, but its text has not been found.

## Historical context
EO 193 shows the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883 being enforced in a live partisan fight. Section 11 of the act forbade federal officers and employees, and also Senators, Representatives and the employees of either House, to solicit or receive political contributions from anyone on the federal payroll. Section 15 made a violation a crime. The core of the rule was older still: an act of August 15, 1876 had already barred federal employees from requesting, giving or receiving political money from one another. The aim was to end the spoils-era habit of "assessing" officeholders a share of their salaries at campaign time.

In the fall campaign of 1902, the Republican state committees of Pennsylvania and Ohio mailed fund-raising circulars to federal officeholders. The Pennsylvania letter was signed by Senator Matthew S. Quay as chairman, with W. R. Andrews, clerk to the Senate Committee on Immigration, as secretary. When the Civil Service Commission objected, the committee reissued the identical letter over its treasurer's signature, leaving Quay's and Andrews's names on the heading. When the Commission objected again, Andrews contested the ruling. The Ohio circular bore the name of Representative Charles Dick, and the state executive committee "declines or neglects" to withdraw it.

At the Commission's request, Roosevelt sent the correspondence to the Attorney-General, Philander C. Knox, with a note dated October 15. The opinion came back within days. Whatever the wording of such circulars, it held, "the explicit and comprehensive words of the statute" forbidding those officials to solicit, "directly or indirectly," "unquestionably condemn all such circulars." Either they could not go to federal officials, or they could not carry the names of the officers the act covered. Roosevelt issued EO 193 on October 18 to publish the opinion to the whole executive service. He knew this ground well: he had spent six years, 1889 to 1895, on the Civil Service Commission, the body bringing these cases.

His closing paragraph did not leave employees free to give to anyone they liked. An employee might "contribute or not, exactly as he pleases, provided he obeys the sections of the civil service act above referred to." Those sections included section 14, which barred any federal employee from giving political money to another federal officer or to a member of Congress. In practice, a clerk could give voluntarily to a committee run by private citizens, outside a government building, and not otherwise. In an election year, Roosevelt was putting the merit-system rule on record against committees of his own party, one of them chaired by Pennsylvania's Republican boss.

## Political impact
EO 193 answered resistance; it did not head it off. The opinion it publishes records a Senate clerk formally contesting the Commission's ruling and the Ohio committee refusing to withdraw its circular. It names Quay, Andrews, Dick and both state committees outright.

The Commission's report for the year struck an optimistic note. "While some political managers still attempt to evade or violate the law," it said, "the great majority of Federal officials feel neither any obligation to make them nor fear of any personal consequences from their refusal," and "public sentiment warmly supports its efforts."

Newspapers carried the order as a Washington wire story, printing the full text under headlines that read it as a warning. The *Indianapolis Journal* of October 19 ran it as "WARNED BY PRESIDENT / ATTENTION OF OFFICEHOLDERS CALLED TO CIVIL-SERVICE LAW." The *San Francisco Call* headed it "OFFICE HOLDERS ARE ADMONISHED," and the *San Diego Union*'s headline named Knox as the opinion's author. No later story and no published reply from either committee turned up in the papers searched. The Commission's next report recorded the episode only as a ruling — "It is a violation of section 11 of the act for a Federal official to permit his name to appear on circulars addressed to other such officials requesting political contributions" — and listed no investigation or prosecution arising from either circular. The one political-assessment prosecution it reported that year came from Louisville, where a former collector of internal revenue, his deputy and a railroad employee pleaded guilty in March 1903.

The other orders drew little notice. The wire did carry EO 190's text. EO 171 drew no newspaper report that has been found, though the State Department printed it in *Foreign Relations* for 1902.

## Legacy
Most of these orders were routine and were superseded as the Civil Service Rules were rewritten again and again. EO 176 lasted less than eight months. EO 171 stood until Executive Order 8396 revoked it on April 18, 1940.

EO 193 reached furthest. The ban it enforced on soliciting political money from federal workers never lapsed; it descends into today's federal criminal code alongside the Hatch Act of 1939 and the modern rules on federal employees' political activity. It belongs to the same family as Roosevelt's 1907 rule barring competitive-service employees from "political management" ([Barring Federal Employees from "Political Management," 1907](story:16)), whose key sentence he first drafted in a 1902 letter to the Commission.

EO 173's definition of "just cause" framed a long argument over how much protection from removal a merit employee should have. On efficiency grounds, it chose discretion over trial-type hearings. Whether EO 194-A deserves a place in the history of the White House staff cannot be judged until its text is found.

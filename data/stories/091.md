## At a glance
The first executive orders of 1907 were mostly routine government work: appointments, military reservations, lighthouse sites and land-office housekeeping. One of them still shows up every time a Navy ship is commissioned. On January 8, 1907, Roosevelt directed in a single sentence that every Navy vessel be designated by its name, preceded by "United States Ship" or "U.S.S." A coal-land order on the eve of the 1907 forest-reserve fight and a Samoan ship-papers order also stand out from the run.

## Background
This run covers Executive Orders 543 through 576, from January 4 to March 2, 1907. That's thirty-five numbers, counting the fractional Executive Order 554½, but only thirty-four separate orders. Executive Orders 546 and 548 are the same order filed under two numbers. The numbers were assigned after the fact and don't follow signing order. Executive Order 549 is dated January 8, a week before Executive Order 548, and Executive Order 547 (January 11) is earlier than Executive Order 546 (January 15).

**Naming the Navy's ships.** Before 1907 the Navy identified its vessels in no consistent way. Ships were known by type ("U.S. Frigate"), by rig ("United States Barque"), by function ("United States Flag-Ship"), or simply as "the Frigate ___" or "Ship ___." "United States Ship," shortened to "USS," went back to the late 1790s, but it was only one form among several. Executive Order 549 settled the question in one sentence:

> In order that there shall be uniformity in the matter of designating naval vessels, it is hereby directed that the official designation of vessels of war, and other vessels of the Navy of the United States, shall be the name of such vessel, preceded by the words, United States Ship, or the letters U.S.S., and by no other words or letters.

**Ship papers for Samoa.** Executive Order 548 (January 15) authorized the Governor of Tutuila to issue "certificates of protection" to merchant vessels. These were ship's papers, not personal documents. The order granted them "to merchant vessels entitled thereto," and they entitled "the vessels to which they are given to the protection and flag of the United States on the high seas and in all ports." In practice the certificate served as a marine registry for Samoan-owned craft that could not be documented as United States vessels. Personal allegiance came into it only through ownership. The vessel had to belong to a citizen living in Tutuila or Manua, to a native inhabitant who had taken the oath of allegiance, or to a resident from before annexation who gave up his former allegiance and swore the oath. The order speaks of "the Governor of Tutuila." The name "American Samoa" was added later by catalogers.

**Coal land in the forest reserves.** Executive Order 576 (March 2) is known only by its catalog title: "Coal land withdrawals included by proclamations within the limits of forest reserves, not to be excluded from the operation of such proclamations." Starting with the Caribou Forest Reserve on January 15, 1907, and running through the "midnight reserves" of March 1–2, Roosevelt's forest-reserve proclamations carried a coal clause. Here it is in the words of the Little Rockies Forest Reserve proclamation of March 2, 1907:

> not excepting from the force and effect of this proclamation, however, any land within the boundary herein described, which has been withdrawn to protect the coal therein but this proclamation does not vacate any such coal land withdrawal

About forty proclamations in that volume of the *Statutes at Large* carry the clause. Coal-withdrawn land inside the new boundaries went into the forest reserve, and the coal withdrawal stayed in force too, so both rules applied to the same ground. Executive Order 576 was signed the same day as the last batch of proclamations and catalogued in exactly those terms.

**The rest of the run:**
- **Coastal reservations.** Four in Alaska: land at Haines for educational purposes (EO 570, February 21); a military-cable station at Cape Fanshaw, where Frederick Sound meets Fanshaw Bay (EO 573, February 26); and a lighthouse site at Cape Spencer, the headland at the northern entrance to Cross Sound (EO 575, March 2). One in Washington State that's easy to misfile: Viti Rocks, in Bellingham Bay, for lighthouse purposes (EO 561, February 12). Viti Rocks lie off the south end of Lummi Island in Whatcom County. The Wilkes Expedition named them after Viti, or Fiji.
- **Military reservations.** About 32.50 acres were added to Fort Lincoln in Burleigh County, North Dakota (EO 551, January 17). Camp John Hay, near Baguio in Benguet, Luzon, grew by 1,433 acres, more or less (EO 553, January 23). The Sitka building that had been the Governor of Alaska's residence and offices, with its grounds, became a naval hospital (EO 550, January 16). Going the other way, the military reservation of Camp Hartshorne in the Philippines was revoked (EO 543, January 4).
- **Hot Springs.** The regulations of the Army and Navy General Hospital at Hot Springs, Arkansas, were amended on rations and subsistence (EO 552, January 19). Executive Order 574 of March 1 amended them again and set the commuted ration at thirty cents.
- **Marine Corps discharge.** Executive Order 558 (February 4) set the rules for buying a discharge from the Marine Corps in peacetime.
- **A funeral courtesy.** Executive Order 554 (January 25) excused members of the Grand Army of the Republic working in the executive departments at Washington from duty at noon on January 26, for the funeral of Russell A. Alger. The former Secretary of War, then a senator from Michigan, had died at his Washington home on January 24. He was a past commander-in-chief of the Grand Army, which is why the courtesy went to its members. The funeral was held at the house on the 26th, and the Senate adjourned to attend. His remains then went by train to Detroit for burial at Elmwood Cemetery.
- **Housekeeping.** The Watertown, South Dakota, land district was folded into Aberdeen (EO 569). Executive Order 554½ (January 29) made Frank P. Nantz eligible for appointment as deputy collector of internal revenue at San Juan, Puerto Rico, outside the civil-service rules. Other orders covered the Isthmian Canal Commission and the usual appointments and reinstatements outside the competitive rules.

## Historical context
The ship-naming order came from a president who cared about the Navy more than almost any before him. Roosevelt had written *The Naval War of 1812* and, as assistant secretary, had helped get the fleet ready in 1898. By the end of 1907 he would send the Great White Fleet around the world. A single uniform way of naming ships fit the same drive toward a modern, professional service.

The Samoa and coal-land orders each sat inside a bigger story. The first touched the unsettled status of the peoples brought under the flag after 1898. The second belonged to Roosevelt's mineral-conservation campaign, which he pressed in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907. His argument there was about ownership:

> In my judgment the Government should have the right to keep the fee of the coal, oil, and gas fields in its own possession and to lease the rights to develop them under proper regulations; or else, if the Congress will not adopt this method, the coal deposits should be sold under limitations, to conserve them as public utilities, the right to mine coal being separated from the title to the soil.

He set that against what he saw already happening: "In the eastern United States the mineral fuels have already passed into the hands of large private owners, and those of the West are rapidly following." True to the period, he made the case on efficiency and fair price rather than preservation. The fuels "should be conserved and not wasted," he said, and the people protected "against unjust and extortionate prices, so far as that can still be done."

The rest of the run reflects ordinary government business. A government was lighting and wiring a huge northern coast that had suddenly become valuable. The Army's medical service was growing. And the Civil War generation, still filling the government's senior ranks, was getting old.

## Political impact
None of these orders made national news. The ship-naming order was noticed, if at all, only inside the Navy, where it settled a housekeeping question. The coal-land order was the technical plumbing of a conservation fight that was argued in public through proclamations and messages, not through a withdrawal order. The Samoa, lighthouse, military-reservation, hospital and personnel orders were routine and drew no recorded press reaction. The funeral courtesy for Russell A. Alger did reach a newspaper: the Washington *Evening Star* of January 25, 1907, reported the President's instruction the day it was given. The issues of early 1907 that did fill the newspapers were the coming forest-reserve fight (see [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)) and the Japanese immigration crisis, which continues in [The Executive Orders of Spring 1907](story:92).

## Legacy
### "U.S.S."
Executive Order 549 has had a longer life than almost any minor order of the presidency, and the Navy says so itself. The Naval History and Heritage Command's account of ship naming states that "in 1907 President Theodore Roosevelt issued an Executive order that established the present usage," quotes the order in full, and cites it as Executive Order 549 of January 8, 1907. The "USS" in front of the name of every commissioned American warship goes straight back to that one sentence.

### Samoa
Executive Order 548 dealt with ships, not people, but the question of personal status in Samoa never went away. People born in American Samoa today are United States nationals but not citizens, under 8 U.S.C. § 1408 and the definition of "outlying possessions" at 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(29), which names American Samoa and Swains Island. Courts have upheld that status in *Tuaua v. United States* (D.C. Cir. 2015) and *Fitisemanu v. United States* (10th Cir. 2021), and the Supreme Court declined to review it in October 2022. Roosevelt's administration had dealt with a parallel question in its passport orders of July 19, 1902, which carried out the act of June 14, 1902, letting passports go to people who owed allegiance to the United States whether or not they were citizens. Those rules were replaced by Executive Order 235 of September 12, 1903, and again by Executive Order 654 of June 13, 1907 (see [The Passport Orders of July 1902](story:79)).

### Coal, lighthouses and small reservations
The coal-land order fed into the larger mineral-withdrawal program that Congress later ratified in the Pickett Act of 1910 and the Mineral Leasing Act of 1920 (see [The Coal-Land Withdrawals, 1906–1909](story:27)). The Alaska lighthouse site and the military and educational parcels passed quietly into the ordinary administrative record. Viti Rocks, in Washington's Bellingham Bay, are now part of the San Juan Islands National Wildlife Refuge.

## At a glance
On June 15, 1908, Roosevelt set apart as a public reservation every unpatented acre of federal land lying within sixty feet of the boundary with Canada. The goal was practical: to give the government "complete control of the use and occupation of lands abutting on international boundary lines" so customs and immigration laws could be better enforced. It was the northern counterpart of the strip he had reserved along the Mexican border a year earlier.

## Background
Proclamation 810 withdrew from the public-land laws all unpatented public land within sixty feet of the United States–Canada line. Existing valid claims were protected and public highways could cross, but no new private claim, use or occupation could attach. Any future patent touching the strip had to carry a recital that it was issued subject to the proclamation. Like its southern sibling, it reached only federal public land. Where the boundary ran through private, state or long-settled ground, the strip did not attach.

It was a counterpart to the Mexican-border "Roosevelt Reservation" (Proclamation 758, May 27, 1907; see [Closing the 1907 Proclamations: The Mexican Border Strip and Thanksgiving](story:70)), not a copy, and the differences mattered. The 1907 order rested on a general "public welfare" preamble, reserved "all public lands" within sixty feet of the line, and allowed public highways "but for no other purpose whatever." The 1908 order tied the public welfare specifically to customs and immigration enforcement, reached only "unpatented" public lands, and openly contemplated future patents to land "affected by this reservation."

## Historical context
The idea began in Alaska. On October 30, 1907, the British Ambassador at Washington passed along to Ottawa "a proposal by the United States Government that joint action be taken for the reservation of a strip of land sixty feet wide on each side of the Canada-Alaska boundary line under similar conditions to that formerly established along the Mexican boundary line." Canada agreed in an Order in Council of April 14, 1908, reserved its own sixty feet along the Yukon line, and suggested doing the same along other parts of the border. It pointed to the "line houses" east of the St. Lawrence that had been "used for smuggling or for evasion of law." Roosevelt's proclamation answered by applying the strip to the whole northern frontier. A second Canadian Order in Council, of August 7, 1908, recorded the Ambassador's note "that the original proposal for reservation of the Alaska frontier has now been extended so as to include the whole frontier, this being in accordance with the wishes of the Dominion Government."

It was a busy year for the boundary. The United States and Great Britain, acting for Canada, had signed a treaty at Washington on April 11, 1908, for the fuller definition and marking of the line. Ratifications were exchanged on June 4, eleven days before the proclamation, and the treaty was proclaimed on July 1. The survey would mark the line. Roosevelt's strip worked from the land-law side, keeping the ground right along the boundary in federal hands so officers had a margin to patrol.

It also fit his instinct to use the executive withdrawal power freely to hold public land for a public purpose ahead of Congress. The forest reserves rested on an express statute, but this proclamation, like the coal-land withdrawals of 1906–07, cites no statute at all. It rests on the President's asserted general power to withdraw public land.

## Political impact
A one-paragraph withdrawal of a sliver of unpatented land that left existing claims alone raised none of the states'-rights heat the forest reserves did. It drew a little notice as a curiosity, and the notice got the width wrong. A syndicated item in the *Auburn Journal* of Placer County, California, on July 23, 1908, ran under the heading "NOVEL RESERVATION. It Is Thirty Feet Wide and Extends Along Canadian Border." It reported that "by proclamation of President Roosevelt a series of long but extremely narrow reservations of public land has been made along the boundary line between the United States and Canada," called the strip "only thirty feet wide" (the proclamation says sixty), and gave as the reason only the proclamation's own words about customs and immigration. No comment for or against has been found, though the Montana, North Dakota, Minnesota and Maine papers have not been searched.

Its practical reach proved narrower than its wording. Because the proclamation itself expected patents to land "affected by this reservation," claims could still be started on the strip after June 15, 1908, and carried toward patent with only a recital. Four years later President Taft reissued the reservation in stronger terms. His Proclamation 1196 of May 3, 1912, countersigned by Huntington Wilson as Acting Secretary of State, declared the strip "reserved from entry, settlement, or other form of appropriation and disposition under the public-land laws." It protected entries and settlements made before June 15, 1908, and let claims started between that date and May 3, 1912 go to patent "subject to the reservation prescribed in proclamation of the President dated June fifteenth, nineteen hundred and eight." Taft's proclamation gives no reason for the reissue.

## Legacy
The sixty-foot strip along the northern border remains one of the least-known of Roosevelt's reservations, the quiet counterpart to the better-known Roosevelt Reservation on the Mexican border, and today Taft's 1912 proclamation is as much its foundation as Roosevelt's.

It should not be confused with the cleared "vista" cut along the border. That is a separate obligation of the International Boundary Commission under the treaty of February 24, 1925, whose Article IV directs the commissioners "to keep the boundary vistas open" and to carry on that work on a standing basis; Article V lets either country end Article IV on twelve months' notice after six years. The vista comes from the treaty, not from this proclamation.

On the ground the strip is mostly invisible, but it remains a live federal interest wherever the line crosses public domain. Which agency looks after it today, and whether any of it has been transferred, is not known.

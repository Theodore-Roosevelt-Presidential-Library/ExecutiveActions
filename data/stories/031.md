## At a glance
On November 28, 1906, Roosevelt set aside "all those lands within the Grand Canyon Forest Reserve, lying north and west of the Colorado River" as a breeding ground for game. Hunting on the high, forested Kaibab Plateau was banned, while grazing went on. With hunters gone and predators trapped and shot by the hundreds, the plateau's mule deer multiplied. The herd's rise and crash became a textbook lesson in what happens when predators are removed from the land.

## Background
Proclamation 694 (34 Stat. 3263) did not create new public land or move any acreage. It laid a hunting ban over part of an existing forest reserve. The plateau that forms the Grand Canyon's North Rim country stayed a national forest, open to grazing. What changed was that "the hunting, trapping, killing, or capturing of game animals upon the lands of the United States" there became unlawful, "except under such regulations as may be prescribed from time to time by the Secretary of Agriculture."

The legal footing was narrow and specific. On June 29, 1906, Congress had passed "An Act For the protection of wild animals in the Grand Canyon Forest Reserve" (34 Stat. 607). It let the President designate breeding areas inside that one reserve. Violations became a federal misdemeanor, with a fine of up to $1,000 or a year in jail. State and territorial game laws stayed in force on non-federal land. The act copied, almost word for word, the 1905 law behind [The Wichita Forest and Game Preserve](story:18), with a different forest named. The proclamation simply used the power Congress had granted.

No survey and no map were needed. The Colorado River drew the boundary on the south and east, and the forest-reserve line drew the rest. The proclamation gives no acreage. The preserve's boundaries changed several times afterward: Roosevelt enlarged it to the south in June 1908 (see [Enlarging the Grand Canyon National Game Preserve, Arizona](story:76)), a 1909 proclamation reduced it, the new Grand Canyon National Park took a piece in 1919, and Hoover reduced it again in 1931. The often-quoted figure of 1,492,928 acres describes the preserve after 1909, not at its creation.

## Historical context
The preserve grew out of the hunter-conservationism Roosevelt had helped build long before he was President. In December 1887, he and George Bird Grinnell founded the Boone and Crockett Club at a dinner in Roosevelt's New York home. Its members were sportsmen who had watched the great Western herds vanish with the frontier. Their case for game preserves was practical: a protected "breeding place" would let deer, antelope and other animals multiply and spill over into the surrounding country, much as a forest reserve kept a working supply of timber.

The Kaibab suited the idea perfectly. With the canyon on the south and desert on the other sides, the plateau was an island of high, well-watered forest and prime range for mule deer. Its isolation made a hunting ban easy to draw on paper and, in theory, to enforce.

The preserve fit the era's practical brand of conservation, in which game, like timber and water, was a renewable resource to be managed for future use. It also carried an assumption almost no one questioned: protecting "good" game meant killing "bad" predators. Mountain lions, wolves, coyotes and bobcats were treated as vermin. For the next three decades, federal hunters would work to clear them off the Kaibab in the name of the deer.

No statement by Roosevelt about the game preserve has been found from his presidency. (His famous 1903 remarks at the canyon rim were about the canyon itself, three years before the preserve.) His words on the subject come from 1913, after he left office, when he rode the plateau on a cougar hunt with the game warden Jim Owens. In "A Cougar Hunt on the Rim of the Grand Canyon," published in the *Outlook* on October 4, 1913, he laid out the logic of the era plainly: "The chief game animal of the Colorado Canyon reserve is the Rocky Mountain blacktail, or mule, deer. The deer have increased greatly in numbers since the reserve was created, partly because of the stopping of hunting by men, and even more because of the killing off of the cougars." In the same article he described Owens's job: "One important feature of his work is to keep down the larger beasts and birds of prey, the arch-enemies of the deer, mountain-sheep, and grouse." He also explained what such preserves were for: "The preservation of game and of wild life generally—aside from the noxious species—on these reserves is of incalculable benefit to the people as a whole. As the game increases in these national refuges and nurseries it overflows into the surrounding country."

## Political impact
The proclamation drew little notice. It reserved no new land, pushed out no homesteader, and, because Congress had already authorized it by name, set off none of the states'-rights and "lock-up" arguments that met the forest reserves and, later, the [Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3).

**Hunting stopped; grazing went on.** From the day of the proclamation, taking game on the federal land north and west of the river was a federal misdemeanor unless the Secretary of Agriculture's regulations allowed it. Nothing else changed. Roosevelt himself noted in his 1913 *Outlook* article that "This forest and game reserve is thrown open to grazing, as with all similar reserves," and cattle on the Kaibab rose from about 9,000 head in 1906 to more than 15,000 by 1913. Logging was a small affair: sawmills on the plateau cut under a million board feet a year, mostly for local use, until the first North Kaibab timber-management plan in 1948. Large commercial harvest came later. The plateau was remote enough that few outside northern Arizona felt the change right away.

**Predators targeted.** The ban on hunting game came paired with a campaign against the animals that hunted the deer. Government hunters went to work on mountain lions, wolves, coyotes and bobcats; between 1906 and 1931 they trapped or shot 781 lions, 30 wolves, 4,889 coyotes and 554 bobcats on the plateau.

**Boundaries in motion.** The preserve did not keep its first shape for long. Roosevelt enlarged it with ground south of the canyon in June 1908 (see [Enlarging the Grand Canyon National Game Preserve, Arizona](story:76)), and that proclamation, unlike the first, carried a map. A proclamation of June 3, 1909, under President Taft, cut it back. The friction that came later was local and slow: stockmen who ran cattle and sheep on the plateau spent years arguing over how much grass the growing deer herd left for their animals. In the national politics of late 1906, though, the game preserve was a small, uncontested act in a very busy conservation season.

## Legacy
**The size.** The often-repeated figure of 1,492,928 acres belongs to the preserve after the 1909 cut, not at its creation, and Roosevelt himself used it correctly. In his *Autobiography* (1913), listing the wildlife measures of his presidency, he included "The passage of the Act of June 29, 1906, providing for the establishment of the Grand Canyon Game Preserve of Arizona, now comprising 1,492,928 acres." The word "now" dates the figure. The preserve shrank again when Congress created Grand Canyon National Park in 1919, and once more under President Hoover's proclamation of October 6, 1931.

**A cautionary tale.** This quiet proclamation became one of the most-cited episodes in American wildlife management, as a warning. With hunting banned and predator control underway, the Kaibab deer herd grew sharply. Counting the government hunters and those who came after, 816 mountain lions, 30 wolves, 7,388 coyotes and 863 bobcats were taken from the plateau between 1906 and 1939, according to D. Irvin Rasmussen's 1941 study; after 1931, lions were hunted for sport and coyotes and bobcats for fur. By the early 1920s, in the standard telling, the deer had outgrown what the range could feed, and hard winters on stripped-bare range killed them in large numbers. For decades ecology textbooks used the herd's rise and crash to show what happens when predators are removed.

**Leopold and the Kaibab.** Aldo Leopold made the Kaibab a case study, though not quite as the popular version has it. In *Game Management* (1933), in "Deer Irruptions" (1943), and in a 1947 survey of overcrowded deer ranges written with Sowls and Spencer, he treated it as data, naming a "Kaibab-Sequence": "It is assumed that the sequence of events first clearly recognized on the Kaibab, and later repeated in many localities in many states, represents the 'normal' sequence of events in an irrupting herd." His famous essay "Thinking Like a Mountain" is a different matter. It names no place, and the wolf whose "fierce green fire" dies in it was shot on Arizona's Apache National Forest in September 1909. There is no evidence Leopold ever visited the Kaibab; as late as 1942 he was still writing to others to ask what had happened there.

**Second looks.** Later scholars questioned the neat story. The famous curve was sketched freehand through four points. The starting figure of about 4,000 deer was a forest supervisor's guess, and the peak of 100,000 was never counted: Forest Service estimates for the 1920s topped out at 30,000, and 100,000 was the highest number offered by local ranchers, later adopted by the supervisor when he revised his own agency's figure after the fact. Reviewing the record in *Ecology* in 1970, the ecologist Graeme Caughley noted that "the population in 1924 was variously estimated as 100,000, 70,000, 60,000, 50,000 and 30,000," and judged the case "unlikely to teach us much about eruption of ungulate populations." The historian Christian C. Young reached a similar verdict on the record in *In the Absence of Predators* (2002). More recent work lands in the middle. In 2006 Binkley and colleagues found that while the numbers were "crude and variable," other evidence, including the age of the plateau's aspen, points to a real and major deer irruption in the late 1910s and 1920s, shaped by drought, livestock grazing and fire suppression as well as predator removal.

What is not in doubt is that the preserve helped drive a deer irruption that damaged the range and killed deer in large numbers, and that the lesson drawn from it, that predators belong in a healthy landscape and are not just competition to be wiped out, helped turn American wildlife policy away from wholesale predator control.

**Still on the books.** The preserve itself was never abolished. Its provisions survive in federal law at 16 U.S.C. §§ 683–687, and the Forest Service still runs it. When Reorganization Plan No. II of 1939 moved the Wichita preserve to the Interior Department, it left the Grand Canyon preserve with Agriculture. The Kaibab National Forest's 2014 plan lists the Grand Canyon Game Preserve as a designated area, closed to locatable mineral entry, with the goal that it "provides quality habitat for game animals." Most of the plateau lies in the forest's North Kaibab Ranger District, with Grand Canyon National Park at its southern end, Bureau of Land Management land on both flanks, and the Ancestral Footprints of the Grand Canyon National Monument, proclaimed in 2023, reaching to its edge. Arizona Game and Fish manages the wildlife under a cooperative agreement, and the North Rim deer herd is still actively managed today.

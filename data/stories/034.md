## At a glance
On July 4, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed "a full and complete pardon and amnesty" for Filipinos who had fought against Spanish and then American rule. The proclamation said the insurrection "is now at an end," except in the Moro country of the southern islands, which it left out entirely. The same day the Army's military governorship of the islands was ended, and civil government took over. Roosevelt called it, from a platform in Pittsburgh that day, "the declaration establishing peace in the Philippines."

## Background
The proclamation reached back across two wars. Its first recital covers the revolt against Spain that began in August 1896, and the resistance to American sovereignty that followed Spain's cession of the archipelago. Its operative clause pardons everyone in the islands who took part in those insurrections, or gave "aid and comfort" to those who did, for "treason or sedition" and for offenses "political in their character" committed under insurrectionary orders or growing out of political feuds among Filipinos, or between Filipinos and Spaniards.

The pardon came with limits:

- **Serious crimes.** It did not reach anyone already convicted of murder, rape, arson, or robbery, or anyone who had committed crimes since May 1, 1902, in a province where civil government was already running. People in those classes could still apply for individual clemency, which, the proclamation promised, "will be liberally extended."
- **Property.** It left untouched the government's title to property taken "by way of confiscation or otherwise."
- **An oath.** Anyone claiming the pardon had to swear to "recognize and accept the supreme authority of the United States of America in the Philippine Islands."
- **The Moro exception.** The second recital excludes "the country inhabited by the Moro tribes, to which this proclamation does not apply." American forces were still fighting there.

The wording matters. The document never uses the word "war"; it says "insurrection" throughout, which was the American government's legal position. And it does not declare peace in its operative clause. It *recites* that peace has come, in a "Whereas," and then grants pardon and amnesty, and nothing else.

The proclamation has no official title. The *Statutes at Large* print it as "[No. 29.]" of volume 32 (32 Stat. 2014–2015), under a plain "A Proclamation," with only the printer's marginal note, "Amnesty to Philippine insurrectionists." The number 483 belongs to the later State Department series. It was issued under the Great Seal and countersigned by Secretary of State John Hay, as every proclamation of the period was.

The War Department also published the amnesty to the troops, because the Army would carry it out in the field. That soldier's version, issued as General Orders No. 69 on July 7, 1902, is set in War Department style: no seal, "Given under my hand," and "By the President: ELIHU ROOT, Secretary of War." It is that version, not the proclamation itself, that most modern collections reprint.

Two separate Army orders went out on July 4 alongside it. General Orders No. 67 relieved "the general commanding the Division of the Philippines," Maj. Gen. Adna R. Chaffee, of his duties as military governor and declared that "the office of military governor in said archipelago is terminated." General Orders No. 66 thanked the Army in the Philippines "for the courage and fortitude, the indomitable spirit and loyal devotion with which they have put down and ended the great insurrection which has raged throughout the archipelago against the lawful sovereignty and just authority of the United States." It was to be "read aloud at parade in every military post on the 4th day of July, 1902, or on the first day after it shall have been received."

The amnesty came the same week as the Philippine Organic Act, approved July 1, 1902 (32 Stat. 691–712). That act did not start elective government right away. A census could be ordered only once the insurrection had ceased and peace was certified, and a Philippine Assembly would follow only "two years after the completion and publication of the census." With the military governorship ended, authority rested with the civil Philippine Commission, which William Howard Taft had led as civil governor. Taft was away that summer, in Rome on July 3 for talks over the friar lands. Luke E. Wright was acting civil governor.

## Historical context
The fighting Roosevelt was pronouncing finished had been long, bloody, and bitterly argued over at home. It grew straight out of the war with Spain. Under the Treaty of Paris, Spain ceded the Philippines to the United States. The Filipino independence movement led by Emilio Aguinaldo had expected liberation, not a new ruler, and fighting broke out at Santa Mesa, Manila, on February 4, 1899. Conventional battles gave way to guerrilla war. Aguinaldo was captured at Palanan, Isabela, on March 23, 1901, organized resistance came apart over the following year, and by mid-1902 the administration judged the islands pacified except among the Muslim Moros.

The proclamation's language carries the era's governing idea about the whole enterprise: that American rule was a "beneficent" trust held for the Filipinos' own good. That idea was under heavy fire even as the document was signed. The war had produced widely reported atrocities on both sides, including the "water cure" and the reprisals on Samar that followed the attack at Balangiga on September 28, 1901. On Samar, General Jacob H. Smith's order, remembered in the phrase "a howling wilderness," entered the public record in April 1902 through sworn testimony at the courts-martial that followed. A vocal anti-imperialist movement attacked the war as a betrayal of the country's founding principles.

The Senate's Committee on the Philippines, chaired by Senator Lodge, had taken testimony on the conduct of the war from January 31 to June 28, 1902, closing six days before the proclamation. Its atrocity testimony had clustered in April and May.

Against that backdrop, the choice of the Fourth of July, and the pairing of amnesty with a sweeping tribute to the Army, framed a disputed colonial war as a finished and honorable achievement, closed with mercy.

## Political impact
Roosevelt drew the connection to the date himself. Speaking in Pittsburgh on July 4, he said: "It is most appropriate that on this Fourth of July, this anniversary of the birth of the nation, it should be our good fortune to have promulgated the declaration establishing peace in the Philippines." With midterm elections coming that November, the announcement let the administration and its allies say the war was won and the killing over, and the tribute read at every Army post turned the news into a celebration of the soldiers.

The press carried it the same day. The proclamation ran on page one in papers across the country on July 4, and within three days at least twenty-seven papers, from the *New-York Tribune* and Washington's *Evening Star* to the *Saint Paul Globe*, *Omaha Daily Bee*, *Richmond Dispatch*, and *Birmingham Age-Herald*, had printed the full text.

Reaction split along the lines the war had already drawn. Washington's *Evening Star* judged the next day that imperialism as an issue had been tested across the country and that the administration's supporters had reason to be satisfied, and it treated the Moros as the exception that proved the rule. William Jennings Bryan's *The Commoner* charged that Roosevelt had seized on the Fourth of July for political effect, and set the item beside a prediction about the November elections. George S. Boutwell, president of the American Anti-Imperialist League, wrote that the struggle would go on regardless of the proclamation of peace.

To critics who had spent months documenting the water cure and the Samar reprisals, the Army order's picture of a campaign marked by humanity and kindness read as a whitewash, and the claim that peace reigned ran into the plain fact that the proclamation itself carved out the Moro country, where fighting had not stopped. Supporters answered that the abuses were the isolated acts of a hard guerrilla war, already being punished, and that amnesty and civil government were how a responsible power ought to close such a conflict. Both readings hung together on their own terms. What is certain is that the proclamation marked the formal handover of the Philippines from military to civil colonial rule.

George S. Boutwell is the only named anti-imperialist known to have answered this particular proclamation. Some of the best-known anti-imperialist protests of 1902 are sometimes linked to it, but they belong to other moments: George F. Hoar's "You have devastated provinces" speech came in May, before the proclamation, and *Secretary Root's Record*, by Moorfield Storey and Codman, answered a Roosevelt speech at The Weirs at the end of August.

The reckoning over Samar continued after the Fourth. Brig. Gen. Jacob H. Smith had been tried by court-martial from April 24 to May 3, 1902, on a charge of conduct prejudicial to good order and military discipline, not murder. He was found guilty and sentenced to be admonished by the reviewing authority. On July 14, 1902, ten days after the amnesty, Roosevelt's review of the case directed that he be retired.

In Manila, the new civil order ran without its best-known figure for most of that summer. Taft did not return to the islands until August 22, and Luke E. Wright served as acting civil governor in the meantime.

## Legacy
### Civil government
The practical legacy was the shift the proclamation sealed. From 1902 the Philippines were governed as a civil colony under the Philippine Commission. The title of the man at its head changed from "Civil Governor" to "Governor-General" by an act of February 6, 1905, long after Taft had left the islands; Luke E. Wright was the first to hold it. The elected Philippine Assembly promised in the Organic Act was inaugurated on October 16, 1907. The road ran on through the Philippine Autonomy Act, known as the Jones Act, approved August 29, 1916, and the Philippine Independence Act, known as the Tydings-McDuffie Act, approved March 24, 1934 (48 Stat. 456), to independence.

### The Moro country
Roosevelt's "peace" was also incomplete, by the proclamation's own terms. The Moro country he exempted saw years more fighting. At Bud Dajo in March 1906, roughly 800 to 1,000 Moros were killed in the crater of the mountain, with six survivors. Maj. Gen. Leonard Wood ordered the expedition on March 2 and was the senior officer present, while Col. Joseph W. Duncan of the 6th Infantry commanded in the field. Wood accepted full responsibility. He and Governor-General Ide gave explanations for the deaths of women and children that could not both be true, and that gap fed charges of a cover-up. The battle of Bud Bagsak, June 11–15, 1913, where Pershing commanded in person as a brigadier general, is widely treated as the last major action, though smaller clashes went on until a last one at Talipao that October. Historians date the start of the Moro conflict variously, from 1899, 1901, or 1902.

### The Fourth of July
The Fourth of July had a long afterlife in the Philippines. When the United States recognized Philippine independence, it chose July 4, 1946 (Proclamation 2695). Filipinos marked Independence Day on the Fourth until 1962, when President Macapagal proclaimed June 12 a holiday for that year, and a 1964 law made June 12 Independence Day permanently while keeping July 4 as Philippine Republic Day. July 4 was later observed as Philippine-American Friendship Day, until the Administrative Code of 1987 dropped it from the list of holidays.

### The document
The amnesty document itself is a footnote. The moment it marks, the formal end of the insurrection as Washington reckoned it and the start of American civil government in the islands, is not.

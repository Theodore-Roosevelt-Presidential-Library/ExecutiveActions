## At a glance
On July 19, 1902, at Sagamore Hill, Roosevelt signed three orders that put a new act of Congress to work. The act, approved June 14, 1902, let passports go to people who owed allegiance to the United States without being its citizens: the residents of Puerto Rico, the Philippines and Guam. The orders built the machinery: one code of passport rules for applications made in the United States, a matching code for applications made in the islands, and a rule letting American diplomats and consuls abroad issue passports to island residents who could prove their loyalty.

## Background
The three orders do one job in three pieces. The change in the law came from Congress. The act of June 14, 1902 (32 Stat. 386) amended three sections of the Revised Statutes:

- **Section 4075** gained the words "and by such chief or other executive officer of the insular possessions of the United States," so the governors of the islands could issue passports.
- **Section 4076** was rewritten to read: "No passport shall be granted or issued to or verified for any other persons than those owing allegiance, whether citizens or not, to the United States."
- **Section 4078**, the penalty clause, now set up to a year in prison or a $500 fine for anyone not lawfully authorized "under the United States, its possessions, or any of the States" who issued a passport, and for any consular officer who knowingly issued one to a person not owing allegiance.

The President's power to set passport rules was not new. The words "under such rules as the President shall designate and prescribe" had stood in section 4075 since the act of August 18, 1856.

The bill came from the executive branch. A wire dispatch from Washington dated February 15, 1902 reported that because residents of Porto Rico and the other possessions "are not citizens of the United States," the House Committee on Foreign Affairs had acted favorably on a bill, credited to Attorney General Knox, letting passports issue to them "the same as citizens." In the House it was carried as "the bill of Representative Adams of Pennsylvania."

The orders divided the work by where the applicant stood:

- **EO 181** laid down seventeen standing rules for passports applied for inside the United States. Only the Secretary of State could grant them. The applicant gave a sworn application, a physical description down to forehead, nose and chin, the oath of allegiance, and a witness's certificate. A passport ran two years and cost one dollar, and the Secretary could refuse one to anyone he believed wanted it "for an unlawful or improper purpose."
- **EO 182** set out thirteen parallel rules for applications made in the possessions, routed through each possession's chief executive, who also held the power to refuse.
- **EO 180** covered the third case: an island resident applying abroad to an American diplomat or consul. Such an applicant had to state that he owed allegiance to the United States and "does not acknowledge allegiance to any other government," and had to submit an affidavit from "at least two credible witnesses having good means of knowledge in substantiation of his statements of birth, residence, and loyalty." Rule 9 of each code demanded the same proof at home.

The orders took three months to reach the field. Secretary of State John Hay sent them to diplomatic and consular officers with a circular of October 22, 1902. The new passports would match the citizen's, he wrote, "except that where the recipient is now described as a 'citizen of the United States' he shall be described as a citizen of Porto Rico, or the Philippine Islands, or the island of Guam, as the case may be, owing allegiance to the United States." Blank forms were still at the printer; until they arrived, "the regular passport, amended to suit the occasion, may be used." Island passports were numbered in the same series as citizens' passports. The enclosed application form required an oath to "support and defend the Constitution of the United States against all enemies, foreign and domestic," and two affidavits that the applicant "is loyal to the Government of the United States."

## Historical context
The "whether citizens or not" language grew out of the war of 1898. The United States took Puerto Rico, the Philippines and Guam from Spain, and Congress gave their peoples a status of their own. The Foraker Act of 1900 made Puerto Rico's inhabitants "citizens of Porto Rico." The Philippine Organic Act, which Roosevelt signed on July 1, 1902, just eighteen days before these orders, did the same for "citizens of the Philippine Islands." Each act said they were entitled to the protection of the United States. Neither made them citizens of the United States.

The Supreme Court had not settled the question either. The *Insular Cases* of 1901 dealt with tariffs and with whether the Constitution applied in full to the new territories; *Downes v. Bidwell* held Puerto Rico to be territory that belonged to the United States without being a part of it. The personal status of the people living there was left to Congress and the executive.

The practical gap was simple. Until June 1902, section 4076 allowed passports only to citizens. A Puerto Rican or Filipino traveling abroad could claim American protection but had no document to prove it. Congress closed the gap, and Roosevelt's July orders set out how.

Most of the code itself was not new. The State Department had published consolidated instructions for passport applicants since 1869, renamed "Rules Governing the Issuance of Passports" in 1896. The two-year term dated from 1873, and the one-dollar fee from the act of 1888, which rule 13 cites. William McKinley had already amended the passport paragraphs of the diplomatic instructions and consular regulations by executive order. What was new in 1902 was rule 9, the separate code for the possessions, and EO 180's opening of consular passports to non-citizens.

Read closely, the rules show what a passport was in 1902: mainly proof of a claim to the government's protection abroad. The State Department was checking who could make that claim — the naturalized citizen with his court papers, the wife whose status followed her husband's, the island resident with two witnesses to his loyalty. Signing at Oyster Bay was routine. Roosevelt's summer orders regularly went out from Sagamore Hill.

## Political impact
Inside the State Department, the orders gave the Passport Bureau a uniform written standard and gave consuls abroad a procedure and a form for a new class of applicant. Passports were still optional for most American travelers, so the rules touched a fairly small public. The bigger argument over the status of the island peoples belonged to the debate over empire and the *Insular Cases*; the orders only carried out a law Congress had already passed.

The press reported them briefly and without argument. A Washington dispatch of July 23 told readers that the Secretary of State had drawn up rules under the new act and "submitted them to the president, who has approved and signed them." Passports would now go to "loyal Filipinos, Porto Ricans, etc.," giving residents of the possessions "the same protection and the same passport that citizens of the United States now enjoy in their travels." A search of California papers for the year turned up only wire news of the bill and the rules, with no editorial comment.

## Legacy
The 1902 code began a long line of passport rules issued by executive order. Fourteen months later, Executive Order 235 of September 12, 1903 replaced the domestic code ([The Executive Orders of Late 1903](story:82)). It had no revoking clause, but it set one standard of evidence for applicants "abroad or in an insular possession" and at home, and no separate insular code appears again in the numbered series before EO 654 of 1907. The rules were rewritten many times after that, and the Passport Act of July 3, 1926 remains the basic statutory authority.

Passports themselves became required only gradually. The requirement of 1918 and the one revived in 1941 both rested on war and national-emergency powers. Congress made the passport a standing requirement for citizens leaving and entering the country only in 1978.

The orders also gave working form to the non-citizen national, a status born of the 1898 empire that has never entirely disappeared. Filipinos held it until independence in 1946. Today it applies chiefly to people born in American Samoa and on Swains Island.

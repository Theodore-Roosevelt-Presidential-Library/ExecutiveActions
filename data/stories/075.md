## At a glance
Grover Cleveland, the twenty-second and twenty-fourth President, died at his home in Princeton, New Jersey, on the morning of June 24, 1908. That same day Roosevelt announced the death to the nation, praising Cleveland's "signal power as an administrator" and "the courage that quailed before no hostility when once he was convinced where his duty lay." He ordered flags on the White House and federal department buildings to half-staff for thirty days, and military and naval honors on the day of the funeral.

## Background
Cleveland died at Westland, his Princeton home, at 8:40 that morning. He was then the only President ever to have served two non-consecutive terms. Roosevelt's proclamation, headed "To the People of the United States," sums up his career in a few lines: a lawyer by profession, then mayor of his city, governor of his state, and twice President. It praises "the simplicity, dignity and uprightness of his private life" since leaving office. The document carried no legal or land effect. Like the proclamations announcing the deaths of William McKinley ([Announcing the Death of William McKinley](story:32)) and Secretary of State John Hay ([Announcing the Death of John Hay](story:57)), it is a ceremonial act of state.

The form was not new. The opening formula and the whole operative paragraph follow, nearly word for word, William McKinley's proclamation of March 14, 1901, on the death of ex-President Benjamin Harrison. McKinley's reads: "In his death the country has been deprived of one of its greatest citizens," and directs that "the flags on the Executive Mansion and the several Departmental buildings be displayed at half staff for a period of thirty days." Roosevelt changed "the country" to "the Nation" and "Executive Mansion" to "White House," the name he had made official in 1901. The biographical tribute in the middle was new.

The proclamation was countersigned by Alvey A. Adee as Acting Secretary of State. Adee, Second Assistant Secretary of State since 1886, was the department's senior permanent officer, Secretary Elihu Root had countersigned the year's proclamations through June 18; Adee signed as Acting Secretary from June 23 into early July.

## Historical context
Cleveland was a Democrat and Roosevelt a Republican, yet the tribute is warm and entirely without partisanship. Their careers had run together. In 1884, Assemblyman Roosevelt's reform bills for New York City went to Governor Cleveland for signature. When Cleveland returned to the White House in 1893, he kept Roosevelt, a Harrison appointee, on the U.S. Civil Service Commission, where Roosevelt served until 1895. They had also differed in public, as partisans of opposite parties do.

By 1908 Cleveland had settled into the role of respected elder statesman. He was admired for his stubborn honesty, remembered for standing against the free-silver wing of his own party, and living quietly at Princeton, where he had become a trustee of the university. Roosevelt's line about courage "when once he was convinced where his duty lay" reflects the reputation Cleveland earned for unbending vetoes and for acting on conviction whatever the political cost.

Presidents had long marked the deaths of their predecessors, and by the 1880s a proclamation was the usual way. Cleveland himself had issued them for Ulysses S. Grant in 1885 and Chester A. Arthur in 1886.

## Political impact
The press treated the proclamation as the government's formal signal for mourning and reported how it was carried out. Nothing found disputed it. The Associated Press reported from Washington that "immediately upon the receipt today of the president's proclamation formally announcing Mr. Cleveland's death steps were taken to carry out its provisions" (*Los Angeles Herald*, June 25, 1908). Army posts, fleets, ships and naval stations were ordered to fly their colors at half staff for thirty days, and diplomatic and consular offices were to do the same. The *San Francisco Call* noted "an innovation at the state department to meet modern conditions": overseas posts were told by telegraph instead of by mail, so the observance would be uniform in time. Two days later the *Call* described the half-hourly guns fired from the fleet in San Francisco harbor and from the Presidio and the bay forts, and closed: "By proclamation of the president the official mourning period will continue for 30 days." California papers from Fresno to Riverside to San Luis Obispo printed the text or a summary, with headlines such as "ROOSEVELT GIVES HIGH PRAISE TO CLEVELAND." No editorial objection was found.

At Cleveland's own wish, the funeral was small and private rather than a grand state ceremony: a simple service at Westland on June 26, with Roosevelt attending, and burial in Princeton Cemetery. The service was "of a strictly private nature," with admittance "limited to personal friends of the family." There was "no eulogy," at Mrs. Cleveland's request. Instead Henry van Dyke read Wordsworth's "Character of the Happy Warrior." Mrs. Cleveland declined the military funeral offered by New Jersey's Governor Fort, and National Guard troops were called out only to police the route and guard the President. Back at Oyster Bay that night, Roosevelt was reported as praising the simplicity of the ceremonies and calling the Wordsworth reading singularly appropriate.

## Legacy
The proclamation is a small ceremonial artifact. What stands out is not the act itself, which had clear precedents, but the warmth of its tribute to a man of the other party. It sits with Roosevelt's other death proclamations, for McKinley and for Hay, though the three do not share one form. Historians have debated Cleveland's standing ever since, but nothing in that later reassessment turns on this document. Cleveland's distinction as the only President to serve two non-consecutive terms lasted more than a century; Donald Trump, the 45th and 47th President, became the second.

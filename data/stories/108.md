## At a glance
Between January 20 and March 2, 1909, Roosevelt signed thirty-six national-forest proclamations, thirteen of them on March 2, two days before he handed the presidency to William Howard Taft. Most enlarged or adjusted forests in the West and Alaska. Five created new forests, three of them in Michigan and Minnesota, the first national forests of any size in the Lake States. Several folded Indian reservation land into national forests.

## Background
These proclamations did the ordinary work of a forest system that was growing up. The executive orders of June and July 1908 had carved the sprawling early reserves into compact, individually named forests ([The Great Reorganization of the National Forests, June 1908](story:99); [The National Forests of July 1908](story:101)). The 1909 proclamations tuned those new forests' boundaries and rounded out the system.

They come in four kinds:

- **Twenty-six enlargements:** Humboldt 832, Moapa 833, Pecos 835, Prescott 836, Tonto 837, Dixie 840, Sioux 845, Tongass 846, Klamath 847, Toiyabe 849, Chugach 852, Modoc 853, Ozark 854, California 855, Arkansas 857, Mono 858, Sitgreaves 859, Lincoln 860, Shasta 861, Alamo 862, Carson 863, Trinity 865, Apache 866, Plumas 868, Tahoe 870 and Sequoia 871.
- **Four boundary changes or transfers:** Cleveland 834, Gila 843, Black Hills 844, and the transfer of part of the Lassen to the Shasta, 867.
- **One consolidation:** Proclamation 851 merged the Magdalena into the Datil, adding 1,035,560 acres of public land for a total of 2,869,888.
- **Five new forests:** Marquette 838, Nevada 839, Michigan 841, Superior 848 and Zuni 864.

Two catalog titles are misleading. Proclamation 833 is titled *Setting Apart the Moapa National Forest*, but the Moapa already existed; the proclamation declares it "hereby enlarged." Proclamation 864 runs the other way. Titled *Enlarging the Zuni National Forest*, it actually creates the Zuni, setting the land apart "as a public reservation" from parts of the Zuni and Navajo Indian Reservations and other land. Proclamation 843 both added land to the Gila and cut land out of it, returning those tracts to the public domain.

The legal footing differed, too. The enlargements and boundary changes rest on the sundry civil act of June 4, 1897, the law for modifying an existing reserve. The five new forests rest on section 24 of the act of March 3, 1891, which let the President set land apart as a forest reserve.

**How big were they?** The new Nevada National Forest was by far the largest of the five, at 1,222,312 acres. The Zuni took in 670,981 acres, 266,981 in Arizona and 404,000 in New Mexico. The Forest Service's own report for 1909 gives the Superior 909,734 acres gross (582,149 net), the Michigan 132,770 gross and the Marquette 30,603 gross.

The enlargements ranged from a few thousand acres to millions. The biggest were in Alaska: about 8.7 million acres added to the Tongass and nearly 6 million to the Chugach. Several of the March 2 additions came out of Indian reservations: 721,280 acres of the White Mountain Apache Reservation to the Sitgreaves, about 483,000 acres to the Apache, 444,200 acres from the Jicarilla Apache Reservation and other land to the Carson, nearly the whole Hoopa Valley Reservation (81,800 acres) to the Trinity, and 28,160 acres of the Tule River Reservation to the Sequoia.

**The Alamo: a closer look.** Proclamation 862 shows the forest machinery working on contested ground. The Alamo National Forest had been formed in July 1908, when Executive Order 908 merged the Guadalupe and Sacramento forests. The proclamation added timbered land from the Mescalero Apache Indian Reservation:

> *Whereas,* it appears that the public good will be promoted by including in the Alamo National Forest certain lands within the Territory of New Mexico, shown on the diagram hereto attached and forming a part hereof, which are in part covered with timber, and which constitute a part of the Mescalero Apache Indian Reservation …

It then promised that, "for the term of twenty-five years from the date hereof," nothing in it would "terminate or abridge the rights of the Secretary of the Interior and of the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, under existing laws, to allot to individual Indians" any of the reservation land it took in. The same proviso protected rights to graze and to take timber and stone for domestic use, all under the Agriculture Department's forest rules. By the Forest Service's later figures, the enlargement brought the Alamo to 1,536,150 acres.

The signature at the bottom tells its own story. Robert Bacon countersigned as Secretary of State. He took that office on January 27, 1909, when Elihu Root resigned to enter the Senate, and held it only until March 5. On Proclamation 833 of January 21, Bacon signs as *Acting* Secretary; from February on, he signs as Secretary.

## Historical context
These proclamations were written in the shadow of the Fulton Amendment of March 4, 1907. Tucked into that year's agriculture appropriations act, it read: "*Provided further*, That hereafter no forest reserve shall be created, nor shall any additions be made to one heretofore created within the limits of the States of Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, Colorado, or Wyoming, except by Act of Congress" ([The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)).

The amendment barred additions as well as new forests, so even a boundary enlargement in those six states was off limits. That explains the shape of the 1909 list. It reaches into New Mexico, Arizona, California, Nevada, Utah, Alaska, Arkansas and South Dakota, and into new ground in Michigan and Minnesota. Oregon, Washington, Idaho and Colorado do not appear at all. Two titles do name a barred state: the Black Hills proclamation (South Dakota and Wyoming) and the Sioux (Montana and South Dakota). In both, the barred state appears only in a description of what a 1908 order had already done. Every acre actually added lies in South Dakota.

The one late action that did reach into a barred state shows how carefully that line was walked. On March 2, 1909, the same day as thirteen of these forest proclamations, Roosevelt created [Mount Olympus National Monument, Washington](story:7). It rested on the Antiquities Act of 1906, which the Fulton Amendment did not touch.

Behind it all was the practical thinking Roosevelt and Gifford Pinchot had pressed for years. Forests were working resources. Managed scientifically and kept in public hands, they would protect watersheds, supply timber and grazing, and guard against a feared "timber famine." The Alamo enlargement also shows how easily the government of the day folded Indian land into the federal forest estate. The twenty-five-year proviso was meant to soften that absorption, not to prevent it.

By the winter of 1909 the big political fight over Roosevelt's forest program had already been fought. These proclamations were more housekeeping than fresh provocation.

## Political impact
As a group, they drew little notice. Boundary proclamations were routine products of the Forest Service's mapmakers and did not make news the way the 1907 "midnight forests" had. Coming in the last days of the term, they read, where they registered at all, as the tidying-up of a departing administration.

The trade press did keep count. A Washington dispatch of March 7, 1909, printed as "More Forest Reserves" in the *New York Lumber Trade Journal* of April 1, listed the March 2 additions forest by forest and gave them a combined total of 4,980,736 acres. Its figures for a few forests differ from the Forest Service's later regional histories, and its line for the Sitgreaves drops a digit (21,280 for 721,280), a misprint in the journal itself.

The new Lake States forests were the real novelty, but even they made little stir. Exact-phrase searches for all three names across 1909 in the Library of Congress's Chronicling America newspaper collection return just eight hits, the earliest in May, months after the proclamations were signed. Local papers in Michigan did report the Michigan forest. On March 11 and 12, 1909, the *Pinconning Press* and the *Clare Sentinel* ran the same short item: the state land commissioner, Huntley S. Russell, had been notified from Washington that "134,630 acres of land in the northern part of the state located in Ogemaw, Oscoda and Alcona counties" had been "set aside as a national forest reserve" near the Au Sable. The item was plainly factual, with no complaint in it. Its acreage is close to, but not the same as, the Forest Service's official 132,770.

The Alamo enlargement's reach into the Mescalero reservation passed without recorded protest at the national level. The objections of the people affected carried little weight in the policy of the day.

Some of the reservation land did not stay in the forests for long. Part of the 1909 Apache addition went to the Crook National Forest in 1910. Then, on February 17, 1912, President William Howard Taft signed orders removing from the Apache, and separately from the Sitgreaves, "all that part of the White Mountain Apache Indian Reservation" added "by said proclamation of March 2, 1909," in order "to restore the White Mountain Apache Indian Reservation in all respects to the status existing prior to" it. About 348,600 acres went back to the reservation from the Apache alone. The 1909 Apache and Sitgreaves additions lasted barely three years.

## Legacy
Most of these forests endure, though their 1909 boundaries have long since been redrawn. The Western enlargements flowed into today's Humboldt-Toiyabe, Tonto, Gila, Tongass, Chugach, Klamath, Modoc, Sequoia, Tahoe and other national forests. Some names disappeared along the way, and the paper trail is worth setting out in order.

### Forests that changed names in the Southwest and Nevada
- **Alamo → Lincoln.** Executive Order 2633, signed June 6, 1917, moved the Alamo into the Lincoln National Forest effective July 1, 1917. (A Forest Service list prints 1919 for this order in one place; Woodrow Wilson's Proclamation 1474 of August 9, 1918 recites the June 6, 1917 order and its July 1, 1917 effective date.) The Lincoln still manages the Sacramento and Guadalupe country of south-central New Mexico, and the Mescalero Apache Tribe's reservation and forest lands remain a live matter of tribal sovereignty.
- **Moapa → Toiyabe.** Executive Order 2162 transferred the whole Moapa to the Toiyabe on July 1, 1915.
- **Nevada → Humboldt and Toiyabe.** On July 1, 1932, Executive Order 5863 folded the entire Toiyabe into the Nevada, not the other way around. The Nevada was then abolished effective October 1, 1957 and divided between the Humboldt and a revived Toiyabe under Public Land Order 1487, signed by Assistant Secretary of the Interior Roger Ernst on September 9, 1957.
- **Magdalena → Datil → Cibola and Gila.** The Datil, created by proclamation on June 18, 1908 and enlarged with the Magdalena in 1909, was split in December 1931 by two orders: Executive Order 5752 of December 3 sent land to the Cibola, and Executive Order 5765 of December 24 sent land to the Gila.
- **Zuni → Manzano → Cibola.** Executive Order 2045 transferred the whole Zuni to the Manzano on September 10, 1914, and the same December 3, 1931 order that split the Datil renamed the Manzano the Cibola.

### The Lake States forests
The Lake States forests had mixed fates. The Superior grew into one of the best-known forests in the system, home to the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness.

The Marquette and Michigan forests took a confusing path. On July 1, 1915, Executive Order 2163 passed the Marquette's land to the Michigan, and the Marquette name was dropped. On February 12, 1931, Proclamation 1938 renamed the Michigan the Marquette, reviving the name for a different forest. That second Marquette was abolished on February 9, 1962, when John F. Kennedy's Executive Order 10993 transferred "all those lands within the State of Michigan that were proclaimed as the Marquette National Forest by Proclamation of February 12, 1931" to the Hiawatha National Forest. No Marquette National Forest exists today. The Upper Peninsula's national forests are the Hiawatha, established January 16, 1931, and the Ottawa, established January 27, 1931.

### The last days in office
That Roosevelt spent some of his last days in office moving forest lines is a legacy in itself. Conservation and the proper use of natural resources, he told Congress in his Seventh Annual Message of December 3, 1907, "constitute the fundamental problem which underlies almost every other problem of our National life." He kept at it to the end.

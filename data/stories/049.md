## At a glance
During 1904 Roosevelt signed fourteen forest-reserve proclamations. Seven created new reserves, from the pine-topped buttes of northwestern South Dakota to the irrigated valleys of far northeastern California. The other seven redrew existing reserves, some growing, some shrinking, some doing both at once. Several began with petitions from people who wanted their water protected, and one, in the Warner Mountains, grew out of a land-fraud scandal.

## Background
**The new reserves.** Half of the fourteen created a reserve outright:
- **Baker City** (Proclamation 515, February 5), eastern Oregon
- **Cave Hills** (517) and **Slim Buttes** (518) (both March 5), on the pine-topped buttes of northwestern South Dakota, about 23,360 and 58,160 acres
- **Grantsville** (525, May 7) and **Salt Lake** (529, May 26), Utah. The Salt Lake proclamation set apart several separate tracts and named them in the plural, "The Salt Lake Forest Reserves."
- **Warner Mountains** (534) and **Modoc** (535) (both November 29), far northeastern California

**The changes.** The other seven reshaped reserves already standing, and they did not all run the same way. Fish Lake (522) in Utah was enlarged. Yellowstone (524) had its boundaries rewritten: it added new timbered land but also cut a large block in western Wyoming out and returned it to the public domain. The General Land Office counted 130,560 acres added and 649,160 removed, a net reduction of 518,600 acres. (Later editors titled this one an "Enlargement"; the *Statutes at Large* lists it as "Reducing area of Yellowstone Forest Reserve.") Battlement Mesa (527) and White River (528) in Colorado had their lines redrawn and lands restored to the public. South Platte (536) in Colorado and Big Horn (537) in Wyoming each lost some land and gained other land in the same document. Only Bitter Root (531) was a straight reduction, releasing lands in Idaho. None of the fourteen states its own acreage.

**Two laws, not one.** The new reserves rest on section 24 of the Act of March 3, 1891, which let the President "set apart and reserve, in any State or Territory having public land bearing forests, in any part of the public lands wholly or in part covered with timber or undergrowth, whether of commercial value or not, as public reservations." That law gave no power to shrink a reserve. So the seven changes instead quote the Sundry Civil Act of June 4, 1897: "The President is hereby authorized at any time to modify any Executive order that has been or may hereafter be made establishing any forest reserve, and by such modification may reduce the area or change the boundary lines of such reserve, or may vacate altogether any order creating such reserve." Land cut out of a reserve opened "to settlement from the date hereof," but not to "entry, filing or selection until after ninety days notice."

The Cave Hills proclamation is typical of the new reserves. It recites the 1891 law, finds that "the public good would be promoted by setting apart and reserving said lands," protects claims already lawfully made, and closes with the standard warning "to all persons not to make settlement upon the lands reserved by this proclamation." Secretary of State John Hay countersigned most of the set; Francis B. Loomis, as Acting Secretary, signed Baker City, Fish Lake, Battlement Mesa and White River.

All through 1904 the reserves were run by the General Land Office in the Interior Department, with Gifford Pinchot's Bureau of Forestry, over in Agriculture, advising on boundaries.

## Historical context
The thinking behind the reserves was the working creed of the conservation program in its practical, use-minded phase, told more fully in [The Forest Reserves of 1902](story:33), [The Forest Reserves of 1903](story:42) and [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9). There was fear of a coming "timber famine," distrust of speculators cornering timber and range, and a firm belief that forested headwaters had to stay public to protect the streams that farms, towns and the new federal irrigation projects depended on ([The Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21)).

**Who asked for them.** These reserves were not all dreamed up in Washington. The usual route ran through Interior: the Secretary withdrew land from entry, the land was examined, and a proclamation followed if the case held up.
- **South Dakota.** On March 4, 1904, the day before Roosevelt signed, Pinchot wrote to the President's secretary, William Loeb, that he agreed with Interior Secretary Ethan Allen Hitchcock that the Cave Hills and Slim Buttes proclamations should be signed and that the boundaries were satisfactory.
- **Oregon.** Baker County residents had petitioned as early as 1901 to protect the water supply around Baker City and Sumpter. The final push came from the state's men in Washington. The *Morning Oregonian* reported that Land Commissioner Richards recommended the reserve, Secretary Hitchcock endorsed it the day before signing, and Senator Fulton and Representative Williamson saw Roosevelt that morning and pressed him to act quickly. The paper said the reserve had been created in record time.
- **Utah.** A Washington dispatch of March 1903 reported that over the previous three years Utah citizens had petitioned for small reserves to protect their water for irrigation and household use, among them areas in Salt Lake and Tooele counties, and that after field study the Interior Secretary had decided to build a system of Utah reserves.
- **California.** In Modoc County the push came from the ground up. At a mass meeting in Alturas in early March 1904, citizens adopted resolutions asking that the land the Secretary had withdrawn be made a permanent Modoc Forest Reserve, reasoning that the streams rising in the mountains fed the Pit River valleys and that a ranch was only as valuable as its water right. The paper reported no dissent. The Modoc County Co-operative Association, more than two hundred farmers and stock raisers, separately petitioned the General Land Office for the reserve, calling forest preservation essential to irrigation.

**The Warner Mountains reserve came out of a scandal.** Since the late 1890s a far bigger "Warner Mountain" reserve had been proposed, about a million acres across northeastern California and southern Oregon, and land had been withdrawn for it. In February 1904 a Washington dispatch reported that the plan had been a scheme of the Hyde–Benson land ring, then under indictment. Its members had bought up barren, treeless land inside the proposed lines, meaning to hand it to the government in exchange for "lieu-land" scrip they could use to claim valuable timber elsewhere. The same dispatch said Pinchot had examined the region in 1903 and judged that no reserve would be made there, since half the land carried no timber. The Alturas resolutions answered the rumor head-on, conceding reports of land frauds tied to the Warner Mountain proposal while insisting the Modoc request was made in good faith.

What Roosevelt signed on November 29, 1904, was something different: two separate reserves, Warner Mountains at 306,518 acres gross and Modoc at 288,218, about 595,000 acres together and none of it in Oregon.

## Political impact
The South Dakota, Utah and Colorado proclamations look like routine uses of a settled power, three years before the 1907 fight over the "midnight" reserves made the President's authority a national controversy (see [The 1907 "Midnight Forest Reserves"](story:9)). But the year was not silent. The Warner Mountain land-fraud story went out by wire from Washington in February 1904 and was reprinted across California; the California Digital Newspaper Collection holds thirty-six items on the Warner Mountain reserve from 1903 to 1905, in twenty-one different papers. And the Modoc reserve was the subject of public mass meetings and petitions before it was signed.

Where there was friction, it was local and about money. Ranchers, sheepmen and timber operators often saw a new reserve as a "lock-up" of range and timber they had used freely, and some welcomed a reduction just as strongly. In Modoc County the economics ran the other way: stock raisers and irrigators asked for a reserve to protect their water. In Idaho, Senator Weldon B. Heyburn, the reserves' most persistent critic in Congress, was writing to the White House about them by September 1904.

**What the press said.** In Oregon, the Baker City reserve drew news but no complaint. The *Morning Oregonian*'s Washington bureau put the signing on page one on February 6 and said the reserve had been created in record time, and short wire items in the *La Grande Morning Observer* and Salem's *Weekly Oregon Statesman* reported it within days, without comment or objection. A search of California papers found no coverage at all of the November signing of the Warner Mountains and Modoc reserves, despite the stir the proposal had caused that spring. How South Dakota papers greeted Cave Hills and Slim Buttes has not yet been checked.

**What the map looked like at year's end.** The General Land Office's own tally for the year ended June 30, 1904, listed Fish Lake as enlarged, to an estimated 199,040 acres, and Yellowstone, Battlement Mesa, White River and Bitter Root as reduced. After its changes Battlement Mesa kept an estimated 808,960 acres, White River 978,880, and Bitter Root 4,089,600.

## Legacy
Responsibility soon changed hands. The Transfer Act of February 1, 1905, moved the reserves to the Department of Agriculture under Pinchot; his bureau took the name Forest Service that July; and in 1907 "forest reserves" became "national forests."

Most of the 1904 reserves live on inside today's national forests, though names and lines have been reshuffled many times.
- **Baker City** was broken up on March 15, 1906. Part went into the new Blue Mountains reserve, the rest returned to the public domain, and the name was dropped. The retained land lies mainly in today's Wallowa-Whitman National Forest in Oregon.
- **Cave Hills and Slim Buttes** became national forests in 1907 and were consolidated into the Sioux National Forest by an executive order effective July 1, 1908. Today they are land units of the Sioux Ranger District, run as part of the Custer Gallatin National Forest.
- **Warner Mountains and Modoc.** Roosevelt enlarged Modoc by 570,000 acres on February 13, 1908, and Warner Mountains was combined into it on July 1, 1908. Another 306,281 acres followed on February 25, 1909, bringing the Modoc National Forest to 1,471,817 acres gross by the end of that year. The pair are the core of today's Modoc National Forest in California.
- **The Utah reserves** survive in the Fishlake and Uinta-Wasatch-Cache National Forests. Grantsville and Salt Lake were merged into the Wasatch in 1908.
- **Yellowstone** was split in 1908 into several forests whose lands now lie chiefly in the Shoshone, Bridger-Teton and Custer Gallatin.
- **The Colorado reserves** live on in the White River, Grand Mesa and Pike forests.
- **Bitter Root**, a vast reserve dating from 1897, was divided in 1908 among the Bitterroot, Nezperce, Salmon and other forests.
- **Big Horn** lives on in the Bighorn National Forest in Wyoming.

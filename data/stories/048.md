## At a glance
When Hawaii became a United States territory, its lighthouses came along, and the Territory was left paying to run them, an arrangement found nowhere else in the country. On December 28, 1903, three weeks after asking Congress to take them over, Roosevelt did it himself by proclamation. He claimed every light, buoy, beacon and record of the old Hawaiian service for the federal government and handed them to the Light-House Board. Some of what the Board inherited was startlingly modest: one harbor light was a pair of ordinary kitchen lamps.

## Background
Hawaii was annexed in 1898 and organized as a territory by the Hawaiian Organic Act of April 30, 1900. Under Section 91 of that act, the public property the Republic of Hawaii had ceded stayed with the territorial government, which maintained it at its own expense unless the United States took it for federal use.

This proclamation was that taking. It declared that all the lighthouse property of the former republic was "taken for the uses and purposes of the United States." That meant the lighthouses themselves and about five acres of adjacent land at each, "when practicable to obtain so much," plus "light-vessels, light-house tenders, beacons, buoys, sea-marks and their appendages," every supply and piece of apparatus, and "all the archives, books, documents, drawings, models, returns" of the old establishment. The exact parcels were left for the Light-House Board to survey later. Charge of the whole system went to the Department of Commerce and Labor, newly created that year, which had taken the Board over from the Treasury. Francis B. Loomis signed as Acting Secretary of State. The federal service took up the work on January 1, 1904.

## Historical context
Hawaii sat astride Pacific shipping and naval routes, so its lights mattered. They were also an awkward leftover and a drain on the new Territory. Testifying before a visiting Senate commission in September 1902, Territorial Secretary Henry E. Cooper pointed out that the lights were "maintained by the territorial government, this not being the case elsewhere." Upkeep had cost $6,727 for the year ending June 30, 1902. Cooper asked for new lights at Makapuʻu Point and on the Maui coast and a replacement for a "dilapidated" Honolulu harbor light. The push, in other words, came from the Territory first.

A federal visitor had already taken a hard look. William H. Eustis, a special commissioner sent to the islands by the Secretary of the Treasury, reported early in 1903 that "the light-houses are generally of a very crude character, the one on the top of the custom-house in Honolulu being a lantern with a red cloth tied around it." He added, "I understand there is not a revolving light on the island coast," and found the island of Hawaii's six lights "all very cheap and of short range." He also reported "a universal desire" among officials and citizens that the lights be put under federal control.

Roosevelt took up the Territory's case in his Third Annual Message, delivered December 7, 1903: "I recommend that an appropriation be made for building light-houses in Hawaii, and taking possession of those already built. The Territory should be reimbursed for whatever amounts it has already expended for light-houses." The *Hawaiian Star* ran the news across page one that afternoon: "PRESIDENT RECOMMENDS APPROPRIATION TO TAKE OVER LIGHTHOUSES IN HAWAII." The proclamation three weeks later handled the "taking possession" by executive act, under a law already on the books. New construction and any repayment still had to come from Congress.

The Board soon saw what it had inherited. On December 29, 1903, it was directed to take charge, and on January 4, 1904, it stretched its Twelfth District to take in "the Hawaiian Islands and their dependencies." Its report for the year described each station as found. Lahaina, on Maui, was "a fixed white light composed of two ordinary kitchen lamps of small power," and "It is difficult to distinguish this light from the lights in the town." Kanahene Point, also on Maui, was another pair of kitchen lamps. Mahukona, on Hawaii, was "an ordinary house lamp." The Board counted 18 lighthouses and beacon lights, 19 unlighted beacons, a bell buoy and 20 other buoys. Only Diamond Head had a Fresnel lens.

## Political impact
The transfer was an administrative act carried out under an existing statute, and it answered a request the Territory itself had pressed. No objection to it has been found. Its interest lies in what it shows: a small, concrete case of a newly acquired territory's property and public services being folded into the national government.

The money followed slowly. The Board's district officers were still urging in 1908 "that the Territory of Hawaii be reimbursed." Congress acted the next winter. The Act of February 4, 1909, appropriated $23,393.69 "to be paid to the Territory of Hawaii to reimburse said Territory for money paid, laid out, and expended by said Territory in maintaining light-houses, bell buoys, and light-house service on its coasts" from annexation until the federal Board took over.

## Legacy
Federal control brought a wave of lighthouse building: at least fifteen new lights went up in the islands between 1906 and 1920. Two became landmarks. Makapuʻu Point Light on Oahu is a short concrete tower, about thirty-five feet, set high on a sea cliff, and its fame is its lens: a twelve-foot hyperradiant Fresnel, the largest size ever made, first shown at the 1893 Chicago exposition. It was first lit on October 1, 1909, about 420 feet above the water. The Molokaʻi Light at Kalaupapa, an octagonal concrete tower that is the tallest in Hawaii, was first lit a month earlier, on September 1, 1909, its light about 213 feet above the sea. Published heights for the tower itself disagree.

Responsibility moved with the rest of the national system. A 1910 act replaced the Light-House Board with a Bureau of Lighthouses, and in 1939 the Bureau was folded into the U.S. Coast Guard, which still maintains Hawaii's aids to navigation today.

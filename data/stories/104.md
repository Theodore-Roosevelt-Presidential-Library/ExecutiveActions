## At a glance
With little more than a month left in office, Roosevelt signed seventeen executive orders in January 1909. Most were clerical. Two were not. On January 19 he created a thirty-member Council of the Fine Arts to advise the government on the design of its buildings, monuments, and coins. On January 26 he enlarged Pelican Island, the little Florida rookery he had made the country's first federal bird reservation almost six years earlier. Congress killed the council within weeks; Pelican Island is still a refuge.

## Background
Ten of the month's seventeen orders were single personnel actions: one clerk, one skilled laborer, one post-office inspector at a time. The others, briefly:
- **Executive Order 1002** (January 6) extended the Safety Appliance Act of 1893, as amended, to the Canal Zone and named the Panama Railroad.
- **Executive Order 1003** (January 7) set rules for Navy and Marine men aboard Army transports and Army men aboard naval vessels.
- **Executive Order 1007** (January 12) moved the vessel *Zafiro* back from the War Department to the Navy.
- **Executive Order 1008** (January 15) handed five and three-quarters acres of the Kaakaukukui Reef Naval Reservation at Honolulu to the War Department for submarine-mine defenses.
- **Executive Order 1009** (January 18) moved the Mitchell, South Dakota, land office to Gregory and folded part of the Chamberlain district into it.
- **Executive Order 1011** (January 22) re-established the Fort Keogh Military Reservation in Montana.
- **Executive Order 1013** (January 23) reserved land in Minnesota for a lighthouse.

**The Council of the Fine Arts.** Executive Order 1010 is four documents and one operative paragraph. It opens with a January 11, 1909, letter from a committee of the American Institute of Architects (Cass Gilbert, S. B. P. Trowbridge, William A. Boring, Glenn Brown, C. Grant La Farge, George B. Post, and Robert S. Peabody). The government, they wrote, spent hundreds of millions on public buildings, statues, coins, and grounds, and "[u]nder our lack of system, and in the absence of proper management, the results do not adequately and properly represent or express the state of enlightenment and cultivation which our people have attained." They asked the President, "as an initiatory step," to name a Council of the Fine Arts.

Roosevelt agreed the same day, asking the Institute "to designate the names of thirty men representing all parts of the country to compose such a Council as you suggest." The Institute sent names on January 16, Roosevelt listed them on January 18, and on January 19 he issued the order. It told the heads of departments, bureaus, and commissions that "[h]ereafter, before any plans are formulated for any buildings or grounds, or for the location or erection of any statue, the matter must be submitted to the Council I have named and their advice followed unless for good and sufficient reasons the President directs that it be not followed." The Supervising Architect of the Treasury would carry out its recommendations.

The council was a roll call of the era's leading designers: twenty-one architects, among them Charles F. McKim, D. H. Burnham, Cass Gilbert, and Frank Miles Day; four painters (John La Farge, E. H. Blashfield, F. D. Millet, Kenyon Cox); four sculptors (Daniel C. French, H. A. MacNeil, Herbert Adams, K. T. Bitter); and one landscape architect, Frederick Law Olmsted, Jr. The Institute had proposed including laymen, but none were named. Its first task, Roosevelt wrote, would be to "report and give their opinion on the character and location of the Lincoln Memorial."

**Pelican Island.** Executive Order 1014 was a single paragraph, in the bird-protection form Roosevelt had used dozens of times. It enlarged the reservation to take in "all unreserved mangrove and other islands" in two sections of the Indian River lagoon, and made it "unlawful for any person to hunt, trap, capture, wilfully disturb, or kill any bird of any kind whatever, or take the eggs of such birds within the limits of this reservation." The order dates the original Pelican Island reservation to "March 13, 1903." The records both departments kept at the time, the Biological Survey's and the General Land Office's, give March 14, 1903, which is the date to use (see [Pelican Island Bird Reservation](story:1)).

## Historical context
The fine-arts council grew out of the City Beautiful movement and the McMillan Plan of 1901–1902, which had revived Pierre L'Enfant's design for Washington. The architects' letter warned that each department's freedom to pick its own sites had worn away L'Enfant's plan, and that "[t]he White House itself, within recent years, has been threatened with mutilation through the efforts of misguided enthusiasm for supposed improvement. Wise action by the present Executive alone saved this monument, but there is no assurance for its future."

Roosevelt, who had backed the McMillan restoration, was sympathetic. But in his reply to the Institute he said plainly that an executive order was not enough: "I earnestly advise your body to take immediate steps to secure the enactment of a law giving permanent effect to what I am directing to be done. The course you advocate, and which I approve, should not be permissive with the Executive; it should be made mandatory upon him, by act of Congress."

Pelican Island belonged to the other great strand of his presidency. By the end of January 1909 Roosevelt had established twenty-five federal bird reservations. He created no new one that month; rounding out the first, on the Indian River lagoon, was a fitting late gesture. The burst was still to come: twenty-six more in the five weeks that followed, seventeen of them in a single order on February 25 (see [The Bird Reservations of February 1909](story:105)).

## Political impact
The council won respectful notice from architects and artists. It met on February 9, 1909, and approved the Senate Park Commission's site for the Lincoln Memorial; McKim, very ill, attended for a few minutes. It lasted nine weeks.

Congress was already at odds with Roosevelt over commissions it had not authorized: the Keep Commission, the Inland Waterways Commission, the National Conservation Commission, the Country Life Commission. On February 22, 1909, with the House debating the sundry civil bill, Representative John J. Fitzgerald of New York offered a proviso barring payment "to members of the so-called Council of Fine Arts." The council might be "highly desirable," he said, but its duty "should be fixed by Congress and not by the Executive." The amendment carried, 67 to 17.

The sundry civil act of March 4, 1909, Roosevelt's last day in office, struck at the council twice. One proviso named it outright: no money in the act "shall be paid to members of the so-called Council of Fine Arts … as compensation or for expenses," nor spent on plans it had approved or suggested. Section 9 added a general rule that no public money could pay for "any commission, council, board, or other similar body" not authorized by law. (Both the debate and the statute misdate the order to January 18; it was signed January 19.) On May 21, 1909, President William Howard Taft, who favored the idea but wanted it grounded in law, revoked Executive Order 1010, citing the sundry civil act.

The Pelican Island enlargement, like the rest of the bird-reservation program, passed without national comment, and the routine orders drew none.

## Legacy
Roosevelt's instinct that the council needed a law behind it proved right. The Act of May 17, 1910, created "a permanent Commission of Fine Arts … composed of seven well-qualified judges of the fine arts." That body still reviews the design of monuments, memorials, and buildings in the capital, and it traces its lineage to the short-lived 1909 council, as does the Lincoln Memorial the council was first asked to consider. The statutory commission also filled the gap in Roosevelt's list: its seats were written to include distinguished laymen.

Pelican Island remains a National Wildlife Refuge, the seed of a system that now spans the country. Franklin Roosevelt's Proclamation 2416 of July 25, 1940, renamed the old reservations, "Pelican Island Reservation" becoming "Pelican Island National Wildlife Refuge." The remaining January 1909 orders carry no lasting legacy of their own.

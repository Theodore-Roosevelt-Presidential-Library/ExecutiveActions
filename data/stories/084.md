## At a glance
These twenty executive orders close out Roosevelt's 1904, running from late summer through Election Day to New Year's Eve. They open with a mourning order for Postmaster-General Henry Clay Payne. Then, in the weeks after the election, comes a burst of rule-making: the civil-service system was stretched to staff the canal enterprise in Panama, and new regulations reached down to the government's unskilled laborers, the bottom rung of the federal workforce.

## Background
Postmaster-General Henry Clay Payne, a Milwaukee businessman and Republican party organizer who had run the Post Office Department since January 1902, died in Washington on October 4, 1904. Executive Order 271, issued the next day through the State Department over Secretary John Hay's signature, was a fuller tribute than the one-sentence order for Senator Hanna that February. It praised a man "of singularly gentle, loyal and lovable nature, inspired by a large sense of the duties of a true citizen," closed the Washington departments from 9 a.m. to 1 p.m. on the day of the funeral, Friday, October 7, and put flags at half-mast on all public buildings until the funeral had taken place. Because it was an order and not a proclamation, it carried no seal and was not printed in the *Statutes at Large*. The Associated Press carried it in full the next morning.

Then the numbered series goes quiet for five weeks. After October 5 the next order, EO 272, is dated November 9, the day after Roosevelt's election, and seventeen more follow before the year ends.

Many of them are named appointments, one person and one position apiece:

- **EO 270** (August 19) authorized the permanent appointment of Dr. William Conyngton as physician at the Grand River Indian School, North Dakota, "as result of special examination." That examination had been "made necessary because increased illness at the school required the immediate services of a physician and all persons eligible under the rules had declined the position."
- **EO 272** named Luther Conant, Jr., a special examiner in the new Bureau of Corporations at $2,500 a year.
- **EO 280** made James Rankin Young "of Pennsylvania" superintendent of the Dead Letter Office. He had just left Congress after three terms as a Republican member from Philadelphia (1897–1903), and he held the post until 1913.
- **EO 276, 281 and 282** filled a matron's post and an inspector's post in the Immigration Service and an assistant inspector of hulls in the Steamboat-Inspection Service.
- **EO 284** named Mrs. William H. Tabberah keeper of the Cumberland Head Light Station on Lake Champlain, New York, one of the era's women lighthouse keepers.
- **EO 287** excepted from examination a "consulting architect, for work of reconstructing United States Military Academy, at West Point, New York."
- **EO 288** made permanent thirteen temporary Census Bureau clerks hired to compile the census of the Philippine Islands, because the Civil Service Commission "was unable to certify eligibles possessing the requisite qualifications."

Two clusters give the group its character. The first is **Panama**. All three Panama orders came in the four weeks after the election. EO 274 (November 15) classified the Isthmian Canal Commission and applied the civil-service act and rules to it, "except persons employed merely as laborers, persons whose appointments are confirmed by the Senate, and engineers detailed from the army." EO 273, signed the same day, excepted twenty-three classes of position from examination, from the Commission's secretary and treasurer to division and resident engineers, the chief sanitary officer, hospital superintendents and attendants, quarantine officers, and the sanitary officers at Colon and Panama. EO 283 (December 8) provided that "the requirements as to United States citizenship may be waived for applicants for positions on the Isthmus of Panama." Since laborers were already outside the rules, the waiver reached only classified positions. The order gives no reason, but the Civil Service Commission did, in its annual report: "This action was taken upon the request of the Isthmian Canal Commission, because the needs of the service could not be met without the employment of persons who were not United States citizens." These orders built the personnel side of the canal enterprise described in [Governing the Panama Canal Zone: the Executive Orders of 1904](story:14).

The second cluster is **labor**. EO 275 (November 15) laid down ten regulations "to govern the selection of unskilled laborers" in the departments at Washington "with a view of securing skill and efficiency and of excluding other considerations." A board of labor employment worked under the Commission's supervision; government physicians rated applicants on physical fitness; and hiring officers had to choose from the top three names certified. The system was not brand new. The order took over "all existing registers" from "the existing board," so it reorganized a scheme already at work. EO 285 (December 12) carried a parallel system to federal offices in New York, Boston, Philadelphia and St. Louis. It stated that it "is distinct from the classified service, and does not classify positions of mere laborers," and it barred any inquiry into "the political or religious opinions or affiliations of any applicant." EO 279 (November 29) shut the side door: no one was to be hired in any executive department "for the performance of any service of the character performed by classified employees except in accordance with the provisions of the civil-service rules."

A few orders moved groups inside the merit system. EO 278 classified the substitute watchmen of the Government Printing Office, bringing in the forty men then serving. EO 286 (December 17) classified everyone employed "in the protection and administration of the forestry reserves" under the General Land Office, laborers and Senate-confirmed officers excepted. EO 277 (November 23) struck parts of Schedule A, "restoring to the competitive service the positions of deputy or assistant collector, deputy naval officer, deputy surveyor, and cashier in the Customs Service." These were posts in the customs houses, not cashiers across the Treasury.

The year's last order, EO 288½ (December 31), is narrower than its catalog title suggests. It did not forbid government notaries to charge fees in general. It barred any executive-branch officer, clerk or employee who was also a notary from charging "any compensation whatever" in three situations: for notarial acts done for a government officer or employee "in his official capacity," in "any matter in which the Government is interested," or for anyone "during the hours of such notary's service to the Government." Disobedience was "ground for immediate dismissal." The fractional number marks a late insertion into the series, and the same order very probably appears a second time in the list of unnumbered orders, under January 4, 1905.

## Historical context
Two currents of the presidency run through these months. The first is Panama. The canal treaty had been ratified in February 1904, and the Isthmian Canal Commission was getting organized. Staffing it collided with a civil-service system built for clerks in Washington, not for engineers, sanitary officers and hospital staff in the tropics. The result was this run of classifications, exceptions and a citizenship waiver.

The second current was the push to define and control the lowest tier of federal workers. Unskilled laborers stood outside the classified service and had long been a temptation for patronage. The November and December orders tightened the Washington labor registers, carried them to four big cities, and, in EO 279, outlawed the old dodge of hiring someone as a "laborer" to do a clerk's work. Roosevelt had been working to close gaps like these since his own years as a Civil Service Commissioner.

EO 286 also points toward the biggest forestry change of the coming year. It classified the Land Office men who guarded the forest reserves about six weeks before the Transfer Act of February 1, 1905 moved the reserves to Gifford Pinchot's Bureau of Forestry in the Department of Agriculture.

## Political impact
The Payne order was a Cabinet courtesy, and the department's work went on. Robert J. Wynne, First Assistant Postmaster-General since 1902, was named acting Postmaster-General the day after Payne's death and Postmaster-General on October 10, serving until March 1905. The courtesy covered a complicated record. Payne had come to the Cabinet as a Wisconsin party manager and railroad and utility man, and his department had spent 1903 under investigation for fraud in contracts and appointments.

In California papers, the death and the order ran as straight news. Even the Democratic *Los Angeles Herald*, five weeks before the election, kept the two subjects apart. Its editorial column of October 6 said of Payne only that "Close application to the exacting duties of his official position seems to have overtaxed his strength." A separate paragraph in the same column quoted the Democratic vice-presidential candidate, Henry G. Davis, on "the frauds in the postoffice department."

The Panama orders drew wire notices, not argument. The *San Francisco Call* of November 15 reported that civil-service rules would now reach the canal commission's employees, apart from presidential appointees, laborers and "about thirty places." By December the Commission was posting recruiting notices for the Isthmus through its local boards. The labor regulations and the citizenship waiver drew no stories in the California press. Any argument over them took place inside the Civil Service Commission and the departments. The standing complaint that "without examination" appointments wore down the merit system applied here as always; the Dead Letter Office post for a just-retired congressman is the clearest example in the group.

## Legacy
As a group these orders left no single dramatic mark; their reach is institutional. The Panama orders were early pieces of the personnel system that would build the canal. EO 273 was amended within weeks (EO 289, January 5, 1905) and superseded in 1906 (EO 394), while the citizenship waiver and the anti-evasion rule stayed on the books until President Truman's Executive Order 9830 revoked them in 1947. The labor regulations were amended again and again through 1908 and fed into the slow, decades-long spread of the merit system across the federal workforce. The notarial-fee rule was loosened in March 1905 to exempt oaths of disinterestedness (EO 319) and replaced in November 1908 (EO 977). The forest guards classified by EO 286 passed with the reserves to the Department of Agriculture in 1905, where the reserves became the foundation of the Forest Service.

The Payne order joins Roosevelt's other mourning documents for public men: [Announcing the Death of William McKinley](story:32), [Announcing the Death of John Hay](story:57), [The Executive Orders of Early 1904](story:83) (Hanna), and [Announcing the Death of Ex-President Grover Cleveland](story:75).

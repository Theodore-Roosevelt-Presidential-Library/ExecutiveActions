## At a glance
John Hay, Lincoln's young secretary turned Secretary of State to McKinley and Roosevelt, died on July 1, 1905. Two days later Roosevelt issued a proclamation calling his death "a national bereavement" and "a serious loss to all mankind." It ordered American flags lowered at embassies, forts, naval stations, and ships for ten days. The working orders were borrowed almost word for word from a mourning proclamation of ten years earlier.

## Background
This was a proclamation of mourning, not a governing order. Hay died shortly after midnight on July 1, 1905, at The Fells, his summer place on Lake Sunapee in Newbury, New Hampshire. The proclamation praised him as a man to whom "it was given to stand as a leader in the effort to better world-conditions by striving to advance the cause of international peace and justice." It then gave the customary orders. American diplomats abroad were to fly their flags at half-mast for ten days, and so were all forts, military posts, naval stations, and vessels of the United States. On the day of the funeral, the executive departments in Washington would close and the flag would fly at half-mast on all public buildings.

"Customary" is literal here. The two working paragraphs copy, nearly word for word, Grover Cleveland's proclamation of May 28, 1895, on the death of Secretary of State Walter Q. Gresham. Roosevelt's version changes one phrase: Cleveland's "a suitable expression of national bereavement" became "a suitable expression of national mourning." Only the opening tribute was new. Cleveland's proclamation was also countersigned by an Acting Secretary of State.

The document has no "In witness whereof" clause, unlike most proclamations of the period. The omission is in the original.

## Historical context
Hay was among the most important public men of his generation. He came to Washington young to serve as Abraham Lincoln's assistant private secretary, and his career ran nearly forty-five years: diplomat, Lincoln biographer, Ambassador to Great Britain, and, from September 30, 1898, Secretary of State. In that office he issued the Open Door Notes on China. He negotiated the Hay–Pauncefote Treaty, which cleared Britain out of the way of an American canal across the isthmus, and he shaped the 1903 canal diplomacy with Colombia and then Panama.

By 1905 his health was failing. He left the country on March 17 on what the State Department called a "vacation trip for health reasons," with stops at Genoa, Nervi, the cure at Nauheim, Paris, and London. He did not get back until June 15, two weeks before he died. Roosevelt had been taking more of foreign affairs into his own hands, and the death came at a busy moment. In June, Roosevelt had offered his good offices to Russia and Japan. He was then arranging the peace conference that would bring their envoys to the Portsmouth Navy Yard that August.

The proclamation was countersigned by Herbert H. D. Peirce, Third Assistant Secretary of State, signing as Acting Secretary. His signature says less about the vacancy than it seems to. Acting secretaries had run the State Department for months while Hay was abroad and ill. Assistant Secretary Francis B. Loomis signed as Acting Secretary through mid-June. Peirce signed a copyright proclamation dated July 1, the day Hay died. Alvey A. Adee took over the role from July 14.

## Political impact
The proclamation followed a fixed precedent, Cleveland's for Gresham ten years earlier, and nothing found suggests it drew any controversy. Newspapers carried it. *The Caucasian* of Clinton, North Carolina, summarized its orders on its front page on July 6, beside a report of King Edward VII's cabled sympathy. The same report gave the cause of death as Hay's physician, Dr. Charles L. Scudder, saw it: a pulmonary embolism. The funeral was held on July 5 in Cleveland, with burial at Lake View Cemetery. The Raleigh *Morning Post* led its front page with "HAY'S BODY LAID TO REST" and described Roosevelt standing "close to the open grave."

The praise crossed party lines. On July 4 the Norfolk *Virginian-Pilot*, a Democratic daily, ran an editorial on Hay that called him the country's first citizen and asked whether it was not fair to speak that plain truth of a patriot, statesman and diplomat of the opposite party. The same page reprinted a tribute from the New Orleans *States* ranking him a great diplomat and a statesman of high ability. The *Pilot*'s praise came with a jab at the administration: Hay, it said, had been nothing like Taft or Roosevelt and had never gone in for the spectacular.

The practical question was who came next. Roosevelt named Elihu Root, his former Secretary of War, who entered on duty at the State Department on July 19, 1905.

## Legacy
Hay is remembered chiefly for the Open Door policy toward China and for the treaties that made the Panama Canal legally possible. His name lives on in the Hay–Pauncefote and Hay–Bunau-Varilla treaties. Root, his successor, went on to reorganize the consular service and later won the Nobel Peace Prize.

As an executive action, this proclamation belongs with Roosevelt's other death proclamations, for William McKinley ([Announcing the Death of William McKinley](story:32)) and for former President Grover Cleveland ([Announcing the Death of Ex-President Grover Cleveland](story:75)). They did not share one fixed form. The McKinley proclamation set a day of mourning and prayer. The Cleveland proclamation ordered flags at half-staff on the White House and department buildings for thirty days, with military and naval honors, and it followed nearly word for word McKinley's 1901 proclamation on the death of Benjamin Harrison. The Hay proclamation, in turn, copied Cleveland's 1895 Gresham proclamation. Each borrowed from whatever precedent fit the case.

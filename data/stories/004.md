## At a glance
On January 9, 1908, Roosevelt signed Proclamation 793, setting aside about 295 acres of old-growth coast redwoods in Redwood Canyon, Marin County, as Muir Woods National Monument. The land was a gift: William Kent and Elizabeth Thacher Kent had bought the canyon to save it from a water company's reservoir, then handed it to the nation. When Roosevelt offered to name the monument for Kent, Kent said no, and the name stayed Muir's.

## Background
The proclamation protected a grove of coast redwoods in Redwood Canyon, just north of San Francisco. Unlike every national monument before it, this land did not already belong to the United States. William Kent, a Marin County businessman and reformer who would not enter Congress until 1911, and his wife Elizabeth Thacher Kent bought it and gave it away. The donation was the whole mechanism: without it, there was nothing for Roosevelt to proclaim.

The proclamation recites that the Kents conveyed the land by deed on December 26, 1907, under the Antiquities Act of June 8, 1906, and that the Secretary of the Interior accepted it. It then explains why the grove mattered: "an extensive growth of redwood trees (*Sequoia sempervirens*) embraced in said land is of extraordinary scientific interest and importance because of the primeval character of the forest in which it is located, and of the character, age and size of the trees." It closes with a warning "not to appropriate, cut, injure, destroy or take away any trees on said land."

A few details in the document's opening lines don't match the record:

- **The spelling.** The proclamation names her "Elizabeth Thatcher Kent." The correct spelling is Thacher. Her father was Thomas Anthony Thacher, professor of Latin at Yale, and her brother Sherman founded the Thacher School in Ojai. The *Statutes at Large* prints "Thatcher," so anyone quoting the proclamation word for word should add *[sic]*. The National Park Service's transcription quietly fixes the spelling, so it can't be used to show what the original says.
- **The address.** The proclamation calls the Kents "of the City of Chicago." Kent had been active in Chicago business for about two decades, but the family moved to Kentfield, California, in 1907, and Kent wrote to Roosevelt from there three weeks after the signing. No source explains the Chicago wording.
- **The buyers.** The proclamation says the 1905 purchase was made by "William Kent" alone. The Marin County deed conveyed the land to both William Kent and Elizabeth Thacher Kent.

Three acreages are in circulation, and each is right in its own context. Kent bought 611.57 acres in 1905, the deed of gift conveyed 298, and the proclamation and the General Land Office diagram describe 295, "a little more or less." Kent kept back roughly 138 forested acres at the north end, around the railway branch line and a hotel he planned to build. Roosevelt's own phrase for the gift was "this tract of nearly 300 acres."

## Historical context
Saving the canyon was a race against time. In the summer of 1905 the Kents agreed to buy 611.57 acres, Redwood Canyon included, from the Tamalpais Land and Water Company for $45,000, to keep the redwoods from being cut. Kent was in real financial trouble at the time. The price was within reach only because the seller had turned down a $100,000 offer for a larger tract. That refusal apparently came from the company's president, Lovell White, whose wife was a prominent Big Trees preservationist. Kent said plainly that he could not have matched the higher offer. Years later Elizabeth Kent recalled raising the family's debts with her husband, and him answering: "If we lost all the money we have and saved those trees it would be worth while, wouldn't it?" Her unpublished biography of him, written around 1950, is the only source for the remark.

The danger came from the North Coast Water Company, an offshoot of the same Tamalpais company, run by James Newlands and William Magee. It already owned six hundred acres north of Kent's land, but that ground couldn't hold a reservoir. Redwood Canyon could. On December 2, 1907, the company filed a condemnation action in Marin County Superior Court for forty-seven acres of Kent's property. Kent was in Hawaii recovering from influenza. He got home on December 3 and wired Gifford Pinchot that same day: "Condemnation and destruction of Redwood Cañon threatened by Water Company. Must have it accepted as National forest at once. … Sole idea is to save trees for public. Wire acceptance and terms. Vitally urgent."

Kent's first idea, as the telegram shows, was a national forest. His friend Frederick E. Olmsted talked him out of it. Olmsted was the Forest Service's chief inspector in San Francisco, Pinchot's right hand on the West Coast, and a forester trained at the Biltmore Forest School. His reasoning was a forester's: after 1905, Forest Service policy stressed sustainable timber production, so national forest status would not guarantee that old-growth redwoods stayed standing. He pointed Kent to the Antiquities Act instead. A monument needed only a presidential signature, with none of the delay of an act of Congress. Olmsted asked Pinchot for a form of deed, and Kent's lawyer, William Thomas, filled it in during the last week of December. Interior Secretary James R. Garfield accepted the deed on December 31, 1907, and Roosevelt signed the proclamation nine days later. (One account, in the *Marin Journal* of January 9, 1908, suggests Olmsted told Kent about the Act only after Kent had already wired Pinchot.)

**The naming letters.** Kent's deed had already named the grove for John Muir, and the proclamation created "the Muir Woods National Monument." The famous exchange over the name came afterward, in four letters.

Roosevelt wrote from the White House on January 22, 1908: "I have a very great admiration for John Muir; but after all, my dear sir, this is your gift. No other land than that which you give is included in this tract of nearly 300 acres and I should greatly like to name the monument the Kent Monument if you will permit it."

Kent answered from Kentfield on January 30: "Your kind suggestion of a change of name is not one that I can accept. So many millions of better people have died forgotten, that to stencil one's own name on a benefaction, seems to carry with it an implication of mandate immortality, as being something purchasable." He went on: "I have five good, husky boys that I am trying to bring up to a knowledge of democracy and to a realizing sense of the rights of the 'other fellow,' doctrines which you, sir, have taught with more vigor and effect than any man in my time. If these boys cannot keep the name of Kent alive, I am willing it should be forgotten."

Roosevelt gave in on February 5: "By George! you are right. It is enough to do the deed and not to desire, as you say, to 'stencil one's own name on the benefaction.' Good for you, and for the five boys who are to keep the name of Kent alive! I have four who I hope will do the same thing by the name of Roosevelt. Those are awfully good photos."

Muir wrote to Kent from Martinez on February 6: "Seeing my name in the tender & deed of the Tamalpais Sequoias was a surprise of the pleasantest kind. This is the best tree-lover's monument that could possibly be found in all the forests of the world." He closed: "Saving these woods from the axe & saw, from money-changers and water-changers & giving them to our country & the world is in many ways the most notable service to God & man I've heard of since my forest wanderings began. … That so fine divine a thing should have come out of money mad Chicago!" The "water-changers" were the water company. "Money mad Chicago" referred to the source of Kent's own fortune. A slightly different version of the second sentence ("the very best monument to a tree-lover's memory…") comes from Muir's first rough draft, which survives in his papers. The wording above is the letter he actually sent.

## Political impact
The gift drew applause and no controversy. It cost the treasury nothing and made a private citizen the hero of a conservation win.

The gift did not end the water company's threat, though. Newlands and Magee kept their condemnation suit going for nearly a year after the land became federal. They argued that because the case was filed first, the land still counted as private property for its purposes. The Justice Department offered no opinion, and the U.S. District Attorney in San Francisco did nothing. By September 1908 Kent had spent more than $1,500 of his own money defending land he no longer owned. Secretary Garfield, meanwhile, would approve no funding for the monument while the case hung over it, a freeze that lasted more than nine months. That fall the company offered to settle for fifteen acres. Kent refused, and on September 22, 1908, he wrote to Roosevelt asking that the district attorney be instructed "to use every possible means to prevent the destruction of a single tree." Roosevelt answered at once. In October the company engineered a four-day water shortage in Mill Valley to build public pressure. Residents worked out that the shutoff was deliberate, and the stunt backfired.

The suit never went to trial. Kent was never served with a summons. The one-year deadline passed in December 1908, and he told his lawyers to move for dismissal, writing that "there is no possibility of the plaintiffs creating any sort of dam, except a dam nuisance." No court records from the case survive. The suit was real, and it was the reason everyone moved so fast in December 1907. It was also never prosecuted.

All of this played out while the conservation movement was splitting into two camps. Pinchot's camp managed resources for "the greatest good of the greatest number in the long run." Muir's believed some places should be kept unspoiled for their own sake. Muir Woods looked like a clean win for Muir's side, but its donor would not stay there.

## Legacy
Muir Woods was the tenth national monument proclaimed under the Antiquities Act, and the first created from land donated by a private individual. Each of the nine before it sat on land the United States already owned: [Devils Tower](story:2), [The December 8, 1906 Monuments: Petrified Forest, El Morro, and Montezuma Castle](story:8), [Chaco Canyon](story:5), [The Volcanic Monuments: Lassen Peak and Cinder Cone](story:23), [Gila Cliff-Dwellings](story:24), and [Tonto](story:25). Each of those proclamations says so. [Grand Canyon](story:3) followed two days after Muir Woods. The National Park Service's historian of the Act, Ronald F. Lee, put it most exactly: Muir Woods was "the first monument to be established on land donated to the United States under Section 2 of the Antiquities Act."

Five years later, the donor helped dam a valley he could have saved. As a congressman, William Kent was a leading champion of the Raker Act of 1913, which dammed the Hetch Hetchy Valley inside Yosemite National Park to supply San Francisco with water. That was the same kind of reservoir he had kept out of Redwood Canyon. The bill was Representative John E. Raker's. Kent sat on the House Committee on the Public Lands, spoke for the bill on the floor, testified before the Senate committee on September 24, 1913, and lobbied President Wilson directly. His reasoning was public power: he insisted the water and power stay in public hands. The fight cost him Muir's friendship. On July 2, 1913, Kent wrote to Representative Sydney Anderson of Minnesota asking him not to "take my friend, Muir, seriously, for he is a man entirely without social sense. With him, it is me and God and the rock where God put it, and that is the end of the story." Muir's chapter on Hetch Hetchy in *The Yosemite* (1912) attacked "these temple destroyers, devotees of ravaging commercialism." One donor saved one canyon from a dam and later helped drown another. That's a fair picture of a movement still arguing about what conservation was for. The loss of Hetch Hetchy became a rallying wound that fed the creation of the National Park Service in 1916.

The grove has carried symbolic weight its founders never imagined. On May 19, 1945, a week late because of rain, some five hundred delegates to the San Francisco conference that founded the United Nations gathered in Cathedral Grove for a memorial to Franklin Roosevelt. Jan Christiaan Smuts of South Africa and Secretary of State Edward R. Stettinius Jr. were among the speakers. FDR did not choose the site for his own memorial. The Save-the-Redwoods League proposed holding a conference session in a redwood grove. NPS Director Newton Drury named Muir Woods and sent the idea up through Interior Secretary Ickes. Roosevelt endorsed it on March 12, 1945, as a conference session. He died on April 12, and Brazil's foreign minister, Pedro Leão Velloso, proposed turning the session into a memorial. The bronze plaque, completed in 1947, calls FDR the "THIRTY-FIRST PRESIDENT." He was the thirty-second, and the error is cast in the metal.

Today Muir Woods is the only old-growth coast redwood forest in the San Francisco Bay Area. It recorded 756,573 recreation visits in 2025. Its all-time high was 1,123,121 in 2016, before advance parking and shuttle reservations began in January 2018.

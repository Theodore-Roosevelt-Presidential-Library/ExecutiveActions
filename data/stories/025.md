## At a glance
On December 19, 1907, Theodore Roosevelt reserved a single square mile of the Tonto Basin in central Arizona to protect "two prehistoric ruins of ancient cliff dwellings" built by the people archaeologists call the Salado. The ruins sat about five miles from the site where the government was building the dam that would come to carry Roosevelt's name, and that construction had brought workers and sightseers to their doorstep. The proclamation drew no notice anyone has found, and the ruins got little real protection for another two decades. Today the monument is 1,120 acres.

## Background
Proclamation 787 set aside "the prehistoric cliff dwelling ruins and one section of land upon which same are located" — section 34, township 4 north, range 12 east, in Gila County. The diagram bound with the proclamation states its size plainly: "Containing 640 acres." (A figure of 480 acres sometimes appears for 1907; the 640-acre original, plus 480 acres added in 1937, makes the monument's present 1,120.)

Like the Gila cliff-dwellings proclaimed thirty-three days earlier ([Gila Cliff-Dwellings National Monument, New Mexico](story:24)), Tonto is the Antiquities Act used for its literal purpose — not scenery or geology but, in the proclamation's words, "relics of a vanished people," of "great ethnologic, scientific and educational interest," reserved with "as much land as may be necessary for the proper protection thereof."

The two dwellings, now known as the Lower and Upper Cliff Dwellings, were built into natural caves in a hillside above the valley. Their builders, the Salado, take their name from the Rio Salado — the Salt River — whose basin they farmed. The Park Service dates the Salado culture to 1250–1450 CE and the building of both dwellings to "about 1300 CE"; "by 1425–1450, many of the people in the Tonto Basin had moved to other areas." The Park Service describes the Salado as arising from three traditions: Ancestral Puebloan, Ancient Sonoran Desert People, and Mogollon.

Unlike its sister monuments of 1907, Tonto did not sit inside a national forest. The Tonto Forest Reserve, created on October 3, 1905, was drawn with a notch open to the south that left the monument's township outside it, and the adjoining ground did not join the Tonto National Forest until January 13, 1908, twenty-five days after the monument. The proclamation accordingly mentions no forest. The monument was nonetheless placed under the Forest Service.

## Historical context
Tonto came out of the same alarm that produced the Antiquities Act: the fear that the Southwest's ancient ruins were being stripped and sold before anyone thought to protect them. The Tonto Basin had a reason of its own, which was the federal government's own construction project there.

The dam on the Salt River was among the first works authorized under the 1902 Reclamation Act ([The Reclamation Act of 1902](story:21)). Secretary Hitchcock approved the project on March 14, 1903, and construction began that August. The roads, work camps and traffic that came with it put the once-remote cliff dwellings within easy reach. In the Park Service's words: "Located just four miles from the construction site, the cliff dwellings felt the impact. Workers, their families, and sightseers began exploring the cliff dwellings."

In December 1907 the dam was still low. Floods had repeatedly interrupted the masonry, and the Salt River was still running through a sluicing tunnel around the works. The Reclamation Service's report for 1907–08 records that by the end of June 1908 the south half of the dam stood at 75 feet above low water and the dam was 42 percent complete. The reservoir began filling around 1910 and first spilled in 1915. The Apache Trail, the wagon road from Mesa built in 1903–05, ended at the dam about five miles from the ruins, and no road reached the cliff dwellings themselves until 1929.

Names were still settling, too. The proclamation calls the works the "Salt River Reservoir." The construction town's post office had opened as "Roosevelt" in January 1904, but the dam's familiar name was still taking hold, and "Theodore" was not added until 1959.

In period terms, reserving the ruins was cheap stewardship of land already in federal hands. The ground was unsurveyed public domain, so the reservation took nothing from any homesteader or rancher and cost almost nothing. The proclamation itself names no threat to the ruins; it simply recites their interest.

**Who asked for it is unknown.** The Lassen and Gila monuments each had a named forest officer behind them. No one has been found for Tonto. The monument's administrative history credits Edgar Lee Hewett's review of ruins on federal land with naming Tonto as worthy of protection, but cites no source, and Hewett's best-known survey, the 1904 General Land Office circular on Southwestern ruins, never mentions Tonto. The standard histories by Ronald F. Lee and Hal Rothman list the monument without naming a proposer. The paper trail may simply be gone: when the Park Service asked in 1956 for the Forest Service's records of its years in charge, "only one file was found, containing a copy of the original proclamation and some correspondence back to 1925."

The phrase "relics of a vanished people" belongs to its era. The law spoke of a people as vanished while their descendants, among today's Puebloan, Hopi, Zuni and other Southwestern nations, were very much present.

## Political impact
The proclamation seems to have gone almost entirely unnoticed. A search of Chronicling America for 1907 and 1908 has found no page that mentions a Tonto *National Monument* at all. Across the seventy-two pages from those two years that contain "Tonto" along with "national" and "monument," the word "Tonto" appears four times, and every time it is the Tonto National *Forest*. The closest near-miss is a wire story that ran in about ten western papers in late January 1908. It was about the Grand Canyon monument and, alongside it, "an addition of 1,288,320 acres of land to the Tonto National forest," as the *Albuquerque Evening Citizen* of January 21, 1908 put it.

The silence carries some weight here, which is unusual for a search like this, because the nearest paper was publishing and has been digitized. The *Daily Arizona Silver Belt* of Globe, the Gila County seat, survives in 306 issues for 1907, including nearly every day of late December, and said nothing. The word "monument" appears on ninety-one of its pages, and none of them is anywhere near "Tonto," "cliff," "dwelling," "ruin" or "antiquities." The *Arizona Republican*, the *Bisbee Daily Review* and the *Tombstone Epitaph* are digitized for 1907 too, and were silent as well. Meanwhile the Grand Canyon proclamation of January 11, 1908 spread across several states by wire ([Grand Canyon National Monument, Arizona](story:3)), which shows that monument proclamations did get reported and that the search would have caught one. California papers from mid-December 1907 through March 1908 yield the same result: every hit is the January wire story. The *Arizona Gazette* of Phoenix, the *New York Times* and the *Washington Post* could not be searched, so the fair conclusion is that no coverage has been found, not that no paper reported it.

Nor is there evidence of controversy, though newspapers are the wrong place to look for it. Opposition in 1907 would show up in Interior Department and General Land Office correspondence or in the letters of territorial officials, and those have not been read. What can be said is that the reservation was small, sat on public land nobody was contesting, and locked up no grazing or timber that anyone had reason to fight over. Whatever heat the Antiquities Act generated in these years came from its bold uses, like the vast Grand Canyon monument and the argument over the "smallest area compatible," not from a square mile of cliff dwellings above the Salt River.

## Legacy
**Slow protection.** Protection on paper came faster than protection on the ground. Under the Forest Service the ruins were lightly guarded, with no fence or trail. No road reached them until 1929, when the Forest Service and the Southern Pacific built one, with a parking area at the mouth of Cholla Canyon and a mile of trail to the Lower Cliff Dwelling.

When the worst damage happened is a real puzzle. Park Service website copy says that chain-link fences were put up and locked at night, but that visitors wandering into unstable areas still damaged the dwellings, and that the ruins "suffered more damage and loss in the 1920's and early 1930's than during the previous 600 years." The Park Service's 1974 visitor guide agreed that the monument "received little protection until 1930" and that "considerable vandalism and thoughtless destruction of much of the prehistoric material took place in the 1920s." But the 1987 National Register nomination says that "most of the vandalism at the three major ruins occurred in the late 1800s and the early 1900s." The monument's administrative history explains why the question cannot be settled: the Forest Service "kept incomplete records," so when the Park Service took over, "it was impossible to piece together enough evidence to document precisely when major changes or damage occurred to the ruins." The fair statement is that unsupervised visitors and vandals damaged the cliff dwellings at some point between the 1880s and the early 1930s.

**To the Park Service.** Custody passed to the National Park Service in 1933 under Executive Order 6166, the reorganization that consolidated the national monuments, signed June 10 and effective August 10. The first Park Service ranger, Charlie Steen, arrived in 1934, which is why some sources give that year for the change.

**The 1937 enlargement.** On April 1, 1937, Franklin Roosevelt's Proclamation 2230, "Enlarging the Tonto National Monument — Arizona," took about 480 acres in three adjoining quarter-sections, ground that had joined the Tonto National Forest in January 1908, and "excluded" them from the forest and "added to and made a part of the Tonto National Monument." That brought the monument to 1,120 acres, its size today. The proclamation also placed the monument under the Director of the National Park Service, "Provided, that the administration of the monument shall be subject to the withdrawal for the Salt River Irrigation project, Arizona."

**Today.** A self-guided trail, one mile round trip, leads to the Lower Cliff Dwelling and is open every day but December 25. The more fragile Upper Cliff Dwelling can be seen only on a guided tour, three miles round trip with six hundred feet of climbing, offered once a day on weekends from November through April, for parties of up to six; reservations open October 1. The monument preserves the best publicly interpreted examples of Salado cliff-dwelling architecture.

It also connects two parts of Roosevelt's conservation program. The dam whose construction first exposed these ruins to harm bears his name, and he signed the proclamation that protects them, so reclamation and preservation meet in a single Arizona valley.

## At a glance
Two proclamations bracket Roosevelt's 1905. In February he called the Senate to meet at noon on March 4, his inauguration day, and that short session took up his fight over Dominican customs. In November he set apart Thursday, November 30, as Thanksgiving, in a proclamation that counted the year's blessings in a few flat lines and then warned that the dangers to the nation lay at home.

## Background
**The Senate call (Proclamation 538, February 23).** Article II, section 3 of the Constitution lets the President, "on extraordinary Occasions, convene both Houses, or either of them." Roosevelt summoned the Senate alone, at noon on March 4, 1905, "to receive such communications as may be made by the Executive." A Senate-only session can handle only the Senate's own business, consenting to treaties and confirming nominations, both done in closed executive session. The session ran from March 4 to March 18, 1905, the first of the 59th Congress. Secretary of State John Hay countersigned. The title it often carries today, *Convening a Special Session of the United States Senate*, is a modern editor's caption; the official printing has none.

**Thanksgiving (Proclamation 605, November 2).** Roosevelt's fifth annual Thanksgiving proclamation appointed Thursday, November 30, 1905, the last Thursday of the month, as a day of thanksgiving and prayer. Elihu Root, who had succeeded Hay after Hay's death on July 1, countersigned.

## Historical context
Roosevelt had won the presidency in his own right in November 1904, and his inauguration for a full term fell on March 4, 1905, the day the 58th Congress expired. Calling the new Senate at the change of term was customary. Roosevelt was succeeding himself, and his Cabinet carried over; the one change at the turn of the term was George B. Cortelyou, who took office as Postmaster General on March 7.

The proclamation's wording was general, but real business was waiting. The Senate had not acted on the protocol with the Dominican Republic, under which the United States would take charge of the Dominican customhouses and divide their revenue among the republic's creditors. On March 6 Roosevelt sent the Senate a special message that opens: "I wish to call the attention of the Senate at this executive session to the treaty with Santo Domingo." The *Morning Oregonian* of March 9 quoted the message's argument that "This treaty offers the only method for preventing the collection of fraudulent debts" and that "Santo Domingo grievously needs the aid of a powerful and friendly nation." Democratic opposition blocked a vote, and the Senate adjourned on March 18 without consenting. The same convening power had been used twice in 1903, each time to get treaties through ([The Convening Proclamations of 1903](story:45)).

A Thanksgiving proclamation was expected of every president. The unbroken yearly chain began with Lincoln's proclamation of October 3, 1863, but Lincoln fixed no permanent date: Andrew Johnson named the first Thursday of December in 1865, and Grant named November 18 in 1869. The last-Thursday date Roosevelt used rested on custom alone ([Thanksgiving Day, 1901](story:20)).

This one stands out for its language. Roosevelt reached back "nearly three centuries" to "the first settlers," who "fronted not only hardship and privation, but terrible risk to their lives." Then he turned the usual counting of blessings into a moral charge. The blessings come in short, flat sentences: "During the past year we have been blessed with bountiful crops. Our business prosperity has been great." So does the country's safety: "We are not threatened by foes from without." The war between Russia and Japan, which Roosevelt had brought to the peace table at Portsmouth, had ended with the treaty of September 5, two months earlier. The danger, the proclamation insists, was at home: "The foes from whom we should pray to be delivered are our own passions, appetites and follies; and against these there is always need that we should war." It closes with a call to "a life of cleanliness, honor and wisdom, so that this nation may do its allotted work on the earth in a manner worthy of those who founded it and of those who preserved it."

## Political impact
Both proclamations went out on the Associated Press wire. The Senate call was printed in full by the Santa Barbara *Morning Press* of February 24, 1905, under "EXTRA SESSION OF CONGRESS / President Calls One for March Fourth / PUBLIC INTERESTS DEMAND SUCH ACTION," and summarized the same day in the *Chico Record* and the *Morning Echo*, without comment. The *Morning Oregonian*'s front page that day said nothing of the Senate call, though it did report the scramble over patronage expected "after March 4." The session drew attention for the Dominican fight, not for the proclamation that called it.

The Thanksgiving proclamation reached far more readers. It was a wire story the day it was signed. The *Morning Oregonian* of November 3 ran the full text on page 3 under "GOOD USE FOR THANKS," with the deck "PRESIDENT ISSUES THANKSGIVING PROCLAMATION. Calls on People to Celebrate Day in Time-Honored Way and Consecrate Themselves," and added no comment of its own. In California, the line about "appetites and follies" turns up in 31 newspapers in 1905, from the *San Francisco Call* and *Los Angeles Herald* (both November 3) to small weeklies printing it through late November. At least one editor read the warning against the year's news: the *San Jose Mercury* of November 4 ran an editorial, "ROOSEVELT ON THANKSGIVING," that praised the proclamation as more than the usual formality and applied its warning to the graft and corruption in the headlines. Neither proclamation carried policy of its own, and no objection to either text has been found. Papers outside Oregon and California have not been searched.

## Legacy
Proclamation 538 belongs to the long practice of calling the Senate at the start of a presidential term, a step made necessary by the old calendar, under which a new Congress did not regularly meet until December. The Twentieth Amendment (1933) moved the start of the congressional term to January 3, ahead of inauguration day, so a sitting Senate now greets each new term without being summoned. The session it opened belongs to the story of the Dominican customs receivership. Roosevelt put the arrangement into effect by an executive *modus vivendi* in April 1905, and the Senate consented to a revised treaty only on February 25, 1907; it was proclaimed July 25, 1907 ([Prohibiting the Export of Arms to the Dominican Republic](story:59)).

Proclamation 605 belongs to the continuous line of annual Thanksgiving proclamations. The date kept resting on custom, usually the last Thursday of November, until Franklin Roosevelt moved the holiday a week earlier in 1939, 1940 and 1941, and Congress fixed it on the fourth Thursday by a joint resolution approved December 26, 1941. Anyone quoting the 1905 proclamation's closing sentence should take it from the *Statutes at Large*, which reads "those who founded it." A widely used online transcription misprints the word as "rounded."

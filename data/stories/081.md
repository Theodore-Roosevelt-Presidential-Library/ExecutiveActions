## At a glance
Most of Roosevelt's first twenty-six executive orders of 1903 kept the civil service running: one person hired here, one rule adjusted there. Three reached much further. One quietly handed the Midway Islands to the Navy. Another moved the papers of Washington, Jefferson, Madison, Hamilton, Monroe, Franklin, and the Continental Congress to the Library of Congress. A third rewrote the Civil Service Rules from top to bottom.

## Background
The bulk of the group is the everyday traffic of government:

- **Eleven named-person orders** reinstated, appointed, or transferred one person or one position "without regard to civil service rules" or "without examination": a State Department clerk, the Public Printer's private secretary, a Treasury draftsman, an Insular Affairs translator, a clerk at army headquarters in Manila, a telegrapher, an inspector of hulls, a customs inspector, a Treasurer's clerk, a customs messenger, and a Post Office clerk (EOs 197, 201, 202, 205, 207, 208, 211, 212, 215, 216, 221).
- **Two temporary-appointment orders**: EO 198 extended eight Internal Revenue clerks for sixty days, and EO 203 made permanent the temporary clerks at naval stations in the insular possessions.
- **Six civil-service rule changes** (EOs 199, 200, 204, 209, 210, 213). They covered how laborers doing the work of classified employees were to be certified, which transfers were barred when a promotion could fill the slot, the copperplate map-engraver examination (EO 204 removed the provision that had let an alien qualify), and how unclassified laborers outside Washington were appointed.
- **One-purpose orders**: Midway (EO 199-A), the State Department's historical papers (EO 206), personnel returns for the *Official Register* (EO 214), consular circulars (EO 217), and three Puerto Rico land orders (EOs 218, 219, 220).

**Midway.** EO 199-A, signed January 20, 1903, is one sentence long. It placed "such public lands as may exist on the Midway Islands, Hawaiian group" under "the jurisdiction and control of the Navy Department." The atoll had been American since August 28, 1867, when Captain William Reynolds of USS *Lackawanna* formally took possession, but no department had ever run it. The Navy announced the order as its General Order No. 120 of January 21, 1903.

**The founders' papers.** EO 206, of March 9, moved the State Department's "Revolutionary archives" to the Library of Congress: the records of the Continental Congress and the papers of Washington, Madison, Jefferson, Hamilton, Monroe, and Franklin. Its authority was one sentence in the legislative appropriation act of February 25, 1903, which let any department head turn over to the Librarian of Congress "any books, maps, or other material" the department no longer needed. The Librarian's 1903 report called it "the first important transfer to be made under this act." The order set the move for July 1, 1903, and it went slowly. By June 30, 1904 the Continental Congress papers (385 volumes), the Monroe, Hamilton, and Washington papers, and the Stevens collection of Franklin papers had arrived, the Washington papers last, on June 29, 1904. The Jefferson and Madison papers were "yet to be transferred."

**The new rulebook.** EO 209, of March 20, replaced the Civil Service Rules of May 6, 1896 with a fresh, consolidated set, "Approved, to take effect on and after April 15, 1903." It was the periodic housecleaning that kept the rules coherent as exceptions and amendments piled up. Eleven days later EO 213 clarified what the new rules did *not* do for employees serving under the old Rule VIII.

**Puerto Rico.** The three late-June orders belong to a deadline. The act of July 1, 1902 gave the President one year to reserve federal lands and buildings on the island for military, naval, court, and other public uses; whatever was not reserved passed to the insular government. The week of June 26–30, 1903, with Proclamations 502 and 503 and EOs 218–220, was the last week of that window ([The Puerto Rico Land Reservations of 1903](story:44)). EO 218 reserved property for the federal courts, district attorney, and marshal. EO 219 kept the President's right to amend Proclamation 502's naval reservation and let the Navy Secretary and the governor of Puerto Rico submit memoranda on how the lands would be used. EO 220 took part of a naval reservation back, withdrawing the Presidio at San Juan from Proclamation 502 of four days earlier.

## Historical context
Two things were converging on Midway that winter. The first was the Pacific cable. In December 1902 newspapers reported the administration's agreement with the Commercial Pacific Cable Company to lay a line from San Francisco to Honolulu and on "by way of the Midway islands and the island of Guam" to the Philippines. A cable station on an atoll no department administered raised the question of who would act as the government's landlord there. The second was a population of Japanese squatters and feather poachers. The atoll's historians record that the order followed "recurring complaints of Japanese squatters and poachers," and that the Navy removed them on June 3, 1903.

The order was made public seven weeks after it was signed, and the announcement tied the two threads together. An Associated Press dispatch from Washington dated March 11, 1903 reported that "the president by executive order has turned the Midway islands over to the navy department. This was done at the instance of the Pacific Cable company, which has asked for protection of its property on the islands. Roving Japanese sailors are in the habit of putting into the islands for the plumage of the sea fowl and for guano." The navy, it added, would probably "establish a small station" there, since the islands were "deemed essential to the operation of the Pacific cable." That is a press account; the Navy Department's own file on the order has not been seen.

About thirty cable-company workers landed on April 20, 1903 to build the station, and the line opened that summer. Roosevelt sent the first round-the-world message over it on July 4, 1903. It was the first *American* transpacific cable; a British line by way of Fanning Island had opened the previous December. Keeping poachers off the cable company's islands was part of the reasoning the press gave for the order. A conservation motive in the later sense is not documented.

EO 209 belongs to the ordinary life of a merit system Roosevelt had helped build. He had served six years as a Civil Service Commissioner (1889–1895), and keeping the rules current came naturally. EO 206 reflects a principle the Librarian of Congress, Herbert Putnam, set out in his 1903 report: historical papers "not necessary for administrative purposes will render a larger service to scholarship if in the Library, which has a specific duty not merely to preserve them, but to make them accessible, than in an Executive Department where this service is purely voluntary and is not sustained by specific appropriation."

## Political impact
None of these orders is known to have drawn a public fight.

**Midway.** The order drew no notice in January, because no one announced it until March. Then the Associated Press dispatch ran widely. In California it appeared in the *Los Angeles Herald*, the Santa Barbara *Morning Press* and *Weekly Press*, the *Stockton Mail* ("TO PROTECT MIDWAY ISLANDS") and the *Marysville Democrat*, all on March 12, and in weekly news digests through late March. In Indiana it ran in the *Indianapolis News* the same day. It was treated as a news item, not a controversy; no editorial comment has turned up. On the atoll itself, things moved quickly. The Navy removed the Japanese squatters and poachers on June 3, 1903, the cable station went up that spring, and the line opened in time for Roosevelt's round-the-world message of July 4, 1903. A detachment of about twenty Marines under Second Lieutenant Clarence S. Owens arrived in May 1904 to keep order, guard the cable station and stop the poaching, and stayed about four years.

**The founders' papers.** Putnam's report records that the State Department's cooperation "has been entirely prompt and cordial," and the order let the department hold back whatever it needed for "the continuity and completeness" of its records. The move itself took time: a year after the July 1, 1903 start date, the Jefferson and Madison papers had still not arrived. No newspaper notice of the order has been found in California or Indiana papers of March and early April 1903, so no notice of it outside the Library and the State Department is on record.

**Everything else.** EO 209 was a matter for the departments and the Civil Service Commission, and the Puerto Rico orders were routine administration carried out under the 1902 act, in the last days before its deadline. The personnel orders passed, so far as anyone has found, without notice.

## Legacy
### Midway
Midway is the lasting thread. The 1903 order began an unbroken line of Navy control that ran through the atoll's years as a cable station and, from the 1930s, a stop for transpacific flying boats; through the June 1942 Battle of Midway, widely regarded as the turning point of the Pacific war; and down to 1996, when Executive Order 13022 transferred the atoll to the Department of the Interior for the Midway Atoll National Wildlife Refuge, which today protects some of the largest albatross colonies in the world.

Because Midway was already the Navy's, Roosevelt's Hawaiian Islands Reservation of February 3, 1909, which swept in the northwestern chain from Cure Island to Necker, left Midway out ([The Bird Reservations of February 1909](story:105)). The later bird reservation did not grow out of the Midway order; the two sit side by side on the map.

### The founders' papers
EO 206's legacy is divided. The papers of Washington, Jefferson, Madison, Hamilton, Monroe and Franklin remain cornerstones of the Library of Congress's Manuscript Division. The records of the Continental and Confederation Congresses, transferred to the Library between 1903 and 1922, went to the National Archives in 1952 and are held there today as Record Group 360.

### The Civil Service Rules
The 1903 rules proved long-lived. They were amended piecemeal (Rule VII, for instance, was replaced by EO 740 in 1908), but the Civil Service Commission's report for fiscal 1918 still printed them under the 1903 preamble, and a 1933 government compilation headed them "[Promulgated Apr. 15, 1903, and revised to July 25, 1933]." Executive Order 9830 of February 24, 1947, effective May 1, 1947, restated the rules in full. Whether any general re-promulgation came in between is not yet known.

The remaining orders carry no independent legacy. What later became of the Puerto Rico tracts covered by EOs 218 and 219 has not been researched.

## At a glance
In the summer of 1905, six proclamations opened the "surplus" lands of the Uintah Indian Reservation in northeastern Utah to non-Indian homesteaders. Close to 38,000 people registered for a lottery to claim about 6,300 homesteads. The Uintah and White River Utes had refused this twice and never agreed to it. Their loss of land became the root of a jurisdiction fight that reached the Supreme Court in 1994.

## Background
Congress ordered the opening in an act of May 27, 1902, then pushed the date back three times, to October 1904, to March 1905, and finally to no later than September 1, 1905. The allotting itself was done by the Uintah Allotment Commission in the spring and summer of 1905, not by proclamation. Under the 1902 act, each head of a Uintah or White River Ute family received eighty acres of agricultural land and each other member forty. A 1902 joint resolution and a 1903 act set aside up to 250,000 acres of grazing land for the Utes in common. The 1905 act also let the President add reservation land to the Uintah Forest Reserve before the opening. He did that on July 14, the same day as the opening proclamation (see [The Forest Reserves of 1905](story:55)). Everything left over, the "surplus," was opened to homestead entry at $1.25 an acre.

Proclamation 581 set the opening for August 28, 1905, and laid out a lottery meant to keep order among the thousands expected. Settlers registered in person at Vernal, Price, or Provo, Utah, or at Grand Junction, Colorado, between August 1 and August 12. Honorably discharged soldiers and sailors could register through an agent. A public drawing at Provo, starting August 17, set the order of entry. Entries at the Vernal land office would then run at fifty numbers a day. For sixty days, only registered applicants could enter. The five proclamations that followed worked out the details on the same land:

- **Proclamation 587 (July 31)** reserved townsites that the Uintah Allotment Commission had picked "as natural and prospective centers of population," at the Interior Department's request. Its three blocks are the sites of today's Myton, Duchesne, and Randlett. Duchesne was first called Dora, then Theodore after the President. It took the name Duchesne when the neighboring town of Roosevelt took his surname.
- **Proclamation 588 (August 2)** changed the pace of entry, not the terms. Reciting "ample reasons for a modification," it raised the numbers heard each day at Vernal from fifty to 111.
- **Proclamation 589 (August 3)** reserved no townsites, whatever its common title says. Using the 1905 act's power to reserve land "to conserve the water supply for the Indians or for general agricultural development," it withdrew one list of "agricultural" lands and another of "reservoir" lands. The reservoir list included all of townships 2, 3, and 4 south in ranges 11 and 12 west.
- **Proclamation 590 (August 14)** added townsite lots next to the lands reserved by Proclamation 587.
- **Proclamation 591 (August 14)** cut Proclamation 589 back sharply. It declared that "only that portion" in ranges 11 and 12 west "is required for reservoir sites and to conserve and protect the water supply." Everything else, including every "agricultural" tract, went back into the opening. The reservoir lands that remained lie in the Strawberry Valley, the site of the Reclamation Service's Strawberry Valley Project. The Salt Lake *Telegram* reported that the Interior Department had "receded from the original order withdrawing 200,000 acres" and, "with the President's approval," cut the withdrawal to about 114,000 acres. The paper said the restored land raised the number of homesteads in the drawing.

The reservation covered about two million acres. The Indian Office's own schedule records 103,265.35 acres allotted to 1,283 Indians, 1,010,000 acres put into the forest reserve, 2,100 acres in townsites, 2,140 acres in mining claims, and 1,004,285 acres opened to homestead entry. The schedule's categories overlap, so they can't simply be added up.

## Historical context
The Utes did not consent. Congress had tried to negotiate twice, through commissions authorized in 1894 and 1898, and the Utes turned down both. The 1902 act still required the consent of a majority of the adult men of the two tribes. Then, in January 1903, the Supreme Court held in *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* that Congress could change reservation boundaries on its own authority. Weeks later, the act of March 3, 1903, directed the Secretary of the Interior to allot the reservation anyway if the Utes had not agreed by June 1. The acts of 1904 and 1905 only moved the opening date.

The pressure came from several directions. Utah settlers and boosters wanted the Uinta Basin's farmland. The basin's asphalt and gilsonite, and a belief that the mountains held minerals, drew more attention. And federal Indian policy held that land owned in common stood in the way of "civilization" and that private property was the cure.

Conservation cut against the tribe twice. In the same year the forest reserves passed to the Forest Service, 1,010,000 acres of the Utes' own reservation went into the Uintah Forest Reserve. A great part of the land the tribe lost went not to settlers but to the government. Then Proclamations 589 and 591 carved a reservoir site out of the same lands for an irrigation project. Roosevelt's administration treated the opening as the carrying-out of settled law. For the Ute people, it was the breaking up of their reservation.

## Political impact
The opening drew a crowd. The General Land Office reported that 37,702 people registered for 6,277 claims of about 160 acres each. A period photograph shows applicants circling a Provo building, waiting to put their names in. The *Salt Lake Tribune* counted 18,858 names at the Provo office alone. The drawing opened at Provo on August 17 under Land Office Commissioner W. A. Richards. The envelopes from all four registration points were mixed in "a great revolving box," and the first name drawn was Ray Daniels of Provo. About 2,000 names were drawn the first day. Of the first two hundred, 80 were from Colorado, 9 from other states, and the rest from Utah. Entry turned out to be slow: in the first four weeks, 824 entries were made out of a possible 2,664.

Utah papers covered the registration and drawing as front-page local news. They wrote for would-be settlers, down to which counties had "fared poorly in the drawing," and none of the articles reported Ute views.

The Ute objection was real, and it failed. A Ute delegation went to Washington in 1905 and came home without relief. In the spring of 1906, several hundred White River Utes, Red Cap among their leaders, left the reservation for the Sioux country. About a thousand cavalrymen met them on the Powder River that fall. They spent the winter at Fort Meade, South Dakota, and did not return to the reservation until October 1908. The closest comparison among the period's openings is the 1904 Rosebud lottery (see [The Sioux Land Openings of 1904](story:51)).

The proclamations themselves were paperwork carrying out an act of Congress, but they did not all hold. Within eleven days the administration made a water withdrawal and then largely undid it, two weeks before entry began.

## Legacy
The 1905 opening led to more than a century of litigation over what land remained "Indian country." In 1985 the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals held in *Ute Indian Tribe v. Utah* that the opening had not shrunk the reservation. The Utah Supreme Court reached the opposite conclusion in *State v. Perank* (1992). In *Hagen v. Utah* (1994) the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the 1902–1905 laws had diminished the Uintah Reservation. That meant Myton, founded on opened land in 1905, is not Indian country, and state jurisdiction was upheld. The ruling has shaped criminal and civil jurisdiction in the Uinta Basin ever since, and the federal courts have returned to the boundary in later cases.

In 1931 Congress paid the Utes for 973,779 acres of the 1905 forest lands. In 1910 it ended the Utes' title to the Strawberry Valley reservoir lands for $1.25 an acre, paid from the reclamation fund in five yearly installments charged to the project's water users. The 1948 Hill Creek Extension added about 510,000 acres to the reservation. Another Uintah parcel was restored to the public domain by Proclamation 648 in 1906 (see [The Indian Reservation Openings of 1906](story:65)). In 1908 the Uinta National Forest was split, and part of it became the Ashley National Forest, based in Vernal. Much of the forest land taken in 1905 is still federal land on the south slope of the Uintas. Today the Uintah and Ouray Reservation is home to the Ute Indian Tribe. The Utah Division of Indian Affairs says it "covers over 4.5 million acres," without separating tribal and trust land from other land within its boundaries.

## At a glance
On March 11, 1907, Roosevelt set aside the great houses of Chaco Canyon in northwestern New Mexico Territory as a national monument of 20,629.40 acres. Pueblo Bonito, the best known of them, had spent the late 1890s being dug up and shipped east. The proclamation ended years of digging and a fight over a homestead claim. It also left out a warning that the Navajo families living in the canyon should not be pushed out.

## Background
Proclamation 740 set aside Pueblo Bonito and its neighbors, an Ancestral Puebloan complex of monumental scale, as the Chaco Canyon National Monument. The proclamation calls the ruins "of extraordinary interest because of their number and their great size and because of the innumerable and valuable relics of a prehistoric people which they contain." It warns "all unauthorized persons, not to appropriate, excavate, injure or destroy any of the prehistoric ruins or remains."

The proclamation itself never states the 20,629.40-acre figure. It comes from the General Land Office map that the proclamation makes part of itself, printed with the text in the *Statutes at Large*. The reservation was made up of six separate parcels.

The proclamation contradicts itself about where the ruins are. The opening *Whereas* clause puts them "in San Juan and McKinley Counties, Territory of New Mexico." The operative clause says "San Juan County" alone. The opening clause is the correct one. Two of the six parcels, including Kin Ya'a at Crownpoint, lie south of the San Juan County line in McKinley County. Nobody on record noticed the error for two decades.

Ronald F. Lee, the National Park Service's historian of the Antiquities Act, called Chaco "probably the most important group of prehistoric ruins ever to be made a national monument under the Antiquities Act."

## Historical context
Chaco became the best-known example of the problem the Antiquities Act was written to solve. For five summers, 1896 through 1900, the Hyde Exploring Expedition dug at Pueblo Bonito. Brothers Benjamin Talbot Babbitt Hyde and Frederick Erastus Hyde Jr. paid for it; they were New York grandsons, on their mother's side, of the soap manufacturer B. T. Babbitt. George H. Pepper, a 22-year-old assistant curator at the American Museum of Natural History, directed the first four seasons. Richard Wetherill, a rancher and excavator, ran the fifth. The finds went east to the American Museum, some of them as whole rooms taken apart for shipping. Pepper counted 198 rooms excavated, "something less than half the rooms in the pueblo." Frank McNitt, measuring in the canyon in 1952, counted 190 rooms and kivas. Close to 200 is the honest figure.

The government's attention came from several directions. In May 1900 the General Land Office acted on an item in the *Santa Fe New Mexican* and sent Special Agent Max Pracht, who reported "vandal acts." That November the Santa Fe Archaeological Society drafted a resolution and sent it to Interior Secretary Hitchcock over the signature of its president, Judge John R. McFie. The archaeologist Edgar Lee Hewett was almost certainly the force behind the society, but his part was indirect. In February 1901 the Navajo agent at Fort Defiance, George W. Hayzlett, was asked about a rumor that the expedition was drilling for oil. He reported that they were drilling only for water, and added that they were carrying off "many thousands of dollars worth of pottery and relics."

Wetherill, meanwhile, had filed a homestead entry covering Pueblo Bonito, Chetro Ketl, and Pueblo del Arroyo. The Land Office ordered a second investigation on December 8, 1900. By the end of that month it had secured an order, passed through Frederic Ward Putnam at the American Museum, stopping further digging at Pueblo Bonito. The 1900 season was the last. The ban became permanent in March 1902, when Wetherill's homestead entry was also suspended.

Special Agent Samuel J. Holsinger reached Pueblo Bonito on April 23, 1901, spent four weeks there, and filed a three-volume, 87-page report on December 5, 1901. He described sound prehistoric roof beams sawn off flush with the walls and carried away. He also described rooms "found in a perfect state" being taken apart and shipped to New York, and he called the work "the purest vandalism." His report also found that the Hyde company sold modern curios and blankets, but "in no instance has a single specimen of pre-historic origin been made an article of merchandise." He recommended that twenty-three townships be made a national park.

Holsinger also spoke up for the people who lived there. Chaco is ancestral homeland to the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico and the Hopi, and it lies within Navajo country, where Navajo families lived and farmed. Holsinger wrote: "The lands are only valuable as an indifferent stock range; for the extensive pre-historic ruins and as the home of numerous Navajo Indians. No interest would be injured by reserving the lands or by the establishment of a National Park, unless such reservation would exclude the Indians. What is of no practical value to the white settler for agricultural purposes is ample for the Indians and to deprive them of the land would be robbing them of that which in all its humbleness and poverty is very dear." He went further: "As a National Park, I take it that the presence of the Navajo would not be detrimental to public interests and that they would not only add picturesqueness and interest to the park but protection to the objects of scientific interest to be preserved." The Land Office drafted letters recommending a park twice in 1902, in April and December. Both repeated his condition. Neither was signed, and the condition never made it into the proclamation.

Chaco was never the subject of a bill in Congress, and it was not a direct cause of the 1906 Act. The legislation grew out of concerns about Casa Grande, the Pajarito Plateau, and Mesa Verde. Hewett's contribution to the law itself was large: he drafted the bill that Representative Lacey introduced on January 9, 1906, as H.R. 11016. Lee wrote that "not a single significant word had been altered" in the final law from Hewett's draft. The Act as signed was the identical Senate bill, S. 4698.

Interior Secretary Hitchcock withdrew the Chaco lands from entry on April 4, 1905. That June, Land Office Special Agent Frank Grygla spent two days in the canyon and came out supporting Wetherill's homestead claim. He found sixty acres of corn, five of wheat, two of vegetables, and a claimed five thousand sheep, along with six rooms and a kiva that looked freshly dug. Frank Bond, Chief Clerk of the Land Office, summed up in 1911 the conclusion: "It was apparent that a cancellation for fraudulent entry would be difficult and probably unjust; that instead of excavating the ruins the entryman was protecting them." On January 14, 1907, Wetherill formally gave up four lots, forty-seven acres in all, that took in the great houses. It was the first surrender of entered land under the Antiquities Act. The monument followed eight weeks later.

## Political impact
The proclamation drew almost no notice of any kind. A search of all twenty-one New Mexico newspapers digitized for 1907 turns up no news story reporting it, and no objection on grounds of grazing, Navajo land use, school sections, or homesteads. The closest thing to coverage was oblique. On March 16, 1907, five days after the signing, the *Santa Fe New Mexican* argued for making the Palace of the Governors a monument. In passing it noted that New Mexico already had "two of them," "Inscription Rock and Pueblo Bonito." People called the canyon by the name of its largest ruin. A syndicated feature on the new monuments ran that spring in papers from Connecticut to Texas, still describing Chaco as pending; the copy had been written before March 11 and nobody corrected it. The *Hopkinsville Kentuckian* of May 16, 1907, two months after the signing, still called it "the next to be included." The paper closest to the canyon, the *Farmington Times-Hustler*, survives for 1907 in no known collection. Its rival, the *Farmington Enterprise*, exists for that year only on microfilm.

The real contest had happened earlier, between commercial excavators and the scientific establishment, and it ended less as a scientific victory than as a negotiated withdrawal. Wetherill had given up the great houses in January; he kept ranching and trading in the canyon. On June 22, 1910, he was shot near Pueblo Bonito, hours after his ranch hand, Bill Finn, had beaten a Navajo man unconscious. The shooter was that man's brother-in-law, Chiishch'ilin Biye'. Some accounts call the shooting an ambush. Navajo accounts, then and now, describe a confrontation. In October 1912 a remnant of the Wetherill homestead was patented.

For the Navajo families in the canyon, the monument changed little at first, but the boundary soon began to matter. In May 1909 five Navajo allotments were taken up inside it. One belonged to an old man named George, whose affidavit reads: "I came to this spring when the Navajos came back from Fort Sumner, and my home has been there ever since." When the overlap was discovered that winter, the allotments were rescinded and four were cancelled. The fifth, Welo's, was not. Near Pueblo Pintado the same year, a man named De-neth-tso and his son Ush-ke-yazzie had to choose replacement allotments after a survey mismatch left their hogans and corrals outside their land.

The county error in the proclamation went unnoticed. In 1911 Frank Bond, Chief Clerk of the General Land Office, described the monument as "lying chiefly in Chaco Canyon … San Juan County, N. Mex.," repeating the mistake without comment.

## Legacy
No one on record caught the county error for two decades. The Interior Department's annual monument reports for 1913 and 1915 gave the legal description and named no county at all. Supplemental Land Office survey plats accepted on March 29, 1923 showed that some of the ruins meant to be protected fell outside the boundary. President Calvin Coolidge's Proclamation 1826 of January 10, 1928 answered both problems at once. It added four parcels totaling 880 acres and quietly described the monument as lying "in San Juan and McKinley Counties." The often-quoted total of 21,509.40 acres is simply the two figures added together; neither proclamation prints it.

For the Navajo, the loss of the canyon came in stages over forty years:

- **1934:** Navajo residents were moved out of the monument. A University of New Mexico hogan survey noted three years later: "#81 Now used as a medicine hogan since the Park Service moved all the Navajos out of the National Monument." On September 2, 1936, Tomasito dictated a letter to Senator Elmer Thomas: "We have lived here for 52 years. Up to 2 years ago, we lost our land here when the National Park people took away our allotted land. Now the land is all fenced in."
- **November 1947:** The boundary fence was finished, ending grazing inside the monument.
- **May 1948:** A number of Navajo people still living at Tomasito's old homesite in the South Gap were moved out. That ended Navajo residence within the monument.
- **Today:** Welo's allotment, the one of the 1909 five that was never cancelled, remains allotted.

The historian David Brugge cautioned: "Exactly when particular Navajos left and under what conditions is vague in the available documentation."

### Chaco Culture National Historical Park
On December 19, 1980, Congress abolished the monument and established Chaco Culture National Historical Park in its place (Public Law 96-550). The law set the park at approximately 33,989 acres; the National Park Service's current listing gives 33,960.19. The park holds some 4,000 recorded archeological sites. The same law designated thirty-three outlying Chaco Culture Archeological Protection Sites covering about 8,771 acres. The Chacoan Outliers Protection Act of 1995 raised that to thirty-nine sites and about 14,372 acres, adding Casamero, Chimney Rock, Dittert, Guadalupe, Navajo Springs, and Salmon Ruin and dropping two others.

### World Heritage
In 1987, at the World Heritage Committee's eleventh session in Paris (December 7–11), UNESCO inscribed Chaco on the World Heritage List under cultural criterion (iii). The property was renamed simply "Chaco Culture" in 2006. It is broader than the park, taking in Aztec Ruins National Monument and several smaller Chacoan sites managed by the Bureau of Land Management, so the park is one part of the World Heritage property rather than the whole. UNESCO's own documents count its pieces two ways: seven components in its Statement of Outstanding Universal Value, and ten, totaling 14,261 hectares, in its 2016 boundary table, which splits the park into its four detached units. Salmon Ruin, which joined the protection sites in 1995, is not part of the World Heritage property.

Chaco remains one of the most significant archaeological preserves on the continent and is central to the heritage of the Pueblo nations, the Hopi, and the Navajo. The questions its creation raised are still live: who controls Native heritage sites, who interprets them, and who benefits from them. Today those questions are argued with descendant communities at the table rather than left out.
